## 1. Carbon and its bonds

- Carbon is **tetravalent**. It uses sp³ (methane, tetrahedral), sp² (ethene, planar) or sp (ethyne, linear) hybrid orbitals.
- More s character: shorter, stronger bonds and a more electronegative carbon. An sp carbon (50% s) is the most electronegative.
- **π bond:** needs parallel p orbitals on neighbouring atoms. So all six atoms of $\ce{H2C=CH2}$ lie in one plane and rotation about C=C is **restricted**. The π cloud lies above and below that plane, open to reagents: π bonds are the most reactive sites.

## 2. Writing structures

- **Complete:** every bond drawn as a dash; lone pairs may or may not be shown.
- **Condensed:** dashes left out, identical groups shown by a subscript: $\ce{CH3(CH2)6CH3}$ for octane.
- **Bond-line:** C and H are not written. Each line end and corner is a carbon with enough H to make 4 bonds. Other atoms (O, N, Cl…) are written.
- **Wedge and dash:** solid wedge **towards** you, dashed wedge **away** from you, plain line in the paper.
- **Models:** framework (bonds only), ball-and-stick (springs for C=C), space-filling (atom sizes from van der Waals radii, no bonds).

## 3. Classification

| Class | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acyclic (aliphatic) | Open chain, straight or branched | Ethane, isobutane, acetic acid |
| Alicyclic | Ring, behaves like aliphatic | Cyclohexane; tetrahydrofuran (O in ring) |
| Benzenoid aromatic | Benzene-type rings | Benzene, aniline, naphthalene |
| Non-benzenoid aromatic | Aromatic, no benzene ring | Tropone |
| Heterocyclic aromatic | Aromatic ring with another atom | Furan, thiophene, pyridine |

- **Functional group:** the atom or group that decides the chemical properties ($-$OH, $-$CHO, $-$COOH…). All alcohols, for example, give $\ce{H2}$ with sodium.
- **Homologous series:** same functional group and general formula; successive members (homologues) differ by **$\ce{CH2}$**.

## 4. IUPAC names: the steps

1. **Find the principal functional group** (highest in the priority list). It gives the **suffix**. All other groups become **prefixes**.
2. **Choose the parent chain:** the longest carbon chain that contains the principal group (and the C=C or C≡C). If two chains are equally long, take the one with **more side chains**.
   - (Exam extra) Current IUPAC rules (2013) pick the longest chain first (it must still carry the suffix group), even if that leaves out a C=C or C≡C, so other books may name such compounds differently. For NCERT-based exams, use NCERT's rule above.
3. **Number the chain** so that the principal group gets the lowest number. Then multiple bonds, then substituents, get the lowest numbers possible.
4. The carbon of $-$CHO, $-$COOH, $-$COOR, $-$COCl, $-$CONH₂ and $-$CN in the chain is **C-1**.
5. **Write prefixes in alphabetical order.** Ignore di, tri, tetra, sec- and tert- when sorting. Count iso and neo as part of the name.
6. Identical groups: di, tri, tetra…, every position listed, commas between numbers, hyphens between numbers and words: **2,2,4-trimethylpentane**, **6-ethyl-2-methylnonane**.
7. Substituents at the same distance from both ends: the one **first in the alphabet** gets the lower number: **3-ethyl-6-methyloctane**.
8. Two or more of the same functional group: keep the full alkane name: **ethane-1,2-diol**, **hexane-2,4-dione**. Two or more C=C or C≡C: add an "a": **buta-1,3-diene**.
9. A branched substituent is numbered from the carbon joined to the main chain and put in brackets: **5-(2,2-dimethylpropyl)nonane**.

:::example{title="NCERT examples"}
$\ce{HOCH2(CH2)3CH2COCH3}$ is **7-hydroxyheptan-2-one** (ketone beats alcohol).

$\ce{BrCH2CH=CH2}$ is **3-bromoprop-1-ene** (C=C is a suffix, Br only a prefix).

$\ce{CH3COCH2CH2CH2COOH}$ is **5-oxohexanoic acid** (acid beats ketone).

$\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH(OH)CH3}$ is **pent-4-en-2-ol**.
:::

**Rings:** add "cyclo": cyclopentane, **3-ethyl-1,1-dimethylcyclohexane**.

**Benzene compounds:**
- One group: nitrobenzene, bromobenzene. Common names are also used: toluene (methylbenzene), anisole (methoxybenzene), aniline (aminobenzene).
- Two groups: 1,2 = **ortho (o-)**, 1,3 = **meta (m-)**, 1,4 = **para (p-)**.
- Three or more: lowest locants, alphabetical order: **1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene**. With a base name (anisole, aniline, phenol), its group is C-1: **4-ethyl-2-methylaniline**.
- A ring on a chain that carries the functional group is a **phenyl** ($\ce{C6H5-}$, Ph) substituent.

## 5. Isomerism

Isomers: the same molecular formula, different properties.

| Type | Differ in | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chain | Carbon skeleton | Pentane, isopentane, neopentane ($\ce{C5H12}$) |
| Position | Place of the group | Propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol |
| Functional group | The group itself | Propanal, propanone ($\ce{C3H6O}$) |
| Metamerism | Alkyl groups on each side of the group | Methoxypropane, ethoxyethane ($\ce{C4H10O}$) |

**Stereoisomers:** same bonds, different arrangement in space (geometrical and optical). This chapter only names them; details come with alkenes (Unit 9) and in Class 12.

## 6. Breaking a bond

| | Heterolytic | Homolytic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electrons | Both go to one fragment | One to each fragment |
| Gives | Carbocation or carbanion | Free radicals |
| Arrow | Full curved arrow (a pair) | Half-headed "fish-hook" (one electron) |

- **Carbocation:** C with only 6 electrons and a + charge. sp², **trigonal planar**, with an empty p orbital.
- **Carbanion:** C with a lone pair and a − charge. Usually sp³, **distorted tetrahedral** (pyramidal).
- **Free radical:** a neutral species with an unpaired electron.
- Carbocations and radicals: tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl.
- Heterolytic reactions are called polar (ionic); homolytic ones free radical (non-polar).

## 7. Reagents

- **Substrate:** the molecule whose carbon forms the new bond. The other reactant is the **reagent**.
- **Nucleophile (Nu:)**, "nucleus-seeking": gives an electron pair. $\ce{HO-}$, $\ce{NC-}$, carbanions; lone-pair molecules such as $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{R3N}$.
- **Electrophile (E⁺)**, "electron-seeking": takes an electron pair. Carbocations, $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{NO2+}$; the δ+ carbon of C=O and of C–X.
- **Curved arrows** show an electron pair moving from its tail (π bond or lone pair) to its head (atom or bond).

## 8. Electron displacement effects

Inductive, resonance and hyperconjugation effects are **permanent**. The electromeric effect is **temporary**.

**Inductive effect** (through σ bonds). In $\ce{CH3CH2Cl}$, C-1 gets δ+ and C-2 a smaller δδ+. It is negligible after **three bonds**. Groups that pull electrons (relative to H) are $-$I; alkyl groups are +I.

**Resonance.** If one Lewis structure can't describe a molecule, the real molecule is a **resonance hybrid** of canonical structures.
- Benzene: all C–C bonds are **139 pm**, between C–C (154 pm) and C=C (134 pm). In nitromethane both N–O bonds are equal.
- **Resonance energy** = energy of the most stable canonical structure − energy of the real hybrid.
- Canonical structures must have the same **positions of nuclei** and the same number of **unpaired electrons**.
- A structure contributes more if it has: more covalent bonds; octets on all atoms (duet for H); less charge separation; − on the more electronegative atom and + on the more electropositive one; more spread-out charge.

**Resonance effect.** +R: the group gives electrons to the conjugated system (aniline: $-$NH₂). −R: the group pulls electrons from it (nitrobenzene: $-$NO₂). A **conjugated system** has alternate single and double bonds (buta-1,3-diene).

**Electromeric effect.** A full shift of the π pair to one atom when a reagent attacks.
- **+E:** the pair moves to the atom the reagent attaches to (adding $\ce{H+}$ to an alkene).
- **−E:** the pair moves to the atom the reagent does **not** attach to (CN⁻ attacking C=O: the pair moves to O).
- When I and E act in opposite directions, **E wins**.

**Hyperconjugation** ("no-bond resonance"). C–H σ electrons of an alkyl group spread into an empty p orbital (carbocation) or a π system (propene). More such C–H bonds: more stable. $\ce{CH3+}$ has none.

## 10. Purification

| Method | When to use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sublimation | Solid turns straight to vapour | Camphor from $\ce{CaSO4}$ |
| Crystallisation | Solubility differs | Most solids |
| Simple distillation | B.p. far apart | Chloroform (334 K), aniline (457 K) |
| Fractional distillation | B.p. close | Crude oil |
| Reduced pressure | High b.p., or decomposes | Glycerol from spent lye |
| Steam distillation | Steam-volatile, immiscible with water | Aniline from water |
| Differential extraction | More soluble in an organic solvent | Separating funnel |

- **Crystallisation:** use a solvent in which the compound dissolves well hot but sparingly cold. Coloured impurities: **activated charcoal**. The filtrate is the mother liquor.
- **Fractional distillation:** each condense-and-re-boil step in the column is a **theoretical plate**.
- **Continuous extraction:** the same solvent is reused when the compound is only slightly soluble in it.
- **Purity check:** a pure compound has a sharp melting point and boiling point.

## 11. Chromatography

- The mixture sits on a **stationary phase** (solid or liquid); a **mobile phase** (liquid or gas) moves over it and the components separate.
- **Adsorption** (silica gel or alumina): **column** (most strongly adsorbed part stays near the top) and **TLC** (0.2 mm layer on a glass plate; each spot has an $R_f$ value).
- Colourless spots: UV light, iodine crystals (brown spots), or a spray (ninhydrin for amino acids).
- **Partition** (paper chromatography): the stationary phase is the **water held in the paper**. The developed paper is the **chromatogram**.

## 12. Detecting elements (qualitative analysis)

- **C and H:** heat with CuO. C gives $\ce{CO2}$ (lime water turns milky); H gives water (white anhydrous $\ce{CuSO4}$ turns blue).
- **N, S, halogens (and P): Lassaigne's test.** Fusing with sodium turns covalent N, S, X into ionic NaCN, $\ce{Na2S}$, NaX. Boiling the fused mass with water gives the **sodium fusion extract**.

$$\ce{Na + C + N -> NaCN}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{6CN- + Fe^2+} \\ & \ce{-> [Fe(CN)6]^4-} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{3[Fe(CN)6]^4- + 4Fe^3+} \\ & \ce{-> Fe4[Fe(CN)6]3.xH2O} \end{aligned}$$
$$\ce{S^2- + Pb^2+ -> PbS}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{S^2- + [Fe(CN)5NO]^2-} \\ & \ce{-> [Fe(CN)5NOS]^4-} \end{aligned}$$
$$\ce{Na + C + N + S -> NaSCN}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Fe^3+ + SCN-} \\ & \ce{-> [Fe(SCN)]^2+} \end{aligned}$$
$$\ce{X- + Ag+ -> AgX}$$

- Phosphorus: oxidise with sodium peroxide to phosphate, boil with $\ce{HNO3}$, add ammonium molybdate: yellow **ammonium phosphomolybdate**.

## 13. Estimating elements (quantitative analysis)

- C and H by combustion; N by **Dumas** or **Kjeldahl**; halogens by **Carius** (as AgX); S as $\ce{BaSO4}$; P as ammonium phosphomolybdate or $\ce{Mg2P2O7}$; O usually by difference. Methods and formulas: see the Rules and Tables Sheet.
- Today a **CHN elemental analyser** does C, H and N on 1–3 mg of sample.
