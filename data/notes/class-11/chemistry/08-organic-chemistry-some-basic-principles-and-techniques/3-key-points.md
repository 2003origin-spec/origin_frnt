## Hybridisation and bonds

- Count σ bonds as one per pair of bonded atoms; add 1 π for each double bond, 2 π for each triple bond.
- An sp carbon is the most electronegative (50% s character). That is why the H on $\ce{HC#CH}$ is more acidic than the H on ethene or ethane (exam extra).
- In $\ce{CH2=CH-CH2-CH2-C#CH}$, the C2–C3 bond is made from **sp²–sp³** orbitals. Name each carbon by its own bonds, not its neighbour's.

:::trap
In $\ce{CH2=C=CH2}$ (allene) the middle carbon is **sp**, not sp². It has two double bonds and no H.
:::

## Naming

:::trap
**Longest chain first (alkanes).** "2-Ethylbutane" is wrong: the longest chain has 5 carbons, so it is **3-methylpentane**. In an alkane, an ethyl group on C-2 always means you picked too short a chain. With a functional group it can be right, because the chain must contain that group: $\ce{CH3CH2CH(C2H5)COOH}$ is 2-ethylbutanoic acid.
:::

:::trap
**Alphabetical order ignores di, tri, sec-, tert-.** "Dimethyl" sorts under m, so **3-ethyl-2,2-dimethylpentane**, not 2,2-dimethyl-3-ethylpentane. But **iso** and **neo** count: isopropyl sorts under i.
:::

:::trap
The carbon of $-$CHO or $-$COOH **is** C-1 of the chain. $\ce{CH3CH2CHO}$ is propanal (3 C), not ethanal.
:::

- A ketone group in a chain named after another group is **oxo**, not "keto": 5-oxohexanoic acid.
- Halogens, $-$NO₂ and $-$OR are **only** prefixes. So $\ce{BrCH2CH=CH2}$ is 3-bromo**prop-1-ene**: the C=C (a suffix) gets the lower number.
- Ethers are named as **alkoxyalkanes**: $\ce{CH3OCH2CH3}$ is methoxyethane. The longer chain is the parent.
- "e" of the alkane is dropped before a vowel (propan**ol**, propan**al**) and kept before a consonant (ethane**diol**, propane**nitrile**) (exam extra).
- (Exam extra) When $-$CHO, $-$COOH or $-$CN sits on a ring, its carbon is not part of the ring: use **-carbaldehyde**, **-carboxylic acid**, **-carbonitrile** (cyclohexanecarboxylic acid).
- (Exam extra) Multiple bonds first get the lowest set of numbers, whichever bond it favours: $\ce{HC#CCH2CH=CHCH3}$ is hex-4-en-1-yne. Only if both ends give the same set does C=C get the lower number: hex-1-en-5-yne. NCERT's own example, hexa-1,3-dien-5-yne, follows this rule.

:::tip
Priority order, in pairs: **acid, sulphonic acid** > **ester, acid chloride** > **amide, nitrile** > **aldehyde, ketone** > **alcohol, amine** > **ene, yne**.
:::

## Isomerism

- Ethoxyethane and methoxypropane are **metamers** (different alkyl groups on each side of O). Ethanol and methoxymethane are **functional** isomers.
- $\ce{C5H12}$ has exactly **3** chain isomers: pentane, 2-methylbutane, 2,2-dimethylpropane.
- (Exam extra) $\ce{C4H10O}$ has **7** structural isomers: 4 alcohols and 3 ethers.

## Reactive intermediates

:::remember
- Carbocation: **sp²**, planar, empty p orbital, 6 electrons on C.
- Carbanion: **sp³**, pyramidal (distorted tetrahedron), 8 electrons on C.
- Carbocation and free-radical stability: **3° > 2° > 1° > methyl**.
:::

:::trap
$\ce{CH3+}$ has **no** hyperconjugation. Its empty p orbital is at right angles to all three C–H bonds, so they can't overlap with it. In $\ce{CH3CH2+}$ one C–H bond can line up with the empty p orbital.
:::

- (Exam extra) Carbanion stability is the **reverse**: methyl > 1° > 2° > 3°.
- Allyl $\ce{CH2=CH-CH2+}$ and benzyl $\ce{C6H5CH2+}$ cations are stabilised by **resonance**: the charge is spread over several carbons (NCERT Exercise 8.11 asks for their resonance structures).
- Neopentyl cation, $\ce{(CH3)3CCH2+}$, is **primary**, despite its bulky group. A tertiary cation like $\ce{(CH3)3C+}$ is more stable (NCERT Exercise 8.38).

## Electronic effects

:::trap
The **electromeric effect is temporary**: it exists only while the reagent is attacking. Inductive, resonance and hyperconjugation effects are **permanent**.
:::

:::trap
The inductive effect works through **σ bonds** and dies out after about **three bonds**. The resonance effect works through **π bonds** along a conjugated chain and does not fade like that.
:::

- When inductive and electromeric effects oppose each other, the **electromeric effect wins** (NCERT).
- Halogens are **−I** but **+R** (they have lone pairs). On benzene, the −I effect wins for reactivity (exam extra).
- $\ce{O2NCH2CH2O-}$ is **more stable** than $\ce{CH3CH2O-}$: the −I effect of $-$NO₂ spreads the negative charge.
- Alkyl groups give electrons to a π system through **hyperconjugation** (and +I).

:::remember
Resonance structures are **imaginary**. They differ only in where electrons are; nuclei stay put and the number of unpaired electrons stays the same. The real molecule (the hybrid) has **lower energy** than any of them.
:::

:::trap
A structure with + on O and − on C (for $\ce{CH2=CH-CHO}$) contributes almost nothing. Charge should sit − on the more electronegative atom.
:::

## Purification

:::trap
**Paper chromatography is partition chromatography**, not adsorption. Its stationary phase is the water held in the paper. TLC and column chromatography are adsorption methods.
:::


## Lassaigne's test

:::trap
- Acidify with **acetic acid**, not $\ce{H2SO4}$, for the lead acetate test: $\ce{H2SO4}$ would give a white $\ce{PbSO4}$ precipitate.
- Boil the extract with **$\ce{HNO3}$** before $\ce{AgNO3}$ when N or S is present: it destroys $\ce{CN-}$ and $\ce{S^2-}$, which would also react with $\ce{Ag+}$.
- N and S together give **blood red** ($\ce{SCN-}$), **not** Prussian blue. With excess sodium, NaSCN breaks into NaCN and $\ce{Na2S}$, and the usual tests work.
:::

- $\ce{CCl4}$ gives **no** AgCl precipitate with $\ce{AgNO3}$: its Cl is bonded covalently, not present as $\ce{Cl-}$. That is why sodium fusion is needed.
- Prussian blue is $\ce{Fe4[Fe(CN)6]3}$: iron(III) hexacyanidoferrate(II).
- (Exam extra) Hydrazine, $\ce{NH2NH2}$, has N but no C, so no NaCN forms and the test for N fails.

## Quantitative analysis

:::trap
**Kjeldahl's method fails** for nitro and azo compounds and for N in a ring (pyridine). Their N does not turn into ammonium sulphate. Use Dumas for these.
:::

- KOH absorbs $\ce{CO2}$ because $\ce{CO2}$ is acidic. Anhydrous $\ce{CaCl2}$ absorbs water.
- In Dumas, subtract the **aqueous tension** from the measured pressure: the gas is collected over a solution and is wet.
- In Kjeldahl, 1 mol $\ce{H2SO4}$ neutralises **2 mol** $\ce{NH3}$. Forgetting the 2 halves the answer.
