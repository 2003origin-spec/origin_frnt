## Units and measurement

- The SI has **7** base units. Amount of substance (mole) is one of them; the litre, the degree Celsius and the gram are **not** SI base units.
- The kelvin is written **K**, never °K. A temperature in kelvin can never be negative.
- The old mass standard (since 1889) was a **platinum–iridium cylinder** kept at Sèvres, France. The current definition (NCERT Table 1.2) fixes the value of **Planck's constant**, $h = 6.62607015 \times 10^{-34}$ J s.

:::trap
**Mass is not weight.** Mass is the amount of matter and is the same everywhere. Weight is the pull of gravity on it and changes from place to place.
:::

## Significant figures

:::trap
Zeros are the usual trap:
- **0.0052** has 2 (leading zeros only mark the decimal point);
- **2.005** has 4 (zeros between digits count);
- **0.200** has 3 (trailing zeros after a decimal point count);
- **100** has 1, but **100.0** has 4.
:::

- Counted numbers (2 balls, 20 eggs) are **exact** and have infinitely many significant figures. They never limit the answer.
- In scientific notation every digit counts: $8.256 \times 10^{-3}$ has 4.
- When you add, look at **decimal places**. When you multiply, look at **significant figures**. Don't mix the two rules up.
- Round only the **final** answer, not each step.

:::trap
**Rounding a 5 (NCERT rule):** if the digit you remove is exactly 5, the digit before it goes **up only if it is odd**. So 6.35 → **6.4**, but 6.25 → **6.2**. Many books always round 5 up; in NCERT-based questions use this rule.
:::

:::trap
**Precise is not accurate.** Readings of 1.95 g and 1.93 g agree with each other (precise), but if the true value is 2.00 g they are not accurate.
:::

## Laws of chemical combination

- **Definite vs multiple proportions:** definite proportions is about **one** compound (fixed composition). Multiple proportions compares **two or more** compounds of the same two elements.
- To test multiple proportions, fix the mass of one element first. NCERT Exercise 1.21: per 28 g of N, oxygen is 32, 64, 32 and 80 g, a ratio of 2 : 4 : 2 : 5.

:::trap
**Gay Lussac's law is for gases only**, all at the same $T$ and $P$. 2 volumes $\ce{H2}$ + 1 volume $\ce{O2}$ → 2 volumes of water **vapour**. Once the water condenses to a liquid, its volume no longer fits the ratio. Volumes are **not** conserved: 3 volumes of gas gave 2.
:::

- Avogadro's law counts **molecules**, not atoms. Equal volumes of $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{O3}$ (same $T$, $P$) have equal numbers of molecules but different numbers of atoms.
- Dalton's theory could not explain Gay Lussac's law. Avogadro did, by taking the gas particles to be molecules of more than one atom (we now know $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{O2}$ are diatomic).
- (Exam extra) Parts of Dalton's theory are now known to be wrong: atoms can be divided (electrons, protons, neutrons), and atoms of one element can differ in mass (**isotopes**).

## Atomic mass and the mole

:::remember
- The standard is $^{12}$C = **exactly 12 u** (since 1961). 1 u $= 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}$ g. "amu" is now **u**.
- 1 mol = **exactly** $6.02214076 \times 10^{23}$ entities (the value has been fixed by definition).
- Periodic-table atomic masses are **average** atomic masses, so they are rarely whole numbers (Cl 35.45, C 12.011).
:::

:::trap
**Say which particle.** "1 mol of oxygen" is unclear: 1 mol of $\ce{O2}$ molecules has $2N_A$ atoms. 1 mol of $\ce{H2O}$ has $3N_A$ atoms and $10N_A$ electrons.
:::

- **Molar mass vs mass of one particle:** water is 18 g mol⁻¹, but one molecule weighs $18/N_A \approx 2.99 \times 10^{-23}$ g.
- NaCl has no molecules, so use "formula unit" and "formula mass", not "molecule".

:::trap
**22.7 or 22.4?** NCERT uses **22.7 L mol⁻¹** (STP = 273.15 K, **1 bar**). 22.4 L mol⁻¹ is for 273.15 K and **1 atm**. Either value applies only to **gases**, never to liquids or solids. Check which value the question gives.
:::

## Empirical and molecular formulas

- Some compounds share an empirical formula: glucose $\ce{C6H12O6}$, ethanoic acid $\ce{C2H4O2}$ and methanal $\ce{HCHO}$ are all $\ce{CH2O}$. Benzene $\ce{C6H6}$ and ethyne $\ce{C2H2}$ are both CH.
- For $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ the empirical and molecular formulas are the same ($n = 1$).

:::trap
Don't round a mole ratio like 1.5 or 1.33 to the nearest whole number. Multiply all ratios: × 2 for 1.5, × 3 for 1.33 or 1.67, × 4 for 1.25 or 1.75. Round only values very close to whole numbers (2.98 → 3).
:::

## Stoichiometry

:::trap
**The limiting reagent is not just the one with fewer moles or less mass.** Divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient; the **smallest** result is limiting. In $\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}$, 1 mol $\ce{N2}$ + 2 mol $\ce{H2}$: $\ce{H2}$ is limiting (2/3 < 1/1) even though it has more moles.
:::

- Coefficients give ratios of **moles** (and gas volumes), never of **masses**. Always convert grams to moles first.
- Balance by changing **coefficients only**. Changing a subscript changes the substance.

## Concentration

:::trap
- **Molarity** uses the volume of the **solution**. **Molality** uses the mass of the **solvent**.
- **Mass per cent** divides by the mass of the **solution** (solute + solvent). 2 g in 18 g water is 10%, not 11.1%.
:::

:::tip
**M**olarity uses **M**easured volume, which swells and shrinks with temperature, so it changes. Molality, mass per cent and mole fraction use only masses and moles, so they don't.
:::

- Mole fractions of all components add up to **1**, and have no unit.
- (Exam extra) For a very dilute aqueous solution, molarity ≈ molality, because 1 L of such a solution holds about 1 kg of water.
