## 1. Chemistry and its development

- **Chemistry** studies the preparation, properties, structure and reactions of material substances. It is also called the science of atoms and molecules.
- It grew out of the search for the **philosopher's stone** (base metals into gold) and the **elixir of life**. Modern chemistry took shape in 18th-century Europe.
- In ancient India chemistry was called **Rasayan Shastra** (also Rastantra, Ras Kriya, Rasvidya).
- **Acharya Kanda** (Kashyap, born 600 BCE) proposed tiny indivisible particles called **Paramãnu**, in the **Vaiseshika Sutras**, about 2500 years before Dalton.
- **Nagarjuna** wrote **Rasratnakar** (mercury compounds, extraction of metals). **Chakrapani** discovered mercury sulphide and is credited with inventing soap.
- **Charaka Samhita** describes metal **bhasmas**, now known to contain nanoparticles.
- Uses: fertilisers, drugs (cisplatin and taxol for cancer, AZT for AIDS patients), safer substitutes for CFCs, superconducting ceramics, conducting polymers, optical fibres.

## 2. Matter and its classification

**Matter** is anything that has mass and occupies space.

| State | Volume | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solid | Definite | Definite |
| Liquid | Definite | Takes the container's shape |
| Gas | Not definite | Not definite (fills the container) |

The three states change into one another when temperature and pressure change.

- **Mixture:** two or more pure substances in **any** ratio, separable by **physical** methods (filtration, crystallisation, distillation).
  - **Homogeneous:** uniform composition throughout (sugar solution, air).
  - **Heterogeneous:** not uniform; parts may be visible (salt with sugar, grains with stones).
- **Pure substance:** fixed composition.
  - **Element:** only one kind of atom. The particles may be atoms (Na, Cu) or molecules ($\ce{H2}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{O2}$).
  - **Compound:** atoms of different elements in a **fixed** ratio. Separated only by **chemical** methods. Its properties differ from its elements: $\ce{H2}$ burns and $\ce{O2}$ supports burning, yet water puts out fire.

## 3. Properties and measurement

- **Physical properties** (colour, odour, melting point, boiling point, density) are measured **without** changing what the substance is.
- **Chemical properties** (composition, combustibility, reaction with acids and bases) need a **chemical change** to observe.
- **SI** (Le Système International d'Unités) was set up in **1960** by the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM). In India the **National Physical Laboratory (NPL), New Delhi** maintains the national standards.

| Base quantity | SI unit | Symbol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Length | metre | m |
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Time | second | s |
| Electric current | ampere | A |
| Thermodynamic temperature | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |
| Luminous intensity | candela | cd |

- **Mass** is measured on an **analytical balance**. (Mass is constant; weight changes with gravity.)
- **Volume:** measured with a graduated cylinder, burette or pipette; a **volumetric flask** makes up a known volume of solution.
- **Density:** higher density means more closely packed particles.

## 4. Scientific notation

- Write every number as $N \times 10^n$, with $1 \le N < 10$: $232.508 = 2.32508 \times 10^2$, $0.00016 = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}$.
- To add or subtract, first make the powers of 10 the same: $6.65 \times 10^4 + 0.895 \times 10^4 = 7.545 \times 10^4$.

## 5. Significant figures

**Significant figures** are the digits known with certainty **plus one** uncertain digit. 11.2 mL means 11 is certain and 2 is uncertain ($\pm 1$ in the last digit is understood).

| Rule | Example | Sig. figs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All non-zero digits count | 285 cm | 3 |
| Zeros before the first non-zero digit don't | 0.0052 | 2 |
| Zeros between non-zero digits count | 2.005 | 4 |
| Trailing zeros after a decimal point count | 0.200 g | 3 |
| Trailing zeros with no decimal point don't | 100 | 1 |
| Exact counts have infinitely many | 20 eggs | ∞ |
| In scientific notation, all digits count | $4.01 \times 10^2$ | 3 |

- Scientific notation removes doubt: $1 \times 10^2$ (1), $1.0 \times 10^2$ (2), $1.00 \times 10^2$ (3). "100." (decimal point written) has 3; "100.0" has 4.
- **Rounding off (NCERT rule):** if the digit removed is more than 5, raise the one before it by 1 (1.386 → 1.39). If less than 5, leave it (4.334 → 4.33). If it is **exactly 5**, raise the one before it only if that digit is **odd**: 6.35 → 6.4, but 6.25 → 6.2.

## 6. Precision and accuracy

- **Precision:** how close repeated measurements are to **each other**.
- **Accuracy:** how close a measurement is to the **true value**.

## 7. Dimensional analysis (unit factor method)

- A **unit factor** is a ratio equal to 1, such as $\dfrac{2.54\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{in}}$. Multiplying by it changes the unit but not the quantity.
- Choose the factor that has the **wanted unit on top**. Units cancel like numbers.
- $3\ \text{in} \times \dfrac{2.54\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{in}} = 7.62$ cm. 2 days $= 2 \times 24 \times 60 \times 60 = 172800$ s.
- For areas and volumes, square or cube the factor: $1\ \text{m}^3 = 10^6\ \text{cm}^3$, so 2 L $= 2000$ cm³ $= 2 \times 10^{-3}$ m³.

## 8. Laws of chemical combination

1. **Conservation of mass** (Lavoisier, 1789): matter is neither created nor destroyed. Total mass stays the same in physical and chemical changes.
2. **Definite proportions** (Proust; also called definite composition): a compound always contains the same elements in the same proportion **by mass**, whatever its source. Proust found natural and synthetic cupric carbonate had the same composition.
3. **Multiple proportions** (Dalton, 1803): when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a **fixed mass** of the other are in a small whole-number ratio. 2 g of hydrogen combines with 16 g of oxygen (water) or 32 g (hydrogen peroxide): 16 : 32 = 1 : 2.
4. **Gaseous volumes** (Gay Lussac, 1808): gases that react or are formed are in a simple whole-number ratio **by volume**, if all are at the same $T$ and $P$. 100 mL $\ce{H2}$ + 50 mL $\ce{O2}$ give 100 mL water **vapour** (2 : 1 : 2). It is the law of definite proportions by volume.
5. **Avogadro's law** (1811): equal volumes of all gases at the same $T$ and $P$ contain equal numbers of **molecules**.

Avogadro explained Gay Lussac's results by taking the gas particles to be molecules of more than one atom (we now know $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{O2}$ are diatomic). His idea did not gain much support until **Cannizzaro** stressed it at the first international chemistry conference, **Karlsruhe, 1860**.

## 9. Dalton's atomic theory (1808)

Published in *A New System of Chemical Philosophy*:
1. Matter is made of indivisible atoms.
2. Atoms of one element are identical, including in mass; atoms of different elements differ in mass.
3. Compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in a fixed ratio.
4. Chemical reactions only rearrange atoms; atoms are neither created nor destroyed.

It explained the laws of chemical combination, but **not** the law of gaseous volumes, and not **why** atoms combine.

## 10. Atomic, molecular and formula mass

- The standard is the **carbon-12** atom, given exactly 12 u (agreed in 1961); earlier, hydrogen was taken as 1. 1 u is $\tfrac{1}{12}$ of the mass of one $^{12}$C atom. Atomic masses are now found by **mass spectrometry**.
- **Average atomic mass** weights each isotope by its abundance. Carbon ($^{12}$C 98.892%, $^{13}$C 1.108%, a trace of $^{14}$C) averages **12.011 u**.
- **Molecular mass** = sum of atomic masses in a molecule. Glucose, $\ce{C6H12O6}$: $6(12.011) + 12(1.008) + 6(16.00) = 180.162$ u.
- **Formula mass** is used for ionic solids like NaCl, which have no separate molecules (each $\ce{Na+}$ is surrounded by six $\ce{Cl-}$ and vice versa): 58.5 u.

## 11. The mole

- **One mole** contains exactly $6.02214076 \times 10^{23}$ elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, electrons or any stated group). This number is the **Avogadro constant** $N_A$ (in mol⁻¹).
- A mole of anything has the **same number** of entities: 1 mol of H atoms, 1 mol of water molecules and 1 mol of NaCl formula units each contain $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ of them.
- **Molar mass** = mass of one mole in grams. It has the same number as the atomic or molecular mass in u: water 18.02 g mol⁻¹, NaCl 58.5 g mol⁻¹.

## 12. Percentage composition and formulas

- **Mass per cent** of each element checks a sample's purity and leads to its formula. Ethanol: C 52.14%, H 13.13%, O 34.73%.
- **Empirical formula:** simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. **Molecular formula:** actual number of each atom in a molecule.
- **Steps (from mass per cent):**
  1. Take 100 g, so each per cent becomes grams.
  2. Divide each by its atomic mass to get moles.
  3. Divide all by the smallest number of moles.
  4. If the ratios are not whole numbers, multiply all by a small number (2, 3, …) to make them whole.
  5. Write the empirical formula; find $n$ = molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass; multiply.
- NCERT example: 4.07% H, 24.27% C, 71.65% Cl gives $\ce{CH2Cl}$; molar mass 98.96 g gives $\ce{C2H4Cl2}$.

## 13. Stoichiometry and balancing equations

- **Stoichiometry** (Greek *stoicheion*, element + *metron*, measure): calculating the masses (sometimes volumes) of reactants and products.
- A balanced equation can be read in moles, molecules, masses or (for gases) volumes:

$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g)} \\ & \ce{-> CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)} \end{aligned}$$

1 mol + 2 mol → 1 mol + 2 mol; 16 g + 64 g → 44 g + 36 g; 22.7 L + 45.4 L → 22.7 L + 45.4 L (NCERT's figures, treating all four as gases at STP).

- The numbers in front (2 for $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$) are **stoichiometric coefficients**. (g), (l), (s) show the state.
- **Balancing:** the same number of atoms of each element on both sides (conservation of mass). Change only the **coefficients**, never the subscripts. For propane: balance C, then H, then O:

$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C3H8 + 5O2} \\ & \ce{-> 3CO2 + 4H2O} \end{aligned}$$

- **Limiting reagent:** the reactant used up first. It fixes the amount of product; the other reactant is left over (in **excess**).

## 14. Concentration of solutions

| Term | Definition | Changes with $T$? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mass per cent | g solute per 100 g **solution** | No |
| Mole fraction | moles of one part ÷ total moles | No |
| Molarity (M) | mol solute per litre of **solution** | **Yes** (volume changes) |
| Molality (m) | mol solute per kg of **solvent** | No |

- **Stock solution:** a concentrated solution that is diluted to make weaker ones. For 1 L of 0.2 M NaOH from 1 M stock, take 200 mL and add water up to 1 L; the 0.2 mol of NaOH stays the same.
- **0.50 mol $\ce{Na2CO3}$** is an amount (53 g). **0.50 M $\ce{Na2CO3}$** is a concentration (0.50 mol in every litre of solution).
