## Words that trip people up

:::trap
The **oxidising agent is reduced**, and the **reducing agent is oxidised**. In $\ce{Zn + Cu^2+ -> Zn^2+ + Cu}$, Zn is oxidised, so Zn is the **reducing** agent.
:::

:::tip
**OIL RIG:** Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). In a cell: **AN OX, RED CAT**: oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode. (Memory aids, not in NCERT.)
:::

## Oxidation number traps

:::trap
**Look for O–O bonds before you trust the formula.** The formula method gives S = +8 in $\ce{H2SO5}$, but S has only 6 valence electrons. $\ce{H2SO5}$ has one peroxide link (two O at −1), so S is **+6**. The same fallacy in $\ce{CrO5}$ (exam extra): two peroxide groups, so Cr is **+6**, not +10.
:::

- **Same element, different ONs in one compound:**
  - $\ce{NH4NO3}$: N is −3 (in $\ce{NH4+}$) and +5 (in $\ce{NO3-}$);
  - $\ce{CH3COOH}$: C is −3 ($\ce{CH3}$) and +3 ($\ce{COOH}$), average 0;
  - $\ce{CH3CH2OH}$: C is −3 and −1, average −2.
- **Averages that look strange:** $\ce{Fe3O4}$ gives Fe = 8/3 (really one Fe at +2 and two at +3). $\ce{KI3}$ gives I = −1/3 (it is $\ce{K+}$ with $\ce{I3-}$, which is $\ce{I2}$ held by $\ce{I-}$).
- **Exceptions to learn:** H is −1 in $\ce{NaH}$ and $\ce{CaH2}$; O is −1 in $\ce{Na2O2}$ and $\ce{BaO2}$, −½ in $\ce{KO2}$, +2 in $\ce{OF2}$.
- F is −1 in **every** compound. O shows a positive ON only with F.
- (Exam extra) ON is not valency. In $\ce{CH2Cl2}$ carbon has valency 4 but ON 0.

## Who can be oxidant, who can be reductant?

:::remember
- Element at its **highest** ON: that element can only be **reduced**, so through it the compound acts only as an oxidant: $\ce{KMnO4}$ (Mn +7), $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ (Cr +6), $\ce{HNO3}$ (N +5).
- Element at its **lowest** ON: that element can only be oxidised, so it acts only as a reductant: $\ce{H2S}$ (S −2), $\ce{I-}$ (−1), $\ce{NH3}$ (N −3).
- **In between**: can act both ways: $\ce{SO2}$ (S +4), $\ce{H2O2}$ (O −1), $\ce{HNO2}$ (N +3).
- NCERT: $\ce{SO2}$ and $\ce{H2O2}$ are both oxidants and reductants; $\ce{O3}$ and $\ce{HNO3}$ act only as oxidants.
:::

- **Excess decides the product (NCERT idea):** with excess reductant you get the lower ON; with excess oxidant, the higher ON.
  - C with limited $\ce{O2}$ gives CO (C +2); with excess $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{CO2}$ (C +4).
  - $\ce{P4}$ with limited $\ce{Cl2}$ gives $\ce{PCl3}$; with excess $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{PCl5}$.
- **Halogens:** oxidising power $\ce{F2} > \ce{Cl2} > \ce{Br2} > \ce{I2}$. Reducing power of halide ions goes the other way: $\ce{I-} > \ce{Br-} > \ce{Cl-} > \ce{F-}$. So HI is the best reductant among the hydrohalic acids.
- **Thiosulphate:** $\ce{I2}$ (milder) takes it only to tetrathionate (S: +2 → +2.5). $\ce{Br2}$ (stronger) takes it to sulphate (S: +2 → +6).
- **Conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$:** with a chloride it gives only HCl gas. With a bromide, the HBr formed reduces $\ce{H2SO4}$, giving red-brown $\ce{Br2}$ vapour. HBr is a stronger reductant than HCl.
- $\ce{AgF2}$ is a very strong oxidant: Ag(II) is unstable and readily takes an electron to become Ag(I).

## Classifying reactions

:::trap
$\ce{2KClO3 -> 2KCl + 3O2}$ is **not** disproportionation. Cl is reduced (+5 → −1) but **O** is oxidised (−2 → 0): two different elements. In disproportionation the **same element** in the **same** state goes both up and down.
:::

- Not every decomposition is redox: $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$ has no ON change.
- (Exam extra) A decomposition with no free element in the products can still be redox. In $\ce{NH4NO3 -> N2O + 2H2O}$, N goes −3 → +1 and +5 → +1. Always check the ONs.
- (Exam extra) **Comproportionation** is the reverse of disproportionation: two states of one element meet in the middle.

$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{IO3- + 5I- + 6H+} \\ & \ce{-> 3I2 + 3H2O} \end{aligned}$$

- Disproportionation needs an element with at least **three** ONs, in the **middle** one. So $\ce{F2}$ (only 0 and −1) and $\ce{ClO4-}$ (Cl already at +7) can't disproportionate.
- Metal ions can disproportionate too (NCERT Exercises 7.21 and 7.24 ask about this):

$$\ce{2Cu+ -> Cu^2+ + Cu}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2Mn^3+ + 2H2O} \\ & \ce{-> Mn^2+ + MnO2 + 4H+} \end{aligned}$$

## Electrode potentials

:::trap
Table 7.1 lists **reduction** potentials. A **higher** (more positive) $E^\ominus$ means the **oxidised** form is a stronger oxidant. A **lower** (more negative) $E^\ominus$ means the **reduced** form is a stronger reductant. Don't call $\ce{F-}$ a strong reductant because $\ce{F2}$ is at the top: $\ce{F-}$ is the **weakest** reductant in the table.
:::

- An oxidised form oxidises the reduced form of any couple **below** it. $\ce{Fe^3+}$ (+0.77 V) oxidises $\ce{I-}$ (+0.54 V) but **not** $\ce{Br-}$ (+1.09 V); $\ce{Br2}$ oxidises $\ce{Fe^2+}$.
- A metal displaces from solution any metal whose couple lies **above** it in Table 7.1 (more positive $E^\ominus$). Zn (−0.76 V) displaces Cu (+0.34 V), not the other way round. Order of displacing power: Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Cu > Ag.
- Metals with negative $E^\ominus$ can give $\ce{H2}$ with dilute acids such as HCl. Cu (+0.34 V), Ag and Au can't.
- (Exam extra) Li (−3.05 V) is the strongest reducing agent in water, even though Na and K react with water more violently. Li⁺ is very strongly hydrated.
- **Daniell cell:** Zn is the anode and the **negative** electrode; Cu is the cathode and positive. Electrons go Zn → Cu in the wire; the current goes Cu → Zn.
- A redox couple is written **oxidised/reduced**: $\ce{Zn^2+}$/Zn, never Zn/$\ce{Zn^2+}$.

## Titrations

- $\ce{KMnO4}$ is its own indicator (pink). $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ needs diphenylamine (blue). Iodine titrations use starch (blue that disappears).
- (Exam extra) $\ce{KMnO4}$ titrations are acidified with $\ce{H2SO4}$, not HCl: $\ce{MnO4-}$ (+1.51 V) would oxidise $\ce{Cl-}$ (+1.36 V) to $\ce{Cl2}$ and waste titrant.

## NCERT lines to know

:::remember
- "Where there is oxidation, there is always reduction."
- The idea of electron transfer in covalent compounds is for **book-keeping** only.
- $\ce{F-}$ can be turned into $\ce{F2}$ only by electrolysis.
- Fluorine attacks water and frees $\ce{O2}$, so halogen displacements with $\ce{F2}$ are not done in water.
- The hypochlorite from $\ce{Cl2 + 2OH-}$ is the household bleaching agent.
- The hydrogen electrode has $E^\ominus = 0.00$ V by convention.
:::
