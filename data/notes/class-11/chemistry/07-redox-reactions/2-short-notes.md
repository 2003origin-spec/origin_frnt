## 1. Three ways to define oxidation

NCERT builds the idea in three steps. Each step is wider than the one before.

| Idea | Oxidation | Reduction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical | Add O or an electronegative element; remove H or an electropositive element | Remove O or an electronegative element; add H or an electropositive element |
| Electron transfer | Loss of electrons | Gain of electrons |
| Oxidation number | ON increases | ON decreases |

- Oxidation and reduction **always happen together**. That is why the reaction is called **redox**.
- Classical examples: $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$ (Mg oxidised); $\ce{2H2S + O2 -> 2S + 2H2O}$ (H₂S oxidised, H removed); $\ce{2HgO -> 2Hg + O2}$ (HgO reduced).
- $\ce{2HgCl2 + SnCl2 -> Hg2Cl2 + SnCl4}$: $\ce{HgCl2}$ is reduced (Hg added) and $\ce{SnCl2}$ is oxidised (Cl added).

## 2. Redox as electron transfer

- $\ce{2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl}$ is really two **half reactions**:

$$\ce{2Na -> 2Na+ + 2e-}$$
$$\ce{Cl2 + 2e- -> 2Cl-}$$

- **Oxidising agent (oxidant):** accepts electrons. It is itself reduced.
- **Reducing agent (reductant):** donates electrons. It is itself oxidised.
- $\ce{2Na + H2 -> 2NaH}$ is redox: NaH is ionic ($\ce{Na+H-}$), so Na loses electrons and H gains them.

## 3. Competitive electron transfer

| Experiment | What you see | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zn strip in $\ce{Cu^2+}$ solution | Red Cu coats the strip; blue colour fades | Goes almost fully to products |
| Cu strip in $\ce{Ag+}$ solution | Solution turns blue; Ag deposits | Goes almost fully to products |
| Co in $\ce{Ni^2+}$ solution | Both $\ce{Co^2+}$ and $\ce{Ni^2+}$ present | Neither side is greatly favoured |

- Cu does **not** react with $\ce{Zn^2+}$ solution: even the very sensitive $\ce{H2S}$ test (black CuS) finds no $\ce{Cu^2+}$.
- Zn²⁺ is detected by passing $\ce{H2S}$ into the solution made alkaline with ammonia: white ZnS.
- Tendency to release electrons: **Zn > Cu > Ag**. Lists like this give the metal activity (electrochemical) series.

## 4. Oxidation number

- **Definition:** the charge an atom would carry if every shared electron pair belonged fully to the **more electronegative** atom. It is a book-keeping device, not a real charge.
- "Oxidation state" is used in the same sense: in $\ce{CO2}$ the oxidation state of C is +4.
- **Rules (NCERT):**
  1. Free element: 0 ($\ce{H2}$, $\ce{O3}$, $\ce{P4}$, $\ce{S8}$, Na).
  2. Monatomic ion: equal to its charge. Alkali metals +1, alkaline earth metals +2, Al +3 in all compounds.
  3. Oxygen −2; peroxides ($\ce{H2O2}$, $\ce{Na2O2}$) −1; superoxides ($\ce{KO2}$, $\ce{RbO2}$) −½; $\ce{OF2}$ +2; $\ce{O2F2}$ +1.
  4. Hydrogen +1; but −1 when bonded to a metal in a binary compound ($\ce{LiH}$, $\ce{NaH}$, $\ce{CaH2}$).
  5. Fluorine always −1. Cl, Br, I are −1 in halides but positive with oxygen.
  6. Sum = 0 for a compound, = charge for an ion.
- Metals show positive ON. Non-metals show positive or negative ON. Transition metals usually show several positive states.
- **Stock notation:** the ON in Roman numerals after the metal: $\ce{FeO}$ is Fe(II)O, $\ce{Fe2O3}$ is Fe₂(III)O₃, $\ce{SnCl4}$ is Sn(IV)Cl₄, $\ce{HAuCl4}$ is HAu(III)Cl₄.

## 5. Redox in terms of oxidation number

- **Oxidation:** increase in ON. **Reduction:** decrease in ON.
- **Oxidant:** raises another element's ON (and its own ON falls).
- **Reductant:** lowers another element's ON (and its own ON rises).
- **Redox reaction:** a reaction in which ON changes.

:::example{title="NCERT Problem 7.4 idea"}
$\ce{2Cu2O + Cu2S -> 6Cu + SO2}$: Cu goes +1 → 0 (reduced), S goes −2 → +4 (oxidised). Cu(I) is the oxidant; the S of $\ce{Cu2S}$ is the reductant.
:::

## 6. Types of redox reactions

**Combination** ($\ce{A + B -> C}$): in NCERT's combination redox reactions, A or B (or both) is an element. Example: $\ce{3Mg + N2 -> Mg3N2}$. All combustion with $\ce{O2}$ is redox.

**Decomposition** ($\ce{C -> A + B}$): in NCERT's decomposition redox reactions, at least one product is an element. Examples: $\ce{2H2O -> 2H2 + O2}$, $\ce{2KClO3 -> 2KCl + 3O2}$. Not redox: $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$.

**Displacement** ($\ce{X + YZ -> XZ + Y}$):
- **Metal displacement:** a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one. Used in metallurgy: $\ce{TiCl4 + 2Mg -> Ti + 2MgCl2}$, $\ce{Cr2O3 + 2Al -> Al2O3 + 2Cr}$, $\ce{V2O5 + 5Ca -> 2V + 5CaO}$.
- **Hydrogen displacement:**
  - from cold water: all alkali metals and Ca, Sr, Ba;
  - from steam: less active metals such as Mg and Fe;
  - from acids: many metals, even Cd and Sn, which don't react with steam. Rate: fastest for Mg, slowest for Fe (among Mg, Zn, Fe);
  - Ag and Au don't react even with HCl.
- **Oxygen displacement (rare):** $\ce{2H2O + 2F2 -> 4HF + O2}$.
- **Halogen displacement:** oxidising power $\ce{F2} > \ce{Cl2} > \ce{Br2} > \ce{I2}$. $\ce{Cl2}$ displaces $\ce{Br-}$ and $\ce{I-}$; $\ce{Br2}$ displaces $\ce{I-}$. This is the basis of the **layer test** for $\ce{Br-}$ and $\ce{I-}$ (the freed halogen colours a $\ce{CCl4}$ layer).
- $\ce{F-}$ can't be oxidised to $\ce{F2}$ by any chemical; only by electrolysis.

**Disproportionation:** one element, in one oxidation state, is oxidised and reduced at the same time. It needs an element with **at least three** oxidation states, present in the intermediate one.

$$\ce{2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{P4 + 3OH- + 3H2O} \\ & \ce{-> PH3 + 3H2PO2-} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{S8 + 12OH-} \\ & \ce{-> 4S^2- + 2S2O3^2- + 6H2O} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Cl2 + 2OH-} \\ & \ce{-> ClO- + Cl- + H2O} \end{aligned}$$

- O: −1 → 0 and −2. P: 0 → −3 and +1. S: 0 → −2 and +2. Cl: 0 → +1 and −1.
- The last one makes household bleach: $\ce{ClO-}$ oxidises coloured stains to colourless compounds.
- Br₂ and I₂ behave like Cl₂, but **F₂ does not**: $\ce{2F2 + 2OH- -> 2F- + OF2 + H2O}$. F can't show a positive ON.
- $\ce{ClO-}$, $\ce{ClO2-}$, $\ce{ClO3-}$ disproportionate; $\ce{ClO4-}$ (Cl at +7, its highest) can't.

## 7. Fractional oxidation numbers

A fractional ON is only an **average**. The structure shows the real, whole-number states.

| Species | Average ON | Real ONs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ce{C3O2}$ (carbon suboxide) | C: 4/3 | +2, 0, +2 |
| $\ce{Br3O8}$ | Br: 16/3 | +6, +4, +6 |
| $\ce{S4O6^2-}$ (tetrathionate) | S: 2.5 | +5, 0, 0, +5 |

- Mixed oxides also give fractions: $\ce{Fe3O4}$, $\ce{Mn3O4}$, $\ce{Pb3O4}$.
- $\ce{Pb3O4}$ behaves as $\ce{2PbO + PbO2}$. With HCl, the $\ce{PbO2}$ (Pb +4) oxidises $\ce{Cl-}$ to $\ce{Cl2}$. With $\ce{HNO3}$ only the basic PbO reacts (acid–base), and $\ce{PbO2}$ is left over.
- Real fractional states also exist: $\ce{O2+}$ (+½) and $\ce{O2-}$ (−½).

## 8. Balancing redox equations

**Oxidation number method**
1. Write correct formulas for all reactants and products.
2. Find the atoms whose ON changes.
3. Make the total increase equal to the total decrease (multiply as needed).
4. In water, balance the charges with $\ce{H+}$ (acidic) or $\ce{OH-}$ (basic).
5. Balance H by adding $\ce{H2O}$, then check that O is balanced.

**Half-reaction (ion–electron) method**
1. Write the unbalanced ionic equation and split it into oxidation and reduction halves.
2. Balance atoms other than O and H.
3. Acidic: add $\ce{H2O}$ to balance O, then $\ce{H+}$ to balance H.
4. Add electrons to balance the charge; multiply so both halves have the same number of electrons.
5. Add the halves, cancel electrons, and check atoms and charges.
6. **Basic medium:** balance as if acidic, then add as many $\ce{OH-}$ as there are $\ce{H+}$ to **both** sides; join $\ce{H+ + OH-}$ into $\ce{H2O}$.

:::example{title="Dichromate and iron(II), acid"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6e-} \\ & \ce{-> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O} \end{aligned}$$
$$\ce{6Fe^2+ -> 6Fe^3+ + 6e-}$$
Adding:
$$\begin{aligned} &\ce{6Fe^2+ + Cr2O7^2- + 14H+} \\ &\ce{-> 6Fe^3+ + 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Charge: $12 - 2 + 14 = 24$ on the left, $18 + 6 = 24$ on the right.
:::

## 9. Redox titrations

| Titration | How the end point shows |
| --- | --- |
| $\ce{MnO4-}$ with $\ce{Fe^2+}$ or $\ce{C2O4^2-}$ | $\ce{MnO4-}$ is its own indicator: first lasting pink (visible at about $10^{-6}$ mol dm⁻³) |
| $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ | Not a self-indicator. Diphenylamine is oxidised just after the equivalence point: intense blue |
| Iodine methods | Starch gives intense blue with $\ce{I2}$; the blue disappears when thiosulphate has used up all the $\ce{I2}$ |

For example, $\ce{Cu^2+}$ is estimated by letting it free iodine, then titrating that iodine with thiosulphate:

$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2Cu^2+ + 4I-} \\ & \ce{-> Cu2I2 + I2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{I2 + 2S2O3^2-} \\ & \ce{-> 2I- + S4O6^2-} \end{aligned}$$

$\ce{I2}$ is insoluble in water but stays in solution with KI as $\ce{KI3}$.

## 10. Limits of the oxidation number idea

The redox idea is still growing. Today oxidation is seen as a **decrease in electron density** around an atom, and reduction as an **increase**.

## 11. Redox reactions and electrode processes

- **Redox couple:** the oxidised and reduced forms of a substance together, written oxidised/reduced: $\ce{Zn^2+}$/Zn, $\ce{Cu^2+}$/Cu.
- **Daniell cell:** Zn rod in $\ce{ZnSO4}$, Cu rod in $\ce{CuSO4}$, joined by a wire and a **salt bridge** (KCl or $\ce{NH4NO3}$ set in agar jelly). The salt bridge lets ions move between the solutions without mixing them.
- Electrons flow from Zn (anode, oxidation) to Cu (cathode, reduction) through the wire. The current flows the opposite way.
- **Standard electrode potential** $E^\ominus$: all species at unit concentration, gases at 1 atm, 298 K. The hydrogen electrode is 0.00 V.
- In NCERT Table 7.1 all the reactions are written as **reductions**. Going **up** the table, the oxidised form is a stronger **oxidising agent** ($\ce{F2}$ at the top, +2.87 V). Going **down**, the reduced form is a stronger **reducing agent** (Li at the bottom, −3.05 V).
