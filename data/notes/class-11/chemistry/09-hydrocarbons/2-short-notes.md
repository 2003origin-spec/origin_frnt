## 1. What hydrocarbons are

- Compounds of **carbon and hydrogen only**. Main sources: petroleum, natural gas and coal.
- LPG is a domestic fuel; CNG runs vehicles. Petrol, diesel and kerosene come from fractional distillation of petroleum.

| Class | Bonds | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alkanes (saturated) | only single bonds | ethane |
| Alkenes, alkynes (unsaturated) | C=C or C≡C | ethene, ethyne |
| Aromatic | special cyclic, delocalised π | benzene |

## 2. Alkanes: structure and isomers

- Formula $\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2}$. Each C is **sp³** and tetrahedral; bond angles are about 109.5° (exactly 109.5° in methane). C–C = 154 pm, C–H = 112 pm.
- Old name **paraffins** ("little affinity"): they are inert to acids, bases and most reagents.
- **Chain isomers** differ in the carbon chain. Number of structural isomers: $\ce{C4H10}$ 2, $\ce{C5H12}$ 3, $\ce{C6H14}$ 5, $\ce{C7H16}$ 9, $\ce{C10H22}$ 75.
- A carbon bonded to 1, 2, 3 or 4 other carbons is **primary (1°), secondary (2°), tertiary (3°) or quaternary (4°)**. End carbons are always 1°.

## 3. Alkanes: physical properties

- Almost non-polar; only weak van der Waals forces. "Like dissolves like": insoluble in water, soluble in non-polar solvents. Grease (higher alkanes) is removed by petrol.
- At 298 K: C1–C4 gases, C5–C17 liquids, C18 and above solids.
- Boiling point rises with molecular mass (bigger surface, stronger van der Waals forces).
- **Branching lowers the boiling point**: the molecule becomes more spherical, so there is less contact area. Pentane 309.1 K > 2-methylbutane 300.9 K > 2,2-dimethylpropane 282.5 K.

## 4. Free radical halogenation (mechanism)

Chlorination of methane needs light or heat. It is a chain reaction in three steps:
1. **Initiation:** light splits $\ce{Cl2}$ by homolysis into two $\ce{Cl^.}$ radicals. Cl–Cl is the weakest bond present.
2. **Propagation:** $\ce{Cl^.}$ takes an H from $\ce{CH4}$, giving $\ce{CH3^.}$ and HCl. Then $\ce{CH3^.}$ takes Cl from $\ce{Cl2}$, giving $\ce{CH3Cl}$ and a new $\ce{Cl^.}$. These two steps repeat.
3. **Termination:** two radicals join: $\ce{Cl^.}$ + $\ce{Cl^.}$, $\ce{CH3^.}$ + $\ce{CH3^.}$ (gives **ethane**, a by-product), or $\ce{CH3^.}$ + $\ce{Cl^.}$.

## 5. Conformations of ethane

- Rotation about a C–C σ bond is possible because the σ electron cloud is symmetrical about the bond axis. The shapes made by this rotation are **conformations** (conformers, rotamers).
- **Staggered:** H atoms on the two carbons as far apart as possible. **Eclipsed:** as close as possible. Anything in between is **skew**.
- Drawn as **Sawhorse** projections (C–C bond seen at an angle) or **Newman** projections (looking straight down the C–C bond; front C is a point, rear C is a circle).
- **Torsional strain** = repulsion between electron clouds of bonds on neighbouring carbons. It depends on the angle of rotation, called the **dihedral (torsional) angle**. Eclipsed has the most, staggered the least, so **staggered is the most stable**.
- Energy difference is only **12.5 kJ/mol**. Collisions supply this at room temperature, so rotation is almost free and the conformers **cannot be isolated**.
- Bond angles and bond lengths are the **same** in every conformation.

## 6. Alkenes: structure and isomerism

- Formula $\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n}$; old name **olefins** (oil forming). Each C of C=C is **sp²**, planar, angles about 120°.
- C=C = one σ bond (sp²–sp², about 397 kJ/mol) + one π bond (sideways p–p, about 284 kJ/mol). Total 681 kJ/mol; length 134 pm.
- The weak, exposed π electrons attract **electrophiles**, so alkenes mainly give **electrophilic addition**.
- **Naming:** choose the longest chain containing C=C; number from the end nearer the double bond; suffix -ene.
- **Structural isomers** start at $\ce{C4H8}$: but-1-ene and but-2-ene are position isomers; 2-methylprop-1-ene is a chain isomer of both.
- **Geometrical (cis–trans) isomers:** rotation about C=C is restricted. They exist only when **each** double-bonded carbon has two **different** groups (XYC=CXY, XYC=CXZ, XYC=CZW).
  - **cis:** same groups on the same side. **trans:** on opposite sides.
  - cis-But-2-ene is polar (μ = 0.33 D); trans-but-2-ene is non-polar (μ ≈ 0), because the two C–CH₃ dipoles cancel.
  - For solids, the trans isomer has the higher melting point (it packs better).
- **Physical:** first three gases, next fourteen liquids. Ethene smells faintly sweet. Each extra $\ce{CH2}$ raises the b.p. by 20–30 K; branching lowers it.

## 7. Markovnikov rule and the peroxide effect

:::remember
**Markovnikov rule:** when an unsymmetrical reagent adds to an unsymmetrical alkene, the **negative part** of the reagent goes to the double-bonded carbon with **fewer hydrogen atoms**.
:::

- **Why (mechanism):** $\ce{H+}$ from HBr attacks the double bond first. On propene this can give a 1° or a 2° carbocation. The **2° carbocation is more stable**, forms faster, and $\ce{Br-}$ joins it, so 2-bromopropane is the main product.
- **Peroxide (Kharash) effect:** with benzoyl peroxide, HBr adds the other way (1-bromopropane). It is a free radical chain:
  1. the peroxide splits; the radicals formed take H from HBr and release $\ce{Br^.}$;
  2. $\ce{Br^.}$ adds to the end $\ce{CH2}$, giving the more stable **2° radical**;
  3. this radical takes H from HBr, giving 1-bromopropane and a new $\ce{Br^.}$.
- Only HBr shows this. H–Cl (430.5 kJ/mol) is too strong to be split by the radical. H–I (296.8 kJ/mol) splits, but the iodine atoms join to form $\ce{I2}$ instead of adding.
- Halogen addition goes through a cyclic **halonium ion** (details in higher classes).

## 8. Alkynes: structure and isomerism

- Formula $\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n-2}$. Each C of C≡C is **sp**; the triple bond and the two atoms on it lie in a straight line (180°). Ethyne is **linear**.
- C≡C = one σ + two π bonds, at right angles to each other. Length 120 pm, bond enthalpy 823 kJ/mol. The π cloud is cylindrical around the C–C axis.
- **Naming:** suffix -yne; numbered from the end nearer the triple bond.
- Isomers: but-1-yne and but-2-yne are position isomers. $\ce{C5H8}$ alkynes: pent-1-yne, pent-2-yne, 3-methylbut-1-yne.
- Ethyne (acetylene) is used in the **oxyacetylene flame** for welding. First three alkynes are gases, next eight liquids.

## 9. Why terminal alkynes are acidic

- An sp orbital has **50% s character** (sp² 33%, sp³ 25%). More s character keeps the electrons closer to the nucleus, so sp carbon is the **most electronegative**.
- It pulls the C–H electron pair towards itself, so the H leaves as $\ce{H+}$ more easily.
- Acid strength: $\ce{HC#CH}$ > $\ce{CH2=CH2}$ > $\ce{CH3-CH3}$.
- Among alkynes: $\ce{HC#CH}$ > $\ce{CH3C#CH}$ ≫ $\ce{CH3C#CCH3}$ (no H on the triple-bonded carbons).
- Only H atoms **on** a triply bonded carbon are acidic, not all the H atoms of an alkyne.
- Additions to alkynes go through a **vinylic cation** and follow Markovnikov's rule. Polyacetylene, made by linear polymerisation, can conduct electricity.

## 10. Benzene: structure and aromaticity

- $\ce{C6H6}$, isolated by Faraday (1825). It forms a **triozonide** (so three C=C), but gives only **one** monosubstituted product, so all six C–H are equal.
- **Kekulé (1865):** a ring of alternate single and double bonds. It predicts **two** 1,2-dibromobenzenes, but only one exists. Kekulé's fix was oscillating double bonds.
- **Resonance:** benzene is a hybrid of the two Kekulé structures, drawn as a hexagon with a circle.
- **Orbital picture:** every C is **sp²**; six C–C and six C–H σ bonds lie in one plane. Each C has one p orbital at right angles to the ring. They overlap all round, giving two "doughnut" π clouds, above and below the ring. The **6 π electrons are delocalised**.
- All C–C bonds are equal, **139 pm**, between single (154 pm) and double (133 pm). So benzene is unusually stable and prefers **substitution** to addition.

:::remember
**Aromatic** = planar ring + complete π delocalisation + $(4n + 2)$ π electrons (**Hückel rule**). Examples: benzene, cyclopentadienyl anion, cycloheptatrienyl cation ($n = 1$, 6 π); naphthalene ($n = 2$, 10 π); anthracene and phenanthrene ($n = 3$, 14 π).
:::

- **Benzenoid** aromatics contain a benzene ring; **non-benzenoid** ones do not.
- Disubstituted benzenes: 1,2 = **ortho**, 1,3 = **meta**, 1,4 = **para**. Example: o-, m- and p-xylene.
- Aromatic hydrocarbons are non-polar, immiscible with water, and burn with a **sooty flame**.

## 11. Electrophilic substitution: mechanism

Nitration, halogenation, sulphonation, Friedel-Crafts alkylation and acylation all follow three steps:
1. **Make the electrophile.** Anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$ (a Lewis acid) gives $\ce{Cl+}$, $\ce{R+}$ or the acylium ion $\ce{RCO+}$, plus $\ce{AlCl4-}$. In nitration, $\ce{H2SO4}$ protonates $\ce{HNO3}$, which loses water to give the **nitronium ion** $\ce{NO2+}$ ($\ce{H2SO4}$ acts as the acid, $\ce{HNO3}$ as the base).
2. **Attack:** $\ce{E+}$ bonds to one ring carbon, which becomes **sp³**. This **σ-complex (arenium ion)** is resonance stabilised but has **lost aromaticity**.
3. **Lose a proton:** $\ce{AlCl4-}$ or $\ce{HSO4-}$ removes $\ce{H+}$ from the sp³ carbon, and aromaticity returns.

## 12. Directive influence of substituents

The group **already on** the ring decides where the next group goes, not the incoming group.

| Group | Effect on ring | Directs to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| –OH, –NH₂, –NHR, –NHCOCH₃, –OCH₃, –CH₃, –C₂H₅ | activating | ortho, para |
| –Cl, –Br, –I | deactivating | ortho, para |
| –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COR, –COOH, –COOR, –SO₃H | deactivating | meta |

- **–OH:** its lone pair is delocalised into the ring (resonance), raising electron density at o and p. Its –I effect lowers density a little, but resonance wins, so the ring is **activated**.
- **Halogens:** strong –I effect lowers density everywhere (**deactivating**), but resonance leaves o and p richer than m, so they still direct **o/p**.
- **–NO₂:** –I and resonance both pull electrons out. o and p become poorer than m, so the electrophile goes **meta**.

## 13. Carcinogenicity and toxicity

- Benzene and **polynuclear hydrocarbons with more than two fused benzene rings** are toxic and **carcinogenic**. They form when tobacco, coal or petroleum burn incompletely, and they damage DNA.
- NCERT examples: 1,2-benzanthracene, 3-methylcholanthrene, 1,2-benzpyrene, 1,2,5,6-dibenzanthracene, 9,10-dimethyl-1,2-benzanthracene.
