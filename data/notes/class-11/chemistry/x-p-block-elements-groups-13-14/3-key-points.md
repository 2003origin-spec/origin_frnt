## Trends that break the pattern

:::trap
**Gallium is smaller than aluminium** (135 pm vs 143 pm), even though it is lower in the group. Its 3d electrons shield the nucleus poorly. For the same reason Ga's first ionisation enthalpy (579) is slightly **above** Al's (577 kJ/mol).
:::

- Group 13 first ionisation enthalpy: **B > Tl > Ga > Al > In**. Thallium is second, not last.
- Group 14 first ionisation enthalpy: **Pb (715) > Sn (708)**. Poor shielding by 4f and 5d electrons again.
- Electronegativity in group 13 falls from B to Al, then **rises** slightly to Tl.

:::remember
**Inert pair effect:** going down groups 13 and 14, the $ns^2$ pair is used less in bonding, so the oxidation state **two below** the group valence becomes more stable.
- Tl: **+1** more stable than +3, so $\ce{Tl^3+}$ is an oxidising agent.
- Pb: **+2** more stable than +4, so $\ce{PbO2}$ is a strong oxidising agent.
- Sn: both are common; $\ce{Sn^2+}$ (e.g. $\ce{SnCl2}$) is a **reducing** agent, because it easily goes to +4.
:::

:::trap
Don't mix up tin and lead. $\ce{Sn^2+}$ **reduces** (it wants to be +4). Lead(IV), as in $\ce{PbO2}$, **oxidises** (it wants to be +2).
:::

## Boron and the group 13 halides

- Boron **never forms $\ce{B^3+}$**: the sum of its first three ionisation enthalpies is too high. All its compounds are covalent.
- $\ce{BX3}$ has 6 electrons around B, so it is a **Lewis acid**. $\ce{BF3 + NH3 -> F3B}\leftarrow\ce{NH3}$.
- Boron has no d orbitals, so its maximum covalence is **4**: $\ce{[BF4]-}$ exists, $\ce{[BF6]^3-}$ does not. Al can exceed 4: $\ce{[AlF6]^3-}$ (in cryolite) exists.

:::trap
**Lewis acid strength of boron halides (exam extra):** $\ce{BF3} < \ce{BCl3} < \ce{BBr3} < \ce{BI3}$, the **reverse** of what electronegativity suggests. In $\ce{BF3}$, fluorine gives back some of its lone-pair electron density into boron's empty 2p orbital (pπ–pπ **back bonding**), which works best between the two small atoms B and F. So $\ce{BF3}$ is the weakest.
:::

- (Old NCERT exercise) The B–F bond is **longer** in $\ce{[BF4]-}$ (143 pm) than in $\ce{BF3}$ (130 pm). B changes from sp² (planar) to sp³ (tetrahedral); the extra back-bonding character of B–F in $\ce{BF3}$ is lost (exam extra reason).
- $\ce{AlCl3}$ gives **white fumes** in moist air: moisture partly hydrolyses it and releases HCl gas.
- $\ce{AlCl3}$ exists as the dimer $\ce{Al2Cl6}$ (chlorine bridges). Each Al then has 4 Cl around it, tetrahedrally (exam extra detail).
- In acidified water: $\ce{[Al(H2O)6]^3+}$, octahedral, Al is **sp³d²**.

:::trap
Aluminium is **amphoteric**: it dissolves in HCl **and** in NaOH, giving $\ce{H2}$ both times. But **concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$** makes it **passive**: a protective oxide layer forms.
:::

## Boron compounds

:::trap
**Boric acid is monobasic, not tribasic**, though it has three H atoms. It is a **Lewis acid**: it takes an $\ce{OH-}$ from water and leaves $\ce{H3O+}$. It does not give away its own protons. It is a weak acid.
:::

- Borax solution is **alkaline** (it gives NaOH and the weak boric acid).
- Borax bead = $\ce{NaBO2 + B2O3}$. Cobalt colours it **blue**.

:::trap
In diborane the B–H–B bridges are **three-centre two-electron bonds** (banana bonds), not hydrogen bonds and not ordinary covalent bonds. Diborane has **4** two-centre bonds (terminal) and **2** three-centre bonds (bridges).
:::

:::tip
**Count the electrons in $\ce{B2H6}$:** 2 B × 3 + 6 H × 1 = **12** valence electrons. 4 terminal B–H bonds use 8; the 2 bridges get only 4. That is why the bridges must be 3-centre 2-electron bonds.
:::

- 6 atoms of diborane lie in one plane (2 B + 4 terminal H). The 2 bridging H are above and below it.
- Diborane **catches fire by itself** in air and is hydrolysed by water to boric acid + $\ce{H2}$.
- **Borazine** $\ce{B3N3H6}$ = "**inorganic benzene**" (ring of alternate BH and NH groups).

## Carbon and group 14

:::trap
Among the group 14 tetrachlorides, **only $\ce{CCl4}$ is not hydrolysed** by water. $\ce{SiCl4}$, $\ce{GeCl4}$ and $\ce{SnCl4}$ are. Their central atom has empty d orbitals to accept a lone pair from water; carbon has none and its octet is complete.
:::

- $\ce{BCl3}$ **is** hydrolysed (to boric acid + HCl), because B has an empty p orbital. Compare with $\ce{CCl4}$.
- $\ce{SnF4}$ and $\ce{PbF4}$ are **ionic**; most other $\ce{MX4}$ are covalent.
- $\ce{PbI4}$ **does not exist**: forming Pb–I bonds does not release enough energy to unpair the $6s^2$ electrons and give Pb four unpaired electrons.

:::trap
$\ce{[SiF6]^2-}$ exists but $\ce{[SiCl6]^2-}$ does not (old NCERT problem): six large Cl atoms cannot fit around the small Si atom, and the lone pairs of Cl interact only weakly with Si.
:::

:::trap
**$\ce{CO2}$ is a gas but $\ce{SiO2}$ is a hard solid.** C forms pπ–pπ double bonds with O, so $\ce{CO2}$ is a small linear molecule with weak forces between molecules. Si cannot form pπ–pπ bonds, so each Si forms four single Si–O bonds and the result is a giant 3D network.
:::

- **Diamond** is covalent, yet it melts very high: melting means breaking strong C–C covalent bonds throughout the 3D network.
- Catenation: **C ≫ Si > Ge ≈ Sn**; **Pb shows no catenation**.

## Allotropes

:::trap
The **most stable** allotrope of carbon is **graphite** ($\Delta_f H^\circ = 0$), not diamond (1.90 kJ/mol). And the only **pure** form of carbon is the **fullerene** (no dangling bonds).
:::

- Graphite conducts electricity **along** its layers (delocalised electrons); diamond does not conduct.
- $\ce{C60}$: **12 five-membered + 20 six-membered** rings. A five-membered ring touches only six-membered rings.

:::tip
In $\ce{C60}$ each C forms 3 σ bonds, so the number of C–C links is $60 \times 3 / 2 = 90$ (exam extra counting).
:::

- Carbon black, coke and charcoal are **impure forms of graphite or fullerenes** (NCERT), not separate allotropes.

## CO and CO₂

- $\ce{CO}$ is a **neutral** oxide. $\ce{CO2}$ is **acidic**. $\ce{SnO2}$, $\ce{PbO2}$, $\ce{SnO}$ and $\ce{PbO}$ are **amphoteric**. $\ce{GeO}$ is acidic.
- CO donates its lone pair **from carbon** (not oxygen) to metals, giving metal carbonyls.
- The CO–haemoglobin complex is about **300 times** more stable than the oxygen–haemoglobin complex.
- Water gas = $\ce{CO + H2}$; producer gas = $\ce{CO + N2}$. Easy to swap; remember: **water** (steam) brings the **H₂**.

## Silicon compounds

- Silica resists most reagents (halogens, $\ce{H2}$, most acids and metals), but **HF** and **NaOH** attack it.
- Silicones: $\ce{(CH3)2SiCl2}$ makes the chains; $\ce{(CH3)3SiCl}$ **ends** them.
- Zeolites are aluminosilicates: Al replaces some Si, so cations are needed to balance the charge.

## NCERT lines to know

:::remember
- Aluminium is the most abundant metal; silicon is the second most abundant element in the earth's crust.
- Gallium melts at 303 K but boils at 2676 K.
- Weight for weight, Al conducts electricity twice as well as copper.
- $^{10}\ce{B}$ absorbs neutrons: borides are used in nuclear control rods and shields.
- Diborane burns with $\Delta_c H^\circ = -1976$ kJ/mol.
- The blood buffer $\ce{H2CO3}/\ce{HCO3-}$ keeps blood pH steady (old NCERT gives 7.26–7.42).
:::
