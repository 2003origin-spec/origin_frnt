## Trends at a glance

Group 13 (B, Al, Ga, In, Tl): outer configuration $ns^2np^1$. Group 14 (C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb): $ns^2np^2$.

| Group 13 | $\Delta_i H_1$ (kJ/mol) | Radius (pm) | EN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B | 801 | 85 | 2.0 |
| Al | 577 | 143 | 1.5 |
| Ga | 579 | 135 | 1.6 |
| In | 558 | 167 | 1.7 |
| Tl | 589 | 170 | 1.8 |

| Group 14 | $\Delta_i H_1$ (kJ/mol) | Covalent radius (pm) | EN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C | 1086 | 77 | 2.5 |
| Si | 786 | 118 | 1.8 |
| Ge | 761 | 122 | 1.8 |
| Sn | 708 | 140 | 1.8 |
| Pb | 715 | 146 | 1.9 |

:::formula{title="Atomic radius, group 13"}
$$\text{B} < \text{Ga} < \text{Al} < \text{In} < \text{Tl}$$
Ga (135 pm) is **smaller** than Al (143 pm). Its 10 extra 3d electrons shield the nucleus poorly, so the outer electrons feel a larger nuclear charge.
:::

:::formula{title="First ionisation enthalpy, group 13"}
$$\text{B} > \text{Tl} > \text{Ga} > \text{Al} > \text{In}$$
Not a smooth fall: the poor shielding by d electrons (Ga) and by d and f electrons (Tl) pushes these values up.
:::

:::formula{title="First ionisation enthalpy, group 14"}
$$\text{C} > \text{Si} > \text{Ge} > \text{Pb} > \text{Sn}$$
Small fall from Si to Ge to Sn, then a slight **rise** from Sn to Pb (poor shielding by 4f and 5d electrons).
:::

:::formula{title="Stability of the lower oxidation state (inert pair effect)"}
$$\text{Al} < \text{Ga} < \text{In} < \text{Tl}\ \ (+1)$$
$$\text{Ge} < \text{Sn} < \text{Pb}\ \ (+2)$$
Tl: +1 is more stable than +3. Pb: +2 is more stable than +4.
:::

:::formula{title="Catenation, group 14"}
$$\text{C} \gg \text{Si} > \text{Ge} \approx \text{Sn}$$
Lead does not show catenation. Bond enthalpies (kJ/mol): C–C 348, Si–Si 297, Ge–Ge 260, Sn–Sn 240.
:::

:::formula{title="Standard electrode potentials (old NCERT problem)"}
$$E^\circ(\ce{Al^3+}/\ce{Al}) = -1.66\ \text{V}$$
$$E^\circ(\ce{Tl^3+}/\ce{Tl}) = +1.26\ \text{V}$$
$\ce{Al}$ easily forms $\ce{Al^3+}$ (strongly electropositive). $\ce{Tl^3+}$ is unstable in solution and is a strong **oxidising** agent.
:::

## Aluminium and boron

:::formula{title="Aluminium with acid and with alkali (amphoteric)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2Al + 6HCl} \\ & \ce{-> 2Al^3+ + 6Cl- + 3H2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2Al + 2NaOH + 6H2O} \\ & \ce{-> 2Na[Al(OH)4] + 3H2} \end{aligned}$$
The product is sodium tetrahydroxoaluminate(III). Boron reacts with neither acids nor alkalis at moderate temperatures. **Concentrated** $\ce{HNO3}$ makes Al passive (a protective oxide layer).
:::

:::formula{title="With nitrogen and with halogens (on heating)"}
$$\ce{2E + N2 -> 2EN}$$
$$\ce{2E + 3X2 -> 2EX3}$$
Nitrides: B and Al at high temperature. Trihalides: all group 13 elements, X = F, Cl, Br, I. NCERT's exception: $\ce{TlI3}$.
:::

## Borax and boric acid

:::formula{title="Borax in water (alkaline solution)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Na2B4O7 + 7H2O} \\ & \ce{-> 2NaOH + 4H3BO3} \end{aligned}$$
Borax is $\ce{Na2B4O7*10H2O}$. Its real formula is $\ce{Na2[B4O5(OH)4]*8H2O}$.
:::

:::formula{title="Heating borax (borax bead)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Na2B4O7*10H2O} \\ & \ce{->[\Delta] Na2B4O7} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Na2B4O7} \\ & \ce{->[\Delta] 2NaBO2 + B2O3} \end{aligned}$$
Products: sodium metaborate and boric anhydride, a glassy bead. Transition-metal metaborates are coloured: cobalt gives a **blue** bead.
:::

:::formula{title="Making boric acid from borax"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Na2B4O7 + 2HCl + 5H2O} \\ & \ce{-> 2NaCl + 4B(OH)3} \end{aligned}$$
:::

:::formula{title="Boric acid is a Lewis acid"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{B(OH)3 + 2H2O} \\ & \ce{-> [B(OH)4]- + H3O+} \end{aligned}$$
It takes $\ce{OH-}$ from water; it does not give its own $\ce{H+}$. Weak and **monobasic**.
:::

:::formula{title="Heating boric acid"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{H3BO3} \\ & \ce{->[\Delta] HBO2 ->[\Delta] B2O3} \end{aligned}$$
Above 370 K it gives metaboric acid, then boric oxide.
:::

## Diborane, $\ce{B2H6}$

:::formula{title="Preparation"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{4BF3 + 3LiAlH4} \\ & \ce{-> 2B2H6 + 3LiF + 3AlF3} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2NaBH4 + I2} \\ & \ce{-> B2H6 + 2NaI + H2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2BF3 + 6NaH} \\ & \ce{->[450 K] B2H6 + 6NaF} \end{aligned}$$
First: in diethyl ether. Second: the lab method. Third: industrial.
:::

:::formula{title="Burning and hydrolysis"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{B2H6 + 3O2} \\ & \ce{-> B2O3 + 3H2O} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{B2H6 + 6H2O} \\ & \ce{-> 2B(OH)3 + 6H2} \end{aligned}$$
Combustion: $\Delta_c H^\circ = -1976$ kJ/mol. It catches fire by itself in air.
:::

:::formula{title="With Lewis bases (cleavage)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{B2H6 + 2NMe3} \\ & \ce{-> 2BH3*NMe3} \end{aligned}$$
$$\ce{B2H6 + 2CO -> 2BH3*CO}$$
$$\ce{2MH + B2H6 -> 2M[BH4]}$$
M = Li or Na (in diethyl ether). $\ce{LiBH4}$ and $\ce{NaBH4}$ are reducing agents.
:::

:::formula{title="Borazine, the inorganic benzene"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{3B2H6 + 6NH3} \\ & \ce{-> 3[BH2(NH3)2][BH4]} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{3[BH2(NH3)2][BH4]} \\ & \ce{->[\Delta] 2B3N3H6 + 12H2} \end{aligned}$$
Borazine: a 6-membered ring of alternate BH and NH groups.
:::

## Group 14 reactions

:::formula{title="Tin with steam; tetrachloride hydrolysis"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Sn + 2H2O} \\ & \ce{->[\Delta] SnO2 + 2H2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{SiCl4 + 4H2O} \\ & \ce{-> Si(OH)4 + 4HCl} \end{aligned}$$
C, Si and Ge are not affected by water; Pb is protected by an oxide film. $\ce{CCl4}$ is **not** hydrolysed.
:::

:::formula{title="Making carbon monoxide"}
$$\ce{2C + O2 ->[\Delta] 2CO}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{HCOOH} \\ & \ce{->[H2SO4][373 K] CO + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
First: limited air. Second: pure CO in the lab, dehydrating formic acid with **concentrated** $\ce{H2SO4}$ at 373 K.
:::

:::formula{title="Water gas and producer gas"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C + H2O} \\ & \ce{->[473-1273 K] CO + H2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2C + O2 + 4N2} \\ & \ce{->[1273 K] 2CO + 4N2} \end{aligned}$$
$\ce{CO + H2}$ = water gas (synthesis gas). $\ce{CO + N2}$ = producer gas. Both are industrial fuels.
:::

:::formula{title="CO as a reducing agent"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Fe2O3 + 3CO} \\ & \ce{->[\Delta] 2Fe + 3CO2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{ZnO + CO} \\ & \ce{->[\Delta] Zn + CO2} \end{aligned}$$
It does not reduce oxides of alkali and alkaline earth metals, Al and a few transition metals.
:::

:::formula{title="Carbon dioxide"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CaCO3 + 2HCl} \\ & \ce{-> CaCl2 + CO2 + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{H2CO3 + H2O} \\ & \ce{<=> HCO3- + H3O+} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{HCO3- + H2O} \\ & \ce{<=> CO3^2- + H3O+} \end{aligned}$$
Lab method, then the two steps of the weak dibasic carbonic acid. The $\ce{H2CO3}/\ce{HCO3-}$ buffer keeps blood pH steady (old NCERT: 7.26–7.42; the usual normal range is 7.35–7.45).
:::

:::formula{title="Silica: unreactive, but attacked by NaOH and HF"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{SiO2 + 2NaOH} \\ & \ce{-> Na2SiO3 + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{SiO2 + 4HF} \\ & \ce{-> SiF4 + 2H2O} \end{aligned}$$
:::

:::formula{title="Silicones"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2CH3Cl + Si} \\ & \ce{->[Cu][573 K] (CH3)2SiCl2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{(CH3)2SiCl2 + 2H2O} \\ & \ce{-> (CH3)2Si(OH)2 + 2HCl} \end{aligned}$$
$\ce{(CH3)2Si(OH)2}$ then condenses (loses water) into chains with the repeating unit $-(\ce{R2SiO})-$. $\ce{(CH3)3SiCl}$ caps the chain ends.
:::
