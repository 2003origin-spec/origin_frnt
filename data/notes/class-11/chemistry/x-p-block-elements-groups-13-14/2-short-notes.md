:::tip
The JEE Main 2026 and NEET 2026 syllabi describe the p-block unit (groups 13–18) only as **general trends** in physical and chemical properties and the **unique behaviour of the first element** (here B and C). They name no compounds. Learn the trends first, then the compounds from the older chapter.
:::

## 1. The two groups

- Group 13 (boron family): B, Al, Ga, In, Tl, outer configuration $ns^2np^1$.
- Group 14 (carbon family): C, Si, Ge, Sn, Pb, outer configuration $ns^2np^2$.
- The **first** element (B, C) behaves differently: see sections 4 and 11.
- Aluminium is the most abundant **metal** in the earth's crust (8.3%); silicon is the second most abundant **element** (27.7%), after oxygen.

## 2. Group 13: physical trends

- **Radius** grows down the group, but **Ga (135 pm) < Al (143 pm)**: Ga's 3d electrons shield the nucleus poorly.
- **Ionisation enthalpy** does not fall smoothly: B > Tl > Ga > Al > In. The sum of the first three is very high.
- **Electronegativity** falls from B (2.0) to Al (1.5), then rises slightly to Tl (1.8).
- **Boron** is a hard, black non-metal with a very high melting point. The rest are soft metals with low melting points and good conductivity.
- **Gallium** melts at 303 K but boils at 2676 K, so it is used to measure high temperatures.

## 3. Group 13: chemical properties

**Oxidation states.** B and Al show +3; Ga, In and Tl show +1 and +3. Down the group the $ns^2$ pair takes less part in bonding (**inert pair effect**), so +1 grows more stable: Al < Ga < In < Tl. For thallium, +1 is more stable than +3, so $\ce{Tl^3+}$ is a strong oxidising agent.

**Lewis acids.** $\ce{BX3}$ has only 6 electrons on B (**electron deficient**), so it accepts a lone pair: $\ce{F3B}\leftarrow\ce{NH3}$. This tendency falls down the group as size grows. $\ce{AlCl3}$ completes its octet by **dimerising** to $\ce{Al2Cl6}$ (chlorine bridges); boron trihalides stay monomers.

**In water.** The trihalides are hydrolysed. In water they give tetrahedral $\ce{[M(OH)4]-}$ (sp³); in acidified water Al gives octahedral $\ce{[Al(H2O)6]^3+}$, using its 3d orbitals (**sp³d²**).

**Reactions**
- **Air:** crystalline B is unreactive; Al is protected by a thin oxide layer. Heated, they give $\ce{B2O3}$, $\ce{Al2O3}$, and nitrides with $\ce{N2}$.
- **Acids and alkalis:** B reacts with neither. Al dissolves in both (**amphoteric**), giving $\ce{H2}$. Concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ makes Al **passive**.
- **Halogens:** all give $\ce{EX3}$; NCERT lists $\ce{TlI3}$ as the exception. (Exam extra: the solid $\ce{TlI3}$ is really $\ce{Tl+[I3]-}$.)
- **Oxides:** $\ce{B2O3}$ acidic; $\ce{Al2O3}$, $\ce{Ga2O3}$ amphoteric; $\ce{In2O3}$, $\ce{Tl2O3}$ basic.

## 4. Why boron is different

| Boron | Aluminium and below |
| --- | --- |
| Non-metal, very hard | Metals |
| No $\ce{B^3+}$; only covalent compounds | Al forms $\ce{Al^3+}$ in water |
| Acidic oxide | $\ce{Al2O3}$ amphoteric |
| Max covalence **4** | Can exceed 4 ($\ce{[AlF6]^3-}$) |

B never forms $\ce{B^3+}$ because the sum of its first three ionisation enthalpies is too high. With no d orbitals, $\ce{[BF4]-}$ exists but $\ce{[BF6]^3-}$ does not.

## 5. Borax, $\ce{Na2B4O7*10H2O}$

- It really contains $\ce{[B4O5(OH)4]^2-}$ units: $\ce{Na2[B4O5(OH)4]*8H2O}$.
- Its water solution is **alkaline** (NaOH + weak boric acid).
- Heated, it swells as it loses water, then melts to a glassy **borax bead** ($\ce{NaBO2 + B2O3}$).
- **Bead test:** transition-metal metaborates are coloured. Cobalt gives a **blue** bead.

## 6. Orthoboric acid, $\ce{B(OH)3}$

- White, soapy crystals. Sparingly soluble in cold water, very soluble in hot water.
- Made by acidifying borax solution, or by hydrolysing most boron compounds.
- **Layer structure:** planar $\ce{BO3}$ units joined by hydrogen bonds.
- **Weak monobasic Lewis acid:** it takes $\ce{OH-}$ from water, which leaves $\ce{H3O+}$. It does not give up its own protons.
- Above 370 K it gives $\ce{HBO2}$ (metaboric acid), then $\ce{B2O3}$.

## 7. Diborane, $\ce{B2H6}$

- Colourless, **highly toxic** gas (boiling point 180 K). Catches fire by itself in air and burns with great heat.
- Water hydrolyses it to boric acid and $\ce{H2}$.
- Lewis bases split it into adducts $\ce{BH3*L}$ (L = $\ce{NMe3}$, CO).
- With $\ce{NH3}$ it gives $\ce{[BH2(NH3)2]+[BH4]-}$, which on heating gives **borazine** $\ce{B3N3H6}$, "**inorganic benzene**" (ring of alternate BH and NH).
- With LiH or NaH it gives $\ce{Li[BH4]}$ or $\ce{Na[BH4]}$ (tetrahedral $\ce{[BH4]-}$), reducing agents in organic synthesis.

**Structure**
- The 2 B and the 4 **terminal** H lie in one plane; 2 **bridging** H lie above and below it.
- The 4 terminal B–H bonds are normal **two-centre two-electron** bonds.
- The 2 B–H–B bridges are **three-centre two-electron** ("banana") bonds, and are longer.
- Each B uses **sp³** hybrid orbitals.

## 8. Uses of boron and aluminium

- **Boron** fibres: bullet-proof vests, light aircraft composites. $^{10}\ce{B}$ absorbs neutrons, so borides are used in nuclear shields and control rods.
- **Borax, boric acid:** heat-resistant (Pyrex) glass, glass wool, fibreglass; borax is a soldering flux; boric acid is a mild antiseptic.
- **Aluminium:** weight for weight it conducts electricity **twice as well as copper**. Wires, packing, utensils, aircraft, alloys. Domestic use has fallen because of its toxicity.

## 9. Group 14: trends

- Tin occurs as cassiterite ($\ce{SnO2}$), lead as galena ($\ce{PbS}$). Ultrapure Si and Ge are semiconductors. $^{14}\ce{C}$ is used in radiocarbon dating.
- **Radius:** a big jump from C (77 pm) to Si (118 pm), then small steps to Pb (146 pm), because of filled d and f orbitals.
- **Ionisation enthalpy** is higher than in group 13 and falls down the group, except that Pb is slightly **above** Sn.
- **Electronegativity** is slightly higher than in group 13; almost the same from Si to Pb.
- C and Si are non-metals, Ge a metalloid, Sn and Pb soft metals. Melting points are much higher than in group 13.

## 10. Group 14: chemical properties

**Oxidation states.** +4 and +2 (carbon also shows negative states). Down the group +2 becomes more stable (inert pair effect). Ge: mainly +4. Sn: both, and $\ce{Sn^2+}$ is a **reducing** agent. Pb: +2 is stable, and Pb(IV) compounds are strong **oxidising** agents.

**Covalence.** $\ce{CCl4}$ is **electron precise** (8 electrons on C): neither acceptor nor donor. Si to Pb have d orbitals, so they exceed covalence 4: $\ce{[SiF6]^2-}$, $\ce{[GeCl6]^2-}$, $\ce{[Sn(OH)6]^2-}$ (sp³d²).

**Reactions**
- **Oxygen:** oxides $\ce{MO}$ and $\ce{MO2}$ (SiO only at high temperature). The higher oxide is generally the more acidic.
- **Water:** C, Si, Ge are unaffected; Sn decomposes steam to $\ce{SnO2}$ and $\ce{H2}$; Pb is protected by an oxide film.
- **Halogens:** all except carbon react directly, giving $\ce{MX4}$ and $\ce{MX2}$. Most $\ce{MX4}$ are covalent, but $\ce{SnF4}$ and $\ce{PbF4}$ are ionic. $\ce{PbI4}$ does not exist.
- Dihalides grow more stable down the group: $\ce{GeX4}$ beats $\ce{GeX2}$, but $\ce{PbX2}$ beats $\ce{PbX4}$.
- All tetrachlorides **except $\ce{CCl4}$** are hydrolysed: the central atom accepts a lone pair from water into a d orbital.

## 11. Why carbon is different

- Small size, high electronegativity and ionisation enthalpy, **no d orbitals** (max covalence 4).
- Forms **pπ–pπ multiple bonds** with itself and small electronegative atoms: C=C, C≡C, C=O, C=S, C≡N. Heavier atoms' orbitals are too large and diffuse to overlap sideways well.
- **Catenation** (chains and rings of one element) is strongest in carbon because C–C is very strong (348 kJ/mol). C ≫ Si > Ge ≈ Sn; **Pb shows none**.
- Catenation and multiple bonds give carbon its **allotropes**.

## 12. Allotropes of carbon

| Property | Diamond | Graphite | $\ce{C60}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C is | sp³ | sp² | sp² |
| Structure | 3D network | Layers, 340 pm apart | Cage |
| C–C | 154 pm | 141.5 pm | 143.5, 138.3 pm |

- **Diamond:** each C bonded tetrahedrally to 4 others; the hardest substance. Used as an abrasive and in dies.
- **Graphite:** hexagonal layers held by weak van der Waals forces. The fourth electron of each C is delocalised over the layer, so graphite **conducts** along it. Layers slide, so it is soft: a dry lubricant for hot machines.
- **Fullerenes:** made by heating graphite in an electric arc in He or Ar (mainly $\ce{C60}$, some $\ce{C70}$).
  - $\ce{C60}$ (buckminsterfullerene) is soccer-ball shaped: 60 C atoms, **20 six-membered and 12 five-membered rings**. A five-membered ring is fused only to six-membered rings.
  - Each C is sp² with 3 σ bonds; the other electrons are delocalised (**aromatic** character).
  - Fullerenes are the **only pure form** of carbon (no dangling bonds).
- **Graphite is the most stable** allotrope: $\Delta_f H^\circ = 0$ (diamond 1.90, $\ce{C60}$ 38.1 kJ/mol).
- Carbon black, coke and charcoal are **impure** forms. Activated charcoal adsorbs poisonous gases; coke is a fuel and reducing agent; carbon black is a tyre filler and ink pigment.

## 13. Carbon monoxide

- Made in limited air, from formic acid, or as water gas / producer gas (reactions on the formula sheet).
- Colourless, odourless, almost insoluble in water.
- A **strong reducing agent**: extracts metals from oxides such as $\ce{Fe2O3}$ and $\ce{ZnO}$.
- $\ce{:C#O:}$: one σ and two π bonds. The lone pair on **carbon** bonds to metals (metal carbonyls).
- **Poisonous:** its haemoglobin complex is about **300 times** more stable than oxyhaemoglobin, so blood stops carrying oxygen.

## 14. Carbon dioxide

- From burning carbon or fuels in excess air; in the lab from $\ce{CaCO3}$ + dilute HCl.
- Slightly soluble in water, giving the weak **dibasic** carbonic acid. The $\ce{H2CO3}/\ce{HCO3-}$ buffer keeps blood pH steady (old NCERT: 7.26–7.42; usual normal range 7.35–7.45).
- Used in photosynthesis; rising levels add to the **greenhouse effect**.
- **Dry ice** (solid $\ce{CO2}$) is a refrigerant. $\ce{CO2}$ also carbonates drinks, puts out fires and is used to make urea.
- **Structure:** C is **sp**, with two σ and two pπ–pπ bonds. **Linear**, both C–O = 115 pm, **zero** dipole moment.

## 15. Silica, $\ce{SiO2}$

- About 95% of the earth's crust is silica and silicates. Crystalline forms: quartz, cristobalite, tridymite.
- **3D covalent network:** each Si bonded tetrahedrally to 4 O, each O to 2 Si; 8-membered rings of alternate Si and O.
- Very unreactive (strong Si–O bonds): resists halogens, $\ce{H2}$, most acids and metals. But **HF** and **NaOH** attack it.
- Quartz is **piezoelectric** (accurate clocks, radio, TV). Silica gel is a drying agent; kieselguhr is used in filters.

## 16. Silicones

- Polymers with repeating unit $-(\ce{R2SiO})-$, R = alkyl or aryl.
- From $\ce{(CH3)2SiCl2}$: hydrolysis, then condensation, gives **straight chains**; $\ce{(CH3)3SiCl}$ caps the ends and controls chain length.
- **Water-repelling** (outer alkyl groups), thermally stable, good insulators, resist oxidation and chemicals.
- Uses: sealants, greases, insulators, waterproof fabrics; biocompatible, so used in surgical and cosmetic implants.

## 17. Silicates and zeolites

- Basic unit: the tetrahedral $\ce{SiO4^4-}$ ion. Units share **1, 2, 3 or 4** corner O atoms to form chains, rings, sheets or 3D networks; metal ions balance the charge.
- Examples: feldspar, zeolites, mica, asbestos. Man-made: **glass** and **cement**.
- **Zeolites:** Al replaces some Si in the 3D network, making a negatively charged **aluminosilicate**; $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$ or $\ce{Ca^2+}$ balance it. **ZSM-5** turns alcohols into gasoline; hydrated zeolites **soften hard water** (ion exchange).
