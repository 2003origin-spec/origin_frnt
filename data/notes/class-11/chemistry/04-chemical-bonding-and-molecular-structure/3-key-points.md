## Shapes and bond angles

:::trap
**Hybridisation is not the shape.** $\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ are both sp³, but $\ce{NH3}$ is pyramidal and $\ce{H2O}$ is bent. The shape describes where the **atoms** are. Lone pairs bend it, but they are not part of it.
:::

- With the **same number of electron pairs** (four here, all sp³), more lone pairs means a smaller angle: $\ce{CH4}$ (109.5°) > $\ce{NH3}$ (107°) > $\ce{H2O}$ (104.5°). Don't stretch this: $\ce{XeF2}$ has 3 lone pairs and is linear (180°).
- (Exam extra) Going down a group the angle falls: $\ce{NH3}$ > $\ce{PH3}$, and $\ce{H2O}$ > $\ce{H2S}$. The bigger, less electronegative central atom holds its bond pairs farther away, so they repel less.
- (Exam extra) $\ce{NH3}$ (107°) has a **bigger** angle than $\ce{NF3}$ (about 102°). In $\ce{NF3}$ fluorine pulls the bond pairs away from N, so they repel less.

:::remember
**Lone pairs go to the equatorial positions** of a trigonal bipyramid, where they have more room:
- $\ce{SF4}$: 1 lone pair, **see-saw**;
- $\ce{ClF3}$: 2 lone pairs, **T-shaped**;
- $\ce{XeF2}$ and $\ce{I3-}$: 3 lone pairs, **linear**.
:::

:::trap
$\ce{SF4}$ is **not** tetrahedral and $\ce{XeF4}$ is **not** tetrahedral. Count all the electron pairs: $\ce{SF4}$ has 5 (4 bond pairs + 1 lone pair), so it is see-saw; $\ce{XeF4}$ has 6 (4 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs), so it is square planar.
:::

## Dipole moments

- A molecule with polar bonds can still have $\mu = 0$ if the bond dipoles cancel: $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{CCl4}$, $\ce{BeF2}$, $\ce{PCl5}$, $\ce{SF6}$, $\ce{XeF4}$.
- $\ce{CO2}$ is linear ($\mu = 0$) but $\ce{SO2}$ is bent ($\mu \ne 0$), because S has a lone pair.

:::trap
$\ce{NH3}$ (1.47 D) has a **much bigger** dipole moment than $\ce{NF3}$ (0.23 D), even though N–F bonds are more polar. In $\ce{NH3}$ the lone pair's moment points the **same way** as the **resultant** of the three N–H bond moments, so they add. In $\ce{NF3}$ it points **against** the resultant of the three N–F bond moments, so they largely cancel.
:::

- Order to remember (exam extra, measured values): $\ce{CH3Cl}$ (about 1.9 D) > $\ce{CH2Cl2}$ (about 1.6 D) > $\ce{CHCl3}$ (about 1.0 D) > $\ce{CCl4}$ (0). Simple vector addition would put $\ce{CH2Cl2}$ first, so learn this order as data.

## Counting σ and π bonds

- In VBT: single bond 1 σ; double bond 1 σ + 1 π; triple bond 1 σ + 2 π.
- Ethyne ($\ce{C2H2}$): 3 σ, 2 π. Ethene ($\ce{C2H4}$): 5 σ, 1 π. Benzene: 12 σ, 3 π.

:::tip
Count every **pair of bonded atoms** as one σ (a double or triple bond still has only one σ). Then add one π for each double bond and two π for each triple bond.
:::

:::trap
In VBT a π bond forms only on top of a σ bond. Hybrid orbitals form σ bonds or hold lone pairs, never π bonds; π bonds come from the leftover, unhybridised p orbitals.

**Exception from MOT (NCERT):** the double bond in $\ce{C2}$ is **two π bonds and no σ bond**. Its 4 bonding electrons are in the two $\pi 2p$ orbitals.
:::

## Molecular orbitals

:::remember
- Bond order **0** means the molecule does not exist: $\ce{He2}$, $\ce{Be2}$, $\ce{Ne2}$.
- $\ce{O2}$ is **paramagnetic** (2 unpaired electrons). Only MOT explains this.
- $\ce{B2}$ is paramagnetic, $\ce{C2}$ is diamagnetic. This happens because $\pi 2p$ is below $\sigma 2p_z$ up to $\ce{N2}$.
:::

- **Stability:** $\ce{O2+}$ > $\ce{O2}$ > $\ce{O2-}$ > $\ce{O2^2-}$ (bond orders 2.5, 2, 1.5, 1). Bond length goes the other way.
- (Exam extra) $\ce{N2+}$ and $\ce{N2-}$ have the same bond order (2.5). $\ce{N2+}$ is **more stable**, because $\ce{N2-}$ has more electrons in antibonding orbitals.
- Taking an electron from $\ce{N2}$ (to make $\ce{N2+}$) **lowers** the bond order, because the electron comes out of a bonding orbital ($\sigma 2p_z$). Taking one from $\ce{O2}$ (to make $\ce{O2+}$) **raises** it, because the electron comes out of an antibonding orbital ($\pi^* 2p$).

:::tip
**Magic 14:** every **diatomic** molecule or ion with **14 electrons in total** ($\ce{N2}$, $\ce{CO}$, $\ce{NO+}$, $\ce{CN-}$, $\ce{C2^2-}$) has bond order 3. Count all the electrons, not just the valence ones: $\ce{F2}$ has 14 valence electrons but 18 in all, and bond order 1.
:::

## Octet rule

:::trap
Don't assume every atom ends up with 8:
- $\ce{BeCl2}$ (4 electrons on Be) and $\ce{BF3}$ (6 on B) have **fewer** than 8;
- $\ce{NO}$ and $\ce{NO2}$ have an **odd** number of electrons;
- $\ce{PCl5}$ (10) and $\ce{SF6}$ (12) have **more** than 8.
:::

- Second-period elements (B, C, N, O, F) can **never** have more than 8: they have no d orbitals. So $\ce{NCl5}$ doesn't exist, but $\ce{PCl5}$ does.

## Ionic or covalent?

- Fajans' rules: **small** cation, **large** anion and **high** charge give more covalent character.
- $\ce{LiCl}$ > $\ce{NaCl}$ > $\ce{KCl}$ in covalent character. Among lithium halides, $\ce{LiI}$ is the most covalent.
- Lattice enthalpy grows with charge and falls with size: $\ce{MgO}$ is far higher than $\ce{NaCl}$.

## Resonance

:::trap
Resonance structures are **not** real molecules flipping back and forth. The real molecule is one hybrid, all the time. In $\ce{CO3^2-}$ all three C–O bonds are identical.
:::

## Hydrogen bonding

- Needs H bonded to **F, O or N**. Cl is too big, so HCl hardly forms any.
- (Exam extra) **o-Nitrophenol** has an intramolecular hydrogen bond, so it is more volatile and boils lower than **p-nitrophenol**, whose hydrogen bonds link neighbouring molecules.
- Water boils far higher than $\ce{H2S}$ because of hydrogen bonding.

## NCERT lines to know

:::remember
- The **lattice enthalpy** of NaCl is 788 kJ/mol.
- The bond order of $\ce{O2}$ is 2, and $\ce{O2}$ is paramagnetic.
- In $\ce{PCl5}$ the axial bonds are slightly longer than the equatorial bonds.
- Formal charges do not show real charge separation in a molecule.
:::
