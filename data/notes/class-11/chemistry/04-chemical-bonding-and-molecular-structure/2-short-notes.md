## 1. Why atoms bond: the octet rule

- **Kössel–Lewis idea:** atoms bond to get 8 electrons in their outer shell (2 for H), like a noble gas.
- They do it by **transferring** electrons (ionic bond) or **sharing** them (covalent bond).
- **Lewis symbol:** the element's symbol with its valence electrons as dots. Na has 1 dot, Cl has 7.
- The usual valence of an element is the number of its valence electrons, or 8 minus that number.

## 2. Covalent bonds and Lewis structures

- Sharing one pair gives a **single** bond, two pairs a **double** bond, three pairs a **triple** bond.
- **Writing a Lewis structure:**
  1. add up the valence electrons (add one per negative charge, remove one per positive charge);
  2. put the least electronegative atom in the centre (H is never central);
  3. join the atoms with single bonds, complete the octets of the **outer** atoms with lone pairs, then put any electrons left over on the central atom;
  4. if the central atom is still short of 8, turn lone pairs on the outer atoms into double or triple bonds.
- **Formal charge** ($V - L - \tfrac12 S$) helps choose between structures: the lowest formal charges win. It is bookkeeping, not the real charge on the atom.

## 3. Where the octet rule fails

| Problem | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Fewer than 8 electrons on the central atom | $\ce{LiCl}$, $\ce{BeH2}$, $\ce{BCl3}$ |
| Odd number of electrons | $\ce{NO}$, $\ce{NO2}$ |
| More than 8 (expanded octet, needs d orbitals: period 3 onwards) | $\ce{PF5}$, $\ce{SF6}$, $\ce{H2SO4}$ |

The octet rule also can't explain the **shape** of a molecule, its **energy**, or why noble gases form compounds such as $\ce{XeF2}$ and $\ce{XeF4}$.

## 4. Ionic bonds

- Form easily between an atom with **low ionisation enthalpy** (a metal) and one with a **large negative electron gain enthalpy** (a non-metal).
- What really makes an ionic solid stable is its **lattice enthalpy**: the energy needed to pull one mole of the crystal apart into gaseous ions. The same energy is released when the ions pack together.
- Small, highly charged ions give a large lattice enthalpy.

## 5. Bond parameters

- **Bond length:** distance between the two nuclei. Single > double > triple (for the same two atoms).
- **Bond angle:** angle between two bonds on the same atom. It shows the shape.
- **Bond enthalpy:** energy needed to break one mole of a bond in the gas phase. Triple > double > single (for the same two atoms).
- **Bond order:** number of bonds between two atoms. Higher bond order means a shorter, stronger bond.

:::remember
**Isoelectronic diatomic species (the same total number of electrons) have the same bond order.** $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{CO}$, $\ce{NO+}$ and $\ce{CN-}$ (14 electrons) all have bond order 3. $\ce{F2}$ and $\ce{O2^2-}$ (18 electrons) both have bond order 1.
:::

## 6. Resonance

- When one Lewis structure can't describe a molecule, it is shown as a **resonance hybrid** of several structures. The structures differ only in where the electrons are; the atoms stay in the same positions.
- Example: in $\ce{O3}$ both O–O bonds are 128 pm, between a single bond (148 pm) and a double bond (121 pm).
- $\ce{CO3^2-}$ has 3 equal C–O bonds, each with bond order $\tfrac43$.
- The separate structures **do not exist**. The molecule is the hybrid all the time, and it is more stable than any one structure.

## 7. Polar bonds and dipole moment

- When the two atoms differ in electronegativity, the shared pair shifts towards the more electronegative atom, making a **polar** bond.
- Dipole moment $\mu = q \times d$, in debye. For a molecule, add the bond dipoles as **vectors**.
- If the central atom has **no lone pair** and identical outer atoms arranged symmetrically, the bond dipoles cancel and $\mu = 0$ even though the bonds are polar: $\ce{CO2}$ (linear), $\ce{BF3}$ (trigonal planar), $\ce{CCl4}$ (tetrahedral). Lone pairs usually spoil this ($\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{H2O}$); $\ce{XeF2}$ and $\ce{XeF4}$ are exceptions, because their lone pairs are arranged symmetrically too.
- $\ce{H2O}$ is bent, so it has a dipole moment (1.85 D).

## 8. Covalent character of ionic bonds (Fajans' rules)

A cation pulls the electron cloud of the anion towards itself (**polarisation**), which gives the ionic bond some covalent character. It is more covalent when:
- the **cation is small** ($\ce{LiCl}$ is more covalent than $\ce{NaCl}$);
- the **anion is large** ($\ce{LiI}$ is more covalent than $\ce{LiF}$);
- the **charge is high** ($\ce{AlCl3}$ is more covalent than $\ce{NaCl}$);
- for cations of the **same size and charge**, one with an $(n-1)d^n ns^0$ configuration (typical of transition metals) polarises more than one with a noble-gas configuration ($\ce{CuCl}$ is more covalent than $\ce{NaCl}$).

## 9. VSEPR theory (shapes)

- Electron pairs around the central atom repel each other and spread out as far apart as possible.
- A double or triple bond counts as **one** group.
- Repulsion: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair.
- So lone pairs squeeze the bond angles. With 4 electron pairs each: $\ce{CH4}$ 109.5° > $\ce{NH3}$ 107° > $\ce{H2O}$ 104.5°.

## 10. Valence bond theory

- A covalent bond forms when half-filled orbitals of two atoms **overlap**. More overlap means a stronger bond.
- $\ce{H2}$: the energy is lowest at 74 pm, and 435.8 kJ/mol is released per mole of bonds.
- **σ bond:** head-on overlap along the axis (s–s, s–p, p–p). Strong, and allows free rotation.
- **π bond:** sideways overlap of parallel p orbitals. Weaker; in VBT it forms only on top of a σ bond ($\ce{C2}$ in MOT is the exception), and it stops rotation.

## 11. Hybridisation

Orbitals of nearly equal energy on the central atom mix to give the same number of identical **hybrid orbitals**. Hybrid orbitals form σ bonds or hold lone pairs (as in $\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{H2O}$); they never form π bonds.

| Hybridisation | Arrangement of hybrid orbitals | Angle | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| sp | Linear | 180° | $\ce{BeCl2}$, $\ce{C2H2}$ |
| sp² | Trigonal planar | 120° | $\ce{BCl3}$, $\ce{C2H4}$ |
| sp³ | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{C2H6}$ |
| sp³d | Trigonal bipyramidal | 120° and 90° | $\ce{PCl5}$ |
| sp³d² | Octahedral | 90° | $\ce{SF6}$ |

- **Ethene:** each C is sp². The C=C is one σ bond (sp²–sp²) and one π bond (unhybridised p orbitals).
- **Ethyne:** each C is sp. The C≡C is one σ bond and two π bonds.
- **PCl₅:** the 2 axial bonds are slightly longer, and so slightly weaker, than the 3 equatorial ones, because they feel more repulsion. That is why $\ce{PCl5}$ is reactive.

## 12. Molecular orbital theory

- Atomic orbitals of similar energy and the same symmetry, which overlap well, combine (**LCAO**) to give molecular orbitals that belong to the whole molecule.
- Two atomic orbitals give one **bonding** MO (lower energy, electrons between the nuclei) and one **antibonding** MO (higher energy).
- Electrons fill MOs by the aufbau rule, Pauli's principle and Hund's rule.
- Bond order $= \tfrac12(N_b - N_a)$. Unpaired electrons make the molecule paramagnetic.

:::remember
MOT explains why $\ce{O2}$ is **paramagnetic**: it has two unpaired electrons in its $\pi^*$ orbitals. The Lewis structure and VBT can't explain this.
:::

## 13. Hydrogen bonding

- A hydrogen atom bonded to a very electronegative atom (**F, O or N**) is attracted to another F, O or N atom: usually in a neighbouring molecule (intermolecular), sometimes in the same molecule (intramolecular).
- It is much weaker than a covalent bond, but strong enough to raise boiling points ($\ce{HF}$, $\ce{H2O}$).
- **Intermolecular:** between different molecules (water, HF). The molecules hold together, so the boiling point goes up.
- **Intramolecular:** inside the same molecule, as in o-nitrophenol. It can't link to neighbours, so its boiling point is lower than p-nitrophenol's (exam extra).
