## Early classification

:::formula{title="Dobereiner's law of triads"}
$$A_{\text{middle}} \approx \frac{A_1 + A_3}{2}$$
$A$ = atomic weight of the three elements of a triad, in order.

Example: Li (7), Na (23), K (39): $(7 + 39)/2 = 23$. It worked for only a few triads, so it was dismissed as coincidence.
:::

:::formula{title="Moseley's observation (1913)"}
$$\sqrt{\nu} \propto Z$$
$\nu$ = frequency of the characteristic X-rays of the element, $Z$ = atomic number.

A plot of $\sqrt{\nu}$ against $Z$ is a straight line; against atomic mass it is not. So atomic number is the more basic property. (Exam extra form: $\sqrt{\nu} = a(Z - b)$, with $a$ and $b$ constants.)
:::

## Structure of the long form

:::formula{title="Number of elements in a period"}
$$N = 2 \times (\text{orbitals filled in that period})$$
Period 5 fills 5s, 4d and 5p: $1 + 5 + 3 = 9$ orbitals, so $N = 18$.

Period 6 fills 6s, 4f, 5d and 6p: $1 + 7 + 5 + 3 = 16$ orbitals, so $N = 32$.

Periods 1 to 6 hold 2, 8, 8, 18, 18 and 32 elements. Period 7 (7s, 5f, 6d, 7p) can also hold 32. NCERT calls period 7 "incomplete" with a theoretical maximum of 32, ending at element 118. Today all elements up to 118 (Og) are named, so period 7 is complete with 32 elements.
:::

:::formula{title="Locating an element from its configuration"}
Period = principal quantum number $n$ of the outermost shell.

Block = the subshell that receives the **last** electron (s, p, d or f).

Group (IUPAC 1–18):
- s-block: group = number of $ns$ electrons;
- p-block: group = $10 + (ns + np$ electrons$)$;
- d-block: group = $(n-1)d$ electrons $+$ $ns$ electrons;
- f-block: shown in two separate rows at the bottom (lanthanoids and actinoids).
:::

:::example{title="Using the rules"}
$3s^2 3p^4$: period 3, p-block, group $10 + 6 = 16$ (sulphur).
$3d^2 4s^2$: period 4, d-block, group $2 + 2 = 4$ (titanium).
:::

## Atomic size

:::formula{title="Covalent and metallic radius"}
$$r = \frac{d}{2}$$
Covalent radius: $d$ = A–A single-bond length in a molecule. $\ce{Cl2}$: $198/2 = 99$ pm.

Metallic radius: $d$ = distance between neighbouring atoms in the metal crystal. Cu: $256/2 = 128$ pm.
:::

## Ionization enthalpy

:::formula{title="First and second ionization enthalpy"}
$$\ce{X(g) -> X+(g) + e-}\quad \Delta_i H_1$$
$$\ce{X+(g) -> X^2+(g) + e-}\quad \Delta_i H_2$$
Unit: kJ mol⁻¹. Always positive. The atom must be **isolated, gaseous and in its ground state**.
$$\Delta_i H_1 < \Delta_i H_2 < \Delta_i H_3$$
A very large jump means the next electron comes from a noble-gas core.
:::

:::formula{title="Per atom to per mole"}
$$\Delta_i H = E_{\text{atom}} \times N_A$$
$E_{\text{atom}}$ = energy to remove the electron from one atom (J), $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$ mol⁻¹.

Hydrogen: $2.18 \times 10^{-18}$ J $\times\, 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 1.313 \times 10^{6}$ J mol⁻¹ (1312 kJ mol⁻¹).

(Exam extra) 1 eV per atom = 96.49 kJ mol⁻¹.
:::

## Electron gain enthalpy

:::formula{title="Electron gain enthalpy"}
$$\ce{X(g) + e- -> X-(g)}\quad \Delta_{eg} H$$
Negative when energy is released (halogens), positive when energy must be supplied (noble gases).
:::

:::formula{title="Link to electron affinity (NCERT footnote)"}
$$\Delta_{eg}H = -A_e - \tfrac52 RT$$
$A_e$ = electron affinity, defined at absolute zero and taken as **positive** when energy is released. $R = 8.314$ J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. At 298 K, $\tfrac52 RT \approx 6.2$ kJ mol⁻¹.

So $\Delta_{eg}H$ and $A_e$ have **opposite signs**. Read which one a question uses.
:::

## Electronegativity

:::formula{title="Mulliken scale (exam extra)"}
$$\chi_M = \frac{IE + EA}{2}$$
$IE$ = ionization energy, $EA$ = electron affinity (positive when energy is released), both in eV per atom. NCERT only names the Mulliken–Jaffe scale; this formula is not in NCERT. With eV values, the usual conversion is Pauling value $\approx \chi_M / 2.8$. Books use slightly different conversions, so use the one the question gives.

NCERT uses the **Pauling scale**, with F = 4.0 (the highest). Electronegativity has no unit and is not directly measurable.
:::

## Valence

:::formula{title="Valence of a main-group element"}
$$\text{Valence} = v \ \text{or}\ 8 - v$$
$v$ = number of valence electrons. This is usually, but not always, true (NCERT). Groups 15, 16, 17 show 3 or 5, 2 or 6, 1 or 7.

Formula of a compound: cross the valences. Si (4) + Br (1) gives $\ce{SiBr4}$; Al (3) + S (2) gives $\ce{Al2S3}$.
:::
