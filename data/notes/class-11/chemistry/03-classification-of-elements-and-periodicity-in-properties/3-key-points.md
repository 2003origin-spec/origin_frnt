## The big picture

:::tip
**Across a period (left to right):** size goes **down**; ionization enthalpy, electronegativity, negative electron gain enthalpy and non-metallic character go **up**.

**Down a group:** the opposite. Size goes **up**; the rest go **down**, and metallic character goes **up**.

Learn this one pattern, then learn the exceptions below.
:::

## Atomic size

- The largest atom in a period is the **group 1** metal; the smallest is the **halogen** (noble gases are left out, because their radii are van der Waals radii).
- Cation < neutral atom < anion, for the same element.
- Among isoelectronic ions, more protons means a smaller ion: $\ce{Al^3+}$ is the smallest of the 10-electron set, $\ce{N^3-}$ the largest.
- (Exam extra) Ga (about 135 pm) is slightly **smaller** than Al (143 pm). The ten 3d electrons in Ga shield poorly, so the outer electrons feel a stronger pull.

:::trap
NCERT's footnote on isoelectronic species describes species with the same number of **atoms**, the same number of valence electrons and the same structure. For **sizes of ions**, as in the text and exercises of this chapter, "isoelectronic" means the same **total number of electrons**: $\ce{O^2-}$, $\ce{F-}$, Ne, $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$.
:::

## Ionization enthalpy

:::trap
The rising trend across a period has exceptions. In period 2: **Be > B** and **N > O**. The same reasons give **Mg > Al** (NCERT Problem 3.6) and **P > S** (exam extra) in period 3.
- Be/B: the electron removed from B is a 2p electron, which is shielded more than a 2s electron.
- N/O: N has a half-filled 2p set; in O two 2p electrons share an orbital and repel.
:::

- The highest first ionization enthalpy of all is **helium**. The lowest is **caesium**.
- **Group 13:** B 801, Al 577, Ga 579, In 558, Tl 589 kJ mol⁻¹. Ga is not lower than Al, and Tl is higher than In. These values are NCERT Exercise 3.19, but the text does not give the reason. (Exam extra reason: poor shielding by the filled 3d in Ga, and by 4f and 5d in Tl.)
- **Second ionization enthalpy:** Na has a **lower** $\Delta_i H_1$ than Mg but a **much higher** $\Delta_i H_2$. After losing one electron Na⁺ has a neon core, so the second electron comes from a filled inner shell.
- A big jump between two successive ionization enthalpies marks the **end of the valence electrons**. The number of electrons removed before the jump = number of valence electrons.

:::trap
Removing an electron from an orbital of **lower $n$** is **harder**, not easier (NCERT Exercise 3.37 tests this).
:::

- Isotopes of an element have the **same** ionization enthalpy: it depends on the electrons and nuclear charge, not on the nuclear mass.

## Electron gain enthalpy

:::trap
The **most negative** electron gain enthalpy belongs to **chlorine** ($-349$), not fluorine ($-328$). Similarly, S ($-200$) is more negative than O ($-141$). Electrons added to the small $n = 2$ shell feel more repulsion.
:::

- Noble gases have **positive** values (Ne +116 kJ mol⁻¹).
- (Exam extra) Be, Mg and N have positive or nearly zero values, much less negative than their neighbours: Be and Mg have a filled $ns^2$, N has a half-filled $2p^3$. These values are only estimates and books differ (NCERT Exercise 3.31 even lists $-40$ kJ mol⁻¹ for a Mg-like element), so learn the comparison, not a number.
- The **second** electron gain enthalpy of O is **positive**. The second electron is added to a negative ion, which repels it.

:::trap
**Sign conventions.** $\Delta_{eg}H$ is negative when energy is released. Electron affinity $A_e$ (used in many books) is **positive** when energy is released. So "highest electron affinity" means "most negative $\Delta_{eg}H$". NCERT uses $\Delta_{eg}H$.
:::

## Electronegativity

| Point | Electron gain enthalpy | Electronegativity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Of | an isolated gaseous atom | an atom in a compound |
| Measurable? | Yes (kJ mol⁻¹) | No (no unit; a scale) |
| Constant? | Yes | No, depends on the partner atom |

- F (4.0) is the most electronegative element. Next: O (3.5).
- On NCERT's Pauling table **N and Cl both have 3.0**. (Exam extra: newer Pauling values put Cl at 3.16, just above N at 3.04.)
- The statement "N has electronegativity 3.0 in all its compounds" is **wrong**: electronegativity varies with the atom it is bonded to.

## Periodic table facts

:::trap
- The **d-block has 10 columns** (a d subshell holds 10 electrons), not 8. The p-block has 6, the s-block 2, the f-block 14.
- Period number = principal quantum number of the **outermost** shell, not the atomic number or atomic mass.
- **He** is s-block by configuration ($1s^2$) but is placed in group 18.
- **Pd** is $4d^{10}5s^0$: a d-block element with no s electron.
:::

- Zn, Cd, Hg ($(n-1)d^{10}ns^2$) do not show most typical transition properties.
- Element 117 is in group 17: $[\text{Rn}]5f^{14}6d^{10}7s^27p^5$. Element 120 would be in group 2: $[\text{Og}]8s^2$ (NCERT writes [Uuo]8s²). Element 114 (Fl) is period 7, group 14.

:::trap
NCERT's text says period 7 is "incomplete" (theoretical maximum 32, ending at element 118), that 114 elements are known, and (Problem 3.3) that 117 is not yet discovered. These lines are out of date: the same chapter also says all elements up to 118 are discovered and officially named (Ts = 117, Og = 118). So period 7 is now complete with 32 elements. If a question quotes NCERT's "incomplete", it means the older wording.
:::

- Arranging by atomic number removes Mendeleev's misfits: Te (127.6) before I (126.9) is the NCERT example. (Exam extra: Ar (39.9) before K (39.1), Co (58.9) before Ni (58.7).)

## Valence and oxidation state

- Oxidation state and covalency can differ. In $\ce{[AlCl(H2O)5]^2+}$ Al has oxidation state **+3** but covalency **6**.
- Second-period elements cannot exceed covalency 4 (no 2d orbitals): $\ce{[BF4]-}$ exists, but not $\ce{[BF6]^3-}$.

## Oxides

:::remember
- Basic: $\ce{Na2O}$ (gives NaOH with water).
- Acidic: $\ce{Cl2O7}$ (gives $\ce{HClO4}$ with water).
- Amphoteric: $\ce{Al2O3}$, $\ce{As2O3}$.
- Neutral: CO, NO, $\ce{N2O}$.
:::

## NCERT lines to know

:::remember
- Mendeleev placed **iodine after tellurium**, even though iodine's atomic weight is lower.
- **Eka-aluminium = gallium**, **eka-silicon = germanium**.
- Metals are **more than 78%** of the known elements; non-metals are fewer than 20.
- **Metalloids:** Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te.
- Ga (303 K) and Cs (302 K) have very low melting points; Hg is a liquid metal.
- The anomaly of second-period elements comes from **small size, large charge/radius ratio, high electronegativity**, and only 4 valence orbitals.
- **Diagonal relationship:** Li with Mg, Be with Al.
- Chemical reactivity is **highest at the two ends** of a period and lowest in the centre.
:::
