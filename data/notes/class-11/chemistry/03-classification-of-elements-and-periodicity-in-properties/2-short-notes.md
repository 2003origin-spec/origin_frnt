## 1. Early attempts at classification

| Scientist | Year | Idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dobereiner | 1829 | Triads: middle element's atomic weight ≈ mean of the other two |
| Chancourtois | 1862 | Cylindrical table in order of atomic weight |
| Newlands | 1865 | Law of Octaves: every 8th element is like the 1st |
| Mendeleev, Lothar Meyer | 1869 | Periodic Law (atomic weights) |

- **Triads** (Li–Na–K, Ca–Sr–Ba, Cl–Br–I) worked for only a few elements. **Octaves** held only up to calcium.
- **Lothar Meyer** plotted atomic volume and other properties against atomic weight and saw a repeating pattern; his table was published after Mendeleev's.

## 2. Mendeleev's Periodic Table

- **Mendeleev's Periodic Law:** the properties of the elements are a periodic function of their **atomic weights**.
- He relied heavily on the formulas and properties of **compounds**.
- He **broke the atomic-weight order** when properties demanded it: iodine (lighter) was placed after tellurium, in group VII with F, Cl and Br.
- He **left gaps** for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties: **eka-aluminium = gallium** and **eka-silicon = germanium**.

| Property | Eka-Al (predicted) | Ga (found) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atomic weight | 68 | 70 |
| Density (g/cm³) | 5.9 | 5.94 |
| Oxide | $\ce{E2O3}$ | $\ce{Ga2O3}$ |

For eka-silicon he predicted atomic weight 72 (found 72.6), oxide $\ce{EO2}$ and chloride $\ce{ECl4}$.

## 3. Modern Periodic Law and the long form

- **Moseley (1913):** $\sqrt{\nu}$ of X-rays plotted against atomic number $Z$ gives a straight line. Atomic number is more basic than atomic mass.
- **Modern Periodic Law:** the physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their **atomic numbers**.
- The real cause of periodicity is the periodic repetition of **similar outer electronic configurations**.
- **Long form:** 7 periods (rows) and 18 groups (columns), numbered 1–18 by IUPAC. This replaces the old IA–VIIA, VIII, IB–VIIB and 0 labels.
- Period number = highest principal quantum number $n$ in the element.
- Elements per period: 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32. NCERT says period 7 is "incomplete" and could hold up to 32 (ending at element 118). All elements up to 118 are now discovered and named, so period 7 is in fact complete with 32 elements. Lanthanoids and actinoids (14 each) are placed in two rows at the bottom.

## 4. Naming elements with Z > 100

- Until a discovery is confirmed, IUPAC gives a **temporary systematic name** built from the digits of $Z$ (roots nil, un, bi, tri, quad, pent, hex, sept, oct, enn) plus "ium", with a **3-letter symbol**.
- It was introduced after the dispute over element 104 (Rutherfordium vs Kurchatovium). The permanent name comes later, by an IUPAC vote, and may honour a country or a scientist (Sg, for Seaborg).
- All elements up to $Z = 118$ now have official names.

## 5. Electronic configuration and the table

- The number of elements in a period = twice the number of orbitals being filled.
- **Period 4** (4s, 3d, 4p) and **period 5** (5s, 4d, 5p) have 18 elements. The 3d series runs from Sc (21) to Zn (30); the 4d series starts at Y (39).
- **Period 6** (6s, 4f, 5d, 6p) has 32. The 4f series (lanthanoids) runs from Ce (58) to Lu (71). **Period 7** is similar; the 5f series (actinoids) comes after Ac (89).
- Elements in one group have the same outer configuration, so similar properties. All alkali metals are $ns^1$: Li [He]2s¹, Na [Ne]3s¹, K [Ar]4s¹, and so on.

## 6. The four blocks

| Block | Groups | Outer configuration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| s | 1, 2 | $ns^{1-2}$ |
| p | 13–18 | $ns^2np^{1-6}$ |
| d | 3–12 | $(n-1)d^{1-10}ns^{0-2}$ |
| f | bottom rows | $(n-2)f^{1-14}(n-1)d^{0-1}ns^2$ |

- **s-block:** reactive metals with low ionization enthalpy. They form $\ce{M+}$ (group 1) or $\ce{M^2+}$ (group 2) ions. Metallic character and reactivity rise down the group. Never found free in nature. Compounds are mostly ionic, except those of Li and Be.
- **p-block:** with the s-block, these are the **representative** (main-group) elements. Each period ends with a noble gas, $ns^2np^6$, which is very unreactive. Halogens (17) and chalcogens (16) have large negative electron gain enthalpies.
- **d-block (transition elements):** all metals. Mostly coloured ions, variable oxidation states, paramagnetism, catalysts. **Pd is $4d^{10}5s^0$.** Zn, Cd and Hg ($(n-1)d^{10}ns^2$) do not show most transition properties.
- **f-block (inner transition):** lanthanoids Ce–Lu and actinoids Th–Lr. All metals. Actinoids are radioactive and show many oxidation states. Elements after uranium are **transuranium** elements.
- **Special cases:** He is $1s^2$ (s-block) but sits in group 18 because its shell is full. H ($1s^1$) is like group 1 and also like group 17 (it can gain one electron), so it is placed on its own at the top.

## 7. Metals, non-metals and metalloids

- **Metals** are more than 78% of the elements, on the left. Usually solid, high melting point, good conductors, malleable and ductile. Hg is liquid; Ga (303 K) and Cs (302 K) melt just above room temperature.
- **Non-metals** (fewer than 20) are at the top right. Usually solids or gases with low melting points (B and C are exceptions), poor conductors, brittle.
- **Metalloids** lie along the zig-zag line: Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te.
- Metallic character **increases down a group** and **decreases across a period**.

## 8. Atomic and ionic radius

- An atom has no sharp edge, so its size is estimated from distances in compounds. **Covalent radius** = half the single-bond length ($\ce{Cl2}$: 99 pm). **Metallic radius** = half the distance between neighbouring metal atoms (Cu: 128 pm). NCERT calls both "atomic radius".
- **Across a period:** radius **decreases**. Electrons enter the same shell while the effective nuclear charge rises.
- **Down a group:** radius **increases**. A new shell is added, and inner shells shield the outer electrons.
- Noble gases are left out: their radii are van der Waals radii, which are much larger, so compare them only with other van der Waals radii.
- **Cation < parent atom** (fewer electrons, same nuclear charge): Na 186 pm, $\ce{Na+}$ 95 pm (the value in NCERT's text). NCERT's table on second-period anomalies lists $\ce{Na+}$ as 102 pm, from a different data set. Both are far smaller than 186 pm, which is the point.
- **Anion > parent atom** (more electron repulsion, lower effective nuclear charge): F 64 pm, $\ce{F-}$ 136 pm.
- **Isoelectronic species** (same number of electrons): more nuclear charge means a smaller ion. For 10 electrons the radius order is

**$\ce{N^3-}$ > $\ce{O^2-}$ > $\ce{F-}$ > $\ce{Na+}$ > $\ce{Mg^2+}$ > $\ce{Al^3+}$**

## 9. Ionization enthalpy ($\Delta_i H$)

- Energy needed to remove the most loosely held electron from an **isolated gaseous atom in its ground state**. Always positive. $\Delta_i H_1 < \Delta_i H_2 < \Delta_i H_3$.
- **Maxima** at noble gases, **minima** at alkali metals.
- **Across a period: increases.** Nuclear charge rises faster than shielding.
- **Down a group: decreases.** The electron is farther away and better shielded.
- Two factors decide it: the nucleus's pull on the electron, and repulsion between electrons. Inner electrons **shield** (screen) the outer ones, so the outer electron feels an effective nuclear charge less than $Z$.
- **Exceptions in period 2:** Be > B (the 2s electron penetrates closer to the nucleus than 2p, so the 2p electron of B is easier to remove). N > O (half-filled 2p in N; in O two electrons share one 2p orbital and repel). Actual order:

$$\mathrm{Li < B < Be < C < O < N < F < Ne}$$

## 10. Electron gain enthalpy ($\Delta_{eg} H$)

- Enthalpy change when an isolated gaseous atom gains an electron. It can be negative (energy released) or positive.
- **Across a period:** becomes **more negative** (smaller atom, higher effective nuclear charge).
- **Down a group:** becomes **less negative** (bigger atom), as a general rule.
- **Halogens** have the most negative values. **Noble gases** have large positive values (the electron must enter a new shell).
- **Exception:** O and F are **less negative** than S and Cl. The small $n = 2$ shell is crowded, so the added electron is repelled more. **Chlorine** ($-349$ kJ mol⁻¹) has the most negative value of all.

## 11. Electronegativity

- A qualitative measure of how strongly an atom **in a compound** pulls the shared electron pair. It is not measurable directly and not constant: it depends on the atom it is bonded to.
- Scales: Pauling (most used; F = 4.0), Mulliken–Jaffe, Allred–Rochow.
- **Increases across a period, decreases down a group**, like ionization enthalpy.
- Higher electronegativity means more non-metallic; lower means more metallic.

## 12. Valence and oxidation state

- Valence of a representative element = number of outer electrons, or 8 minus that number.
- **Oxidation state:** the charge an atom gets when shared electrons are given to the more electronegative atom. In $\ce{OF2}$, O is +2 (F is more electronegative). In $\ce{Na2O}$, O is −2.
- Transition elements and actinoids show **variable** valence.

| Group | 1 | 2 | 13 | 14 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydride | NaH | $\ce{CaH2}$ | $\ce{B2H6}$ | $\ce{CH4}$ |
| Oxide | $\ce{Na2O}$ | MgO | $\ce{Al2O3}$ | $\ce{CO2}$ |

| Group | 15 | 16 | 17 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydride | $\ce{NH3}$ | $\ce{H2O}$ | HF |
| Oxide | $\ce{N2O5}$ | $\ce{SO3}$ | $\ce{Cl2O7}$ |

## 13. Anomalous second-period elements

- Li, Be and B to F differ from the rest of their groups because of **small size, high charge/radius ratio and high electronegativity**.
- They have only **4 valence orbitals** (2s, 2p), so their **maximum covalency is 4**: B forms only $\ce{[BF4]-}$, while Al can form $\ce{[AlF6]^3-}$.
- They form **pπ–pπ multiple bonds** easily: C=C, C≡C, N≡N, C=O, C≡N, N=O.
- Li and Be form compounds with marked covalent character.
- **Diagonal relationship:** Li resembles Mg, and Be resembles Al.

## 14. Reactivity and the nature of oxides

- Reactivity is **highest at the two ends** of a period and lowest in the middle. On the left it comes from easy electron **loss** (low $\Delta_i H$, alkali metals); on the right from easy electron **gain** (halogens).
- Down a group: alkali metals get **more** reactive (Li < Na < K < Rb < Cs); halogens get **less** reactive (F > Cl > Br > I).
- Oxides: left side **basic** ($\ce{Na2O}$), right side **acidic** ($\ce{Cl2O7}$), middle **amphoteric** ($\ce{Al2O3}$, $\ce{As2O3}$) or **neutral** (CO, NO, $\ce{N2O}$).

$$\ce{Na2O + H2O -> 2NaOH}$$

$$\ce{Cl2O7 + H2O -> 2HClO4}$$

- Transition metals: radius changes much less across a period, and even less for the 4f series. Their $\Delta_i H$ lies between the s- and p-blocks, so they are less electropositive than groups 1 and 2. Down a transition group the trend is **reversed** compared with main-group metals.
