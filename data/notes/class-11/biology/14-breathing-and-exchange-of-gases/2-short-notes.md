## 1. What breathing is

- Cells use O₂ to break down simple molecules (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids) and get energy. This also makes **CO₂, which is harmful**.
- So O₂ must reach the cells all the time and CO₂ must be removed.
- **Breathing** (commonly called respiration): exchange of O₂ from the air with the CO₂ made by the cells.

## 2. Respiratory organs in animals

How an animal breathes depends mainly on its **habitat** and its **level of organisation**.

| Animal | Breathes by |
| --- | --- |
| Sponges, coelenterates, flatworms | Simple diffusion over the whole body surface |
| Earthworm | Moist cuticle |
| Insects | Tracheal tubes (network of tubes) |
| Most aquatic arthropods and molluscs | Gills (branchial respiration) |
| Terrestrial animals | Lungs (pulmonary respiration) |

- Among vertebrates, **fishes** use gills. **Amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals** use lungs.
- **Frogs** can also breathe through their moist skin (**cutaneous respiration**).

## 3. Human respiratory system

The air path, in order:

External nostrils (above the upper lip) → nasal passage → nasal chamber → pharynx → larynx → trachea → primary bronchi → secondary and tertiary bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles → alveoli.

- **Pharynx:** part of it is the **common passage for food and air**.
- **Larynx:** a cartilaginous box that helps make sound, so it is called the **sound box**.
- **Epiglottis:** a thin, elastic, cartilaginous flap. It covers the **glottis** during swallowing, so food does not enter the larynx.
- **Trachea:** a straight tube that reaches the middle of the thoracic cavity. It divides into the right and left **primary bronchi** at the level of the **5th thoracic vertebra**.
- **Cartilage rings:** the trachea, primary, secondary and tertiary bronchi and the first bronchioles are held open by **incomplete** cartilaginous rings.
- **Alveoli:** each terminal bronchiole ends in many very thin, irregular-walled, vascularised bag-like structures. The branching bronchi, bronchioles and alveoli make up the **lungs**.
- **Pleura:** each lung is covered by a **double-layered pleura**, with **pleural fluid** between the layers. The fluid **reduces friction** on the lung surface. The outer layer touches the thoracic lining; the inner layer touches the lung surface.

### Two parts of the system

| Part | From – to | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conducting part | External nostrils up to the terminal bronchioles | Carries air to the alveoli; cleans, humidifies and warms it to body temperature |
| Respiratory (exchange) part | Alveoli and their ducts | Real diffusion of O₂ and CO₂ between blood and air |

### The thoracic chamber

- Formed **dorsally** by the vertebral column, **ventrally** by the sternum, **laterally** by the ribs and **below** by the dome-shaped **diaphragm**.
- It is **air-tight**. Any change in the volume of the thoracic cavity shows up in the lungs. This is essential, because we **cannot change the lung volume directly**.

## 4. Steps of respiration

1. **Breathing (pulmonary ventilation):** air is drawn in and CO₂-rich alveolar air is pushed out.
2. **Diffusion** of O₂ and CO₂ across the alveolar membrane.
3. **Transport** of the gases by the blood.
4. **Diffusion** of O₂ and CO₂ between blood and tissues.
5. **Use of O₂** by the cells for catabolic reactions, releasing CO₂ (cellular respiration).

## 5. Mechanism of breathing

Air moves because of a **pressure gradient** between the lungs and the atmosphere.
- **Inspiration:** pressure inside the lungs (**intra-pulmonary pressure**) is **less** than atmospheric pressure (a negative pressure in the lungs).
- **Expiration:** intra-pulmonary pressure is **higher** than atmospheric pressure.
- The muscles that make these gradients: the **diaphragm** and the **external and internal intercostal** muscles between the ribs.

### Inspiration (active)

1. The **diaphragm contracts**: the thoracic volume increases in the **antero-posterior** axis.
2. The **external intercostal muscles contract**: they lift the ribs and the sternum, so the volume increases in the **dorso-ventral** axis.
3. Thoracic volume rises, so pulmonary volume rises.
4. Intra-pulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric pressure, and air rushes into the lungs.

### Expiration (normal)

1. The diaphragm and intercostal muscles **relax**. The diaphragm and sternum return to their normal positions.
2. Thoracic volume, and so pulmonary volume, falls.
3. Intra-pulmonary pressure rises **slightly above** atmospheric pressure, and air is pushed out.

- **Additional muscles in the abdomen** can increase the strength of both inspiration and expiration.
- A healthy human breathes **12–16 times per minute**.
- A **spirometer** estimates the volume of air moved in breathing. This helps in the clinical assessment of lung function.

## 6. Respiratory volumes and capacities

| Term | Meaning | NCERT value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tidal volume (TV) | One normal breath in or out | About 500 mL |
| IRV | Extra air in by a forcible inspiration | 2500–3000 mL |
| ERV | Extra air out by a forcible expiration | 1000–1100 mL |
| Residual volume (RV) | Left in the lungs after a forcible expiration | 1100–1200 mL |

| Capacity | Sum | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IC | TV + IRV | All you can breathe in after a normal expiration |
| EC | TV + ERV | All you can breathe out after a normal inspiration |
| FRC | ERV + RV | Left in the lungs after a normal expiration |
| VC | ERV + TV + IRV | Most air in after a forced expiration (or out after a forced inspiration) |
| TLC | RV + ERV + TV + IRV = VC + RV | All the air in the lungs at the end of a forced inspiration |

- With TV about 500 mL, a healthy person breathes about **6000–8000 mL per minute**.
- The capacities are used in **clinical diagnosis**.

## 7. Exchange of gases

- **Alveoli** are the main sites of gas exchange. Gases are also exchanged between **blood and tissues**.
- O₂ and CO₂ move by **simple diffusion**, mainly along a **pressure (concentration) gradient**.
- The rate of diffusion also depends on:
  - the **solubility** of the gas;
  - the **thickness** of the membrane.

### Partial pressures (mm Hg, NCERT Table 14.1)

| Site | pO₂ | pCO₂ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atmospheric air | 159 | 0.3 |
| Alveoli | 104 | 40 |
| Deoxygenated blood | 40 | 45 |
| Oxygenated blood | 95 | 40 |
| Tissues | 40 | 45 |

- O₂ gradient: alveoli → blood → tissues. CO₂ gradient: the opposite way, tissues → blood → alveoli.
- CO₂ is **20–25 times more soluble** than O₂. So for each unit of partial pressure difference, much more CO₂ diffuses across the membrane than O₂.

### The diffusion membrane: three layers

1. The thin **squamous epithelium** of the alveoli.
2. The **endothelium** of the alveolar capillaries.
3. The **basement substance** between them: a thin basement membrane under the squamous epithelium, and the basement membrane around the single layer of endothelial cells.

Its total thickness is **much less than a millimetre**. So everything in the body favours diffusion of O₂ from alveoli to tissues, and of CO₂ from tissues to alveoli.

## 8. Transport of oxygen

- Blood carries both gases. About **97% of O₂** goes in the **RBCs**; the other **3%** is dissolved in **plasma**.
- **Haemoglobin** is the red, iron-containing pigment in RBCs. It binds O₂ **reversibly**, forming **oxyhaemoglobin**.
- Each haemoglobin molecule carries **at most 4 O₂** molecules.
- Binding depends mainly on **pO₂**. **pCO₂, H⁺ concentration and temperature** also affect it.

### Oxygen dissociation curve

- A plot of **% saturation of haemoglobin with O₂** (y-axis) against **pO₂** (x-axis). It is **sigmoid** (S-shaped).
- It is used to study how pCO₂, H⁺ and other factors affect O₂ binding.
- **In the alveoli:** high pO₂, low pCO₂, fewer H⁺, lower temperature. All favour **forming** oxyhaemoglobin.
- **In the tissues:** low pO₂, high pCO₂, more H⁺, higher temperature. All favour **releasing** O₂ from oxyhaemoglobin.
- So O₂ binds at the lungs and is given up at the tissues.
- Every **100 mL** of oxygenated blood delivers about **5 mL of O₂** to the tissues under normal conditions.

## 9. Transport of carbon dioxide

| Form | Share |
| --- | --- |
| Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) | About 70% |
| By RBCs, as carbamino-haemoglobin | 20–25% |
| Dissolved in plasma | About 7% |

- **Carbamino-haemoglobin:** CO₂ bound to haemoglobin. Binding depends on **pCO₂**, and **pO₂** is a major factor that affects it.
  - Tissues (high pCO₂, low pO₂): **more** CO₂ binds.
  - Alveoli (low pCO₂, high pO₂): CO₂ **comes off**.
- **Carbonic anhydrase:** an enzyme found at a very high concentration in **RBCs** and in tiny amounts in plasma. It speeds up the reaction in **both** directions:

$$\ce{CO2 + H2O <=> H2CO3}$$

$$\ce{H2CO3 <=> HCO3- + H+}$$

- At the tissues, pCO₂ is high (from catabolism). CO₂ enters the blood (RBCs and plasma) and forms HCO₃⁻ and H⁺.
- At the alveoli, pCO₂ is low. The reaction runs backwards and gives CO₂ and H₂O. So CO₂ carried as bicarbonate is breathed out as CO₂.
- Every **100 mL** of deoxygenated blood delivers about **4 mL of CO₂** to the alveoli.

## 10. Regulation of respiration

The **neural system** adjusts the rhythm of breathing to what the tissues need.
- **Respiratory rhythm centre** (in the **medulla**): mainly responsible for regulating breathing.
- **Pneumotaxic centre** (in the **pons**): moderates the rhythm centre. Its signals can **shorten inspiration** and so change the breathing rate.
- **Chemosensitive area** (next to the rhythm centre, in the medulla): very sensitive to **CO₂ and H⁺**. When they rise, it signals the rhythm centre to adjust breathing so that they are removed.
- **Receptors** on the **aortic arch** and **carotid artery** also detect changes in CO₂ and H⁺ and signal the rhythm centre.
- The role of **oxygen** in regulating the rhythm is **quite insignificant**.

## 11. Disorders of the respiratory system

| Disorder | What happens | Cause or note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asthma | Difficulty breathing, with wheezing | Inflammation of the bronchi and bronchioles |
| Emphysema | Chronic; alveolar walls damaged, so the respiratory surface decreases | A major cause is cigarette smoking |
| Occupational respiratory disorders | Long exposure to dust causes inflammation, then fibrosis (growth of fibrous tissue): serious lung damage | Industries with grinding or stone-breaking; workers should wear protective masks |
