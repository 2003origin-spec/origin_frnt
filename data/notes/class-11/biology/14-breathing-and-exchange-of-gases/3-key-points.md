## Respiratory organs

- Sponges, coelenterates and flatworms have **no** special respiratory organ: gases diffuse over the whole body surface.
- Earthworm: **moist cuticle**. Insects: **tracheal tubes**, not gills or lungs.
- Frogs breathe by **lungs and moist skin** (cutaneous respiration).

## The air passages

:::remember
- The trachea divides into the two primary bronchi at the level of the **5th thoracic vertebra**.
- Cartilage rings support the trachea, bronchi and **initial** bronchioles, and they are **incomplete** rings.
- The **epiglottis** covers the **glottis** during swallowing.
- **Pleural fluid** lies between the two pleural layers and **reduces friction**.
:::

:::trap
The **terminal bronchioles** belong to the **conducting** part, not the exchange part. Only the **alveoli and their ducts** form the exchange (respiratory) part. Gases do not diffuse in the rest of the system.
:::

## Mechanism of breathing

:::trap
Don't swap the axes:
- **Diaphragm** contracts → volume increases in the **antero-posterior** axis.
- **External intercostals** contract → ribs and sternum lift → volume increases in the **dorso-ventral** axis.
:::

- Inspiration needs muscles to **contract**. Normal expiration happens when they **relax**.
- During inspiration the lung pressure is **below** atmospheric pressure (negative). During expiration it is only **slightly above** atmospheric pressure.
- We **cannot change the lung volume directly**. It changes only because the air-tight thoracic chamber changes volume.
- Forceful breathing uses **additional muscles in the abdomen**.

## Volumes and capacities

:::trap
**After a normal expiration** the air left in the lungs is the **FRC** (ERV + RV). **After a forcible expiration** it is the **RV**. Read which expiration the question means.
:::

:::trap
**ERV** is the extra air pushed out by force. **EC** (expiratory capacity) is TV + ERV. The same goes for IRV and IC.
:::

- **VC does not include RV.** TLC = VC + RV.
- RV never leaves the lungs, so a spirometer cannot measure it (nor FRC or TLC, which contain it). This follows from NCERT's definitions; NCERT does not state it.
- Tidal volume is about 500 mL **per breath**, not per minute. The air breathed per minute is 6000–8000 mL.

:::tip
**Capacity = volumes added.**
- IC and EC: TV plus its own reserve (IRV or ERV).
- FRC: what is left after a normal breath out = ERV + RV.
- VC: everything except RV.
- TLC: everything.
:::

## Partial pressures

:::trap
Atmospheric air has **more O₂** (pO₂ 159) and **far less CO₂** (pCO₂ 0.3) than alveolar air (104 and 40). So, compared with alveolar air, atmospheric air has pO₂ **higher** and pCO₂ **lower**.
:::

- pO₂ of **deoxygenated blood = pO₂ of tissues = 40** mm Hg.
- pCO₂ of **oxygenated blood = pCO₂ of alveoli = 40** mm Hg.
- pCO₂ of **deoxygenated blood = pCO₂ of tissues = 45** mm Hg.
- The CO₂ gradient (5 mm Hg) is much smaller than the O₂ gradient (64 mm Hg at the lungs). CO₂ still diffuses well because it is **20–25 times more soluble** than O₂.

:::remember
The diffusion membrane has **three layers**: alveolar squamous epithelium, capillary endothelium and the basement substance between them. Its total thickness is **much less than a millimetre**.
:::

## Transport of gases

:::trap
Don't mix up the two sets of numbers:
- **O₂:** 97% in RBCs, 3% dissolved in plasma.
- **CO₂:** 70% as bicarbonate, 20–25% by RBCs as carbamino-haemoglobin, 7% dissolved in plasma.
:::

- Most CO₂ travels as **bicarbonate**, not bound to haemoglobin.
- **Carbonic anhydrase** is mostly in **RBCs**; plasma has only minute amounts. It works in **both directions**.
- One haemoglobin molecule carries **at most 4** O₂ molecules.
- 100 mL of oxygenated blood gives about **5 mL O₂** to tissues; 100 mL of deoxygenated blood gives about **4 mL CO₂** to the alveoli.
- The O₂ dissociation curve is **sigmoid**, with % saturation of haemoglobin on the y-axis and pO₂ on the x-axis.

:::tip
**Tissues are "high and hot":** high pCO₂, high H⁺, higher temperature, low pO₂. All of these make haemoglobin **give up** O₂. The alveoli are the opposite, so haemoglobin **takes up** O₂ there.

Beyond NCERT and the NEET syllabus (only for questions that use the words): high pCO₂, high H⁺ and higher temperature shift the curve **to the right**. Low pO₂ does not shift the curve; it only moves down the same curve. The effect of CO₂ and H⁺ on O₂ binding is called the **Bohr effect**.
:::

## Regulation

:::trap
- **Rhythm centre:** medulla. **Pneumotaxic centre:** pons. Not the other way round.
- The pneumotaxic centre **shortens inspiration**; it does not start breathing.
- The main chemical triggers are **CO₂ and H⁺**. The role of **O₂** is **quite insignificant**.
:::

- The **chemosensitive area** lies in the medulla, **next to the rhythm centre**.
- Receptors on the **aortic arch** and **carotid artery** also sense CO₂ and H⁺.

## Disorders

- **Asthma:** difficulty in breathing with wheezing, from **inflammation of the bronchi and bronchioles**.
- **Emphysema:** a **chronic** disorder; the **alveolar walls** are damaged, so the respiratory surface **decreases**. A major cause: **cigarette smoking**.
- **Occupational disorders:** dust from grinding or stone-breaking → inflammation → **fibrosis** (growth of fibrous tissue). Prevention: **protective masks**.

## Numbers to remember

:::remember
- Breathing rate: **12–16 per minute**.
- TV **500 mL**; IRV **2500–3000**; ERV **1000–1100**; RV **1100–1200** mL.
- pO₂ (mm Hg): air **159**, alveoli **104**, oxygenated blood **95**, deoxygenated blood and tissues **40**.
- pCO₂ (mm Hg): air **0.3**, alveoli and oxygenated blood **40**, deoxygenated blood and tissues **45**.
- CO₂ is **20–25 times** more soluble than O₂.
:::
