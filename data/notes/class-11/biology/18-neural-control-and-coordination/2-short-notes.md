## 1. Coordination

- **Coordination:** the process by which two or more organs interact and complement each other's functions.
- Example: during exercise, muscles need more energy and oxygen. So breathing rate, heart beat and blood flow all rise. When exercise stops, the nerves, lungs, heart and kidney slowly return to normal.
- Two systems coordinate and integrate the body's activities together:
  - the **neural system** gives an organised network of **point-to-point** connections for **quick** coordination;
  - the **endocrine system** gives **chemical** integration through **hormones**.

## 2. Neural systems in animals

- Every animal's neural system is made of **neurons**: highly specialised cells that detect, receive and transmit different kinds of stimuli.
- **Hydra** (lower invertebrate): just a **network of neurons**.
- **Insects:** a **brain** plus a number of **ganglia** and neural tissues.
- **Vertebrates:** a more developed neural system.

## 3. The human neural system

- **Central neural system (CNS):** the brain and the spinal cord. It is the site of **information processing and control**.
- **Peripheral neural system (PNS):** all the nerves of the body associated with the CNS.
- PNS nerve fibres are of two types:
  - **afferent fibres** carry impulses **from** tissues and organs **to** the CNS;
  - **efferent fibres** carry regulatory impulses **from** the CNS **to** the tissues and organs.
- The PNS has two divisions:
  - **somatic neural system:** relays impulses from the CNS to **skeletal muscles**;
  - **autonomic neural system:** carries impulses from the CNS to the **involuntary organs and smooth muscles**. It is further split into the **sympathetic** and **parasympathetic** neural systems.
- **Visceral nervous system:** the part of the PNS made of the whole complex of nerves, fibres, ganglia and plexuses that carry impulses from the CNS to the **viscera** and back from the viscera to the CNS.

(Exam extra, not in this NCERT chapter: humans have **12 pairs of cranial nerves** from the brain and **31 pairs of spinal nerves** from the spinal cord.)

## 4. The neuron

A neuron is the structural and functional unit of the neural system. It has three main parts.

- **Cell body:** cytoplasm, the usual cell organelles, and granular bodies called **Nissl's granules**.
- **Dendrites:** short fibres that branch repeatedly and project out of the cell body. They also contain Nissl's granules. They carry impulses **towards** the cell body.
- **Axon:** one long fibre whose far (distal) end is branched. Each branch ends in a bulb-like **synaptic knob**, which holds **synaptic vesicles** filled with **neurotransmitters**. The axon carries impulses **away from** the cell body to a **synapse** or a **neuro-muscular junction**.

| Feature | Dendrites | Axon |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number and length | Many, short | One, long |
| Nissl's granules | Present | Absent (exam extra, not in NCERT) |
| Impulse direction | Towards the cell body | Away from the cell body |

**Types by number of processes:**
- **Multipolar:** one axon and two or more dendrites. In the **cerebral cortex**.
- **Bipolar:** one axon and one dendrite. In the **retina** of the eye.
- **Unipolar:** cell body with one axon only. Usually in the **embryonic stage**.

**Myelinated and non-myelinated axons:**
- **Myelinated:** **Schwann cells** wrap the axon and form a **myelin sheath**. The gaps between two neighbouring myelin sheaths are the **nodes of Ranvier**. Found in **spinal and cranial nerves**.
- **Non-myelinated:** a Schwann cell encloses the axon but does **not** form a myelin sheath. Commonly found in the autonomous and somatic neural systems (NCERT's wording).

## 5. Why a resting neuron is polarised

Neurons are **excitable** cells because their membranes are polarised. The membrane has different **ion channels**, each selectively permeable to different ions.

At rest (not conducting an impulse) the axon membrane is:
- comparatively **more permeable to $\text{K}^+$**;
- **nearly impermeable to $\text{Na}^+$**;
- **impermeable** to the negatively charged proteins in the axoplasm.

So:
- the **axoplasm** (inside) has a **high** concentration of $\text{K}^+$ and negative proteins, and a **low** concentration of $\text{Na}^+$;
- the **fluid outside** has **low** $\text{K}^+$ and **high** $\text{Na}^+$.

These gradients are kept up by **active transport** by the **sodium-potassium pump**, which moves **3 $\text{Na}^+$ out for every 2 $\text{K}^+$ in**.

Result: the **outer surface is positive** and the **inner surface is negative**. The membrane is **polarised**. The electrical potential difference across the resting membrane is the **resting potential**.

(Exam extra, not in NCERT: the resting potential is about **−70 mV**, inside negative.)

## 6. Action potential: making a nerve impulse

1. A stimulus is applied at a site (say **A**) on the polarised membrane.
2. At A the membrane becomes **freely permeable to $\text{Na}^+$**. $\text{Na}^+$ rushes **in** (rapid influx).
3. The polarity reverses: the **outer surface becomes negative** and the **inner surface positive**. Site A is now **depolarised**.
4. The potential difference across the membrane at A is the **action potential**. This is the **nerve impulse**.

## 7. Conduction along the axon

- Just ahead of A, at site **B**, the membrane is still positive outside and negative inside.
- So a current flows:
  - on the **inner surface from A to B**;
  - on the **outer surface from B to A**, which completes the circuit.
- The polarity at B reverses, and an action potential is made at B. The impulse has moved from A to B.
- This repeats along the length of the axon, so the impulse is **conducted**.

**Repolarisation (getting back to rest):**
- The rise in $\text{Na}^+$ permeability is **extremely short-lived**.
- It is quickly followed by a rise in permeability to **$\text{K}^+$**.
- Within a fraction of a second, **$\text{K}^+$ diffuses out** and restores the resting potential at the excited site.
- The fibre can now respond to another stimulus.

:::remember
NCERT's summary: the nerve impulse is conducted along the axon membrane as a **wave of depolarisation and repolarisation**.
:::

**Myelinated fibres conduct faster** (exam extra, not in the NCERT text; NCERT Exercise 10 asks you to compare the two). In a myelinated fibre the impulse jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next (**saltatory conduction**). In a non-myelinated fibre it moves continuously along the whole membrane, which is slower.

## 8. Synapse: passing the impulse on

- A **synapse** is the junction where an impulse passes from one neuron to the next. It is formed by the membranes of a **pre-synaptic** and a **post-synaptic** neuron, which may or may not be separated by a gap, the **synaptic cleft**.
- **Electrical synapse:**
  - the two membranes are very close together;
  - electric current flows **directly** from one neuron to the other;
  - it works much like conduction along a single axon;
  - it is **always faster** than a chemical synapse;
  - it is **rare** in our neural system.
- **Chemical synapse:** the membranes are separated by a fluid-filled **synaptic cleft**, and **neurotransmitters** carry the signal across.

**How a chemical synapse works:**
1. An impulse (action potential) arrives at the axon terminal.
2. It makes the **synaptic vesicles** move to the plasma membrane. They fuse with it and **release neurotransmitters** into the synaptic cleft.
3. The neurotransmitters bind to their **specific receptors** on the **post-synaptic membrane**.
4. This binding **opens ion channels**, and ions enter.
5. A new potential is produced in the post-synaptic neuron. It may be **excitatory or inhibitory**.

## 9. The brain: command and control

The brain is the body's central information-processing organ, its **"command and control system"**. It controls:
- voluntary movements and the balance of the body;
- vital involuntary organs (lungs, heart, kidneys);
- thermoregulation, hunger and thirst;
- **circadian (24-hour) rhythms**;
- several endocrine glands, and human behaviour.

It is also where vision, hearing, speech, memory, intelligence, emotions and thoughts are processed.

**Protection:**
- The **skull** protects the brain.
- Inside the skull, the **cranial meninges** cover it:
  - **dura mater:** outer layer;
  - **arachnoid:** very thin middle layer;
  - **pia mater:** inner layer, in contact with the brain tissue.

The brain has three major parts: **forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain**.

## 10. Forebrain

The forebrain has the **cerebrum, thalamus and hypothalamus**.

**Cerebrum**
- The **largest** part of the human brain.
- A deep cleft divides it lengthwise into the **left and right cerebral hemispheres**. They are joined by a tract of nerve fibres, the **corpus callosum**.
- **Cerebral cortex:** the layer of cells covering each hemisphere, thrown into prominent folds. It is the **grey matter**: its greyish colour comes from the neuron **cell bodies** concentrated there.
- The cortex has **motor areas**, **sensory areas**, and large **association areas** that are neither clearly sensory nor motor. The association areas handle complex functions: intersensory associations, memory and communication.
- **White matter:** the inner part of the hemisphere, made of **myelinated** fibres of the tracts. The myelin gives it an opaque white look.

**Thalamus**
- The cerebrum wraps around it.
- A **major coordinating centre** for sensory and motor signalling.

**Hypothalamus**
- Lies at the **base of the thalamus**.
- Has centres that control **body temperature** and the **urge for eating and drinking**.
- Has groups of **neurosecretory cells** that secrete the **hypothalamic hormones**.

**Limbic system (limbic lobe)**
- Made of the inner parts of the cerebral hemispheres and a group of deep structures such as the **amygdala** and **hippocampus**.
- Together with the hypothalamus, it controls **sexual behaviour**, the expression of **emotions** (excitement, pleasure, rage and fear) and **motivation**.
- NCERT's summary also links it with **olfaction** and **autonomic responses**.

## 11. Midbrain

- Lies between the thalamus/hypothalamus of the forebrain and the **pons** of the hindbrain.
- A canal, the **cerebral aqueduct**, passes through it.
- Its dorsal part has four round swellings (lobes): the **corpora quadrigemina**.
- NCERT's summary: it **receives and integrates visual, tactile and auditory inputs**.

## 12. Hindbrain and brain stem

The hindbrain has the **pons, cerebellum and medulla** (medulla oblongata).

- **Pons:** fibre tracts that interconnect different regions of the brain.
- **Cerebellum:** a very convoluted (folded) surface, to make room for many more neurons. NCERT's summary: it integrates information from the **semicircular canals of the ear** and the auditory system. (Exam extra, not stated in NCERT: so it helps keep the body's balance and posture, and it coordinates muscle movements.)
- **Medulla:** connected to the **spinal cord**. It has centres that control **respiration, cardiovascular reflexes and gastric secretions**.

**Brain stem** = **midbrain + pons + medulla oblongata**. It connects the brain to the spinal cord.

| Part | Division | Key fact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Corpus callosum | Forebrain | Joins the two hemispheres |
| Hypothalamus | Forebrain | Temperature, hunger, thirst; hormones |
| Corpora quadrigemina | Midbrain | Four lobes on the dorsal side |
| Cerebellum | Hindbrain | Very convoluted surface; not in brain stem |
| Medulla | Hindbrain | Respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, gastric secretions |
