## What this chapter now covers

:::remember
The current (rationalised) NCERT chapter covers the neural system, the neuron, the nerve impulse, the synapse and the parts of the brain. **Reflex action, the reflex arc and the sense organs (eye and ear)** are no longer in this chapter, and they are not in the NEET 2026 syllabus for this unit either.
:::

## Divisions of the neural system

- **Afferent** = towards the CNS (from tissues and organs). **Efferent** = away from the CNS (to tissues and organs).
- **Somatic** neural system goes to **skeletal** muscles. **Autonomic** goes to **involuntary organs and smooth** muscles.
- The autonomic neural system has two parts: **sympathetic** and **parasympathetic**.

:::trap
The **visceral nervous system** is part of the **PNS**, not the CNS. It carries impulses **both ways**: CNS → viscera and viscera → CNS.
:::

:::tip
**A**fferent **A**rrives at the CNS. **E**fferent **E**xits the CNS.
:::

## The neuron

- **Dendrites** carry impulses **towards** the cell body. The **axon** carries them **away**.
- **Nissl's granules** are in the cell body **and** in the dendrites (NCERT).
- Synaptic **vesicles** with neurotransmitters are in the **synaptic knobs** at the end of the axon branches, not in the dendrites.

:::trap
Match neuron types to places carefully:
- **multipolar:** cerebral cortex;
- **bipolar:** retina of the eye;
- **unipolar:** usually the embryonic stage.
:::

- The **myelin sheath** is formed by **Schwann cells**. A non-myelinated fibre is **also** enclosed by a Schwann cell, but the cell does not form a myelin sheath.
- **Nodes of Ranvier** are the **gaps** between two neighbouring myelin sheaths, not part of the sheath.

## Resting potential

:::remember
At rest: **$\text{K}^+$ high inside, $\text{Na}^+$ high outside**. Outer surface **+**, inner surface **−**. The membrane is **polarised**.
:::

:::trap
The sodium-potassium pump moves **3 $\text{Na}^+$ out** and **2 $\text{K}^+$ in**. Students often swap the numbers or the directions. Remember: more positive charge goes **out** than comes in.
:::

- At rest the membrane is **more permeable to $\text{K}^+$** than to $\text{Na}^+$ (nearly impermeable to $\text{Na}^+$).
- The negatively charged **proteins** inside cannot leave: the membrane is impermeable to them.

## Action potential and conduction

:::trap
**Depolarisation is caused by $\text{Na}^+$ moving in.** Getting back to rest (repolarisation) is caused by **$\text{K}^+$ moving out**. Don't swap the ions, and don't say $\text{K}^+$ moves in during repolarisation.
:::

- After depolarisation the polarity is **reversed**: outer surface **−**, inner surface **+**.
- The action potential **is** the nerve impulse.
- The rise in $\text{Na}^+$ permeability is **extremely short-lived**.

:::trap
Direction of current between the excited site A and the site B just ahead:
- **inner** surface: **A → B**;
- **outer** surface: **B → A**.

The outer current flows **back** towards the excited site. That completes the circuit.
:::

- (Exam extra, not in NCERT) Myelinated fibres conduct impulses **faster** than non-myelinated ones, because the impulse jumps from node to node (saltatory conduction).

## Synapse

:::trap
"Synaptic cleft is always present" is **wrong**. NCERT: the two membranes **may or may not** be separated by a cleft. At an **electrical** synapse they are very close; a fluid-filled cleft is a feature of the **chemical** synapse.
:::

- **Electrical** synapses are **always faster** but **rare** in our system. **Chemical** synapses use **neurotransmitters**.
- The **receptors** for neurotransmitters are on the **post-synaptic** membrane. The vesicles are in the **pre-synaptic** axon terminal.
- The new potential in the post-synaptic neuron can be **excitatory or inhibitory**, not only excitatory.

:::tip
Chemical synapse order: **Arrive → Fuse → Release → Bind → Open**. (Impulse arrives, vesicles fuse, neurotransmitter is released, it binds receptors, ion channels open.)
:::

## Brain

:::tip
Meninges from outside in: **D**ura, **A**rachnoid, **P**ia: "**DAP**". The pia mater touches the brain.
:::

:::trap
The **brain stem** is midbrain + pons + medulla. The **cerebellum** is in the hindbrain but is **not** part of the brain stem. The **thalamus and hypothalamus** are forebrain, not brain stem.
:::

- **Grey matter** (cerebral cortex) = neuron **cell bodies**. **White matter** (inner part) = **myelinated** fibres of the tracts.
- The **corpus callosum** joins the **two cerebral hemispheres**. It is a tract of nerve fibres.
- The **hypothalamus** lies at the **base of the thalamus**.
- The **cerebral aqueduct** passes through the **midbrain**. The **corpora quadrigemina** are on its **dorsal** side.
- The **cerebellum** has a very folded surface to make room for **more neurons**.
- The **medulla** is joined to the spinal cord.

:::remember
Who controls what (NCERT):
- **Hypothalamus:** body temperature, eating and drinking; makes hypothalamic hormones.
- **Limbic system + hypothalamus:** sexual behaviour, emotions (excitement, pleasure, rage, fear), motivation.
- **Thalamus:** major coordinating centre for sensory and motor signals.
- **Medulla:** respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, gastric secretions.
- **Association areas** of the cortex: intersensory associations, memory, communication.
:::

## NCERT exercise one-liners

- Most developed part of the human brain: the **cerebrum**.
- Part of the CNS that acts as the **master clock** (circadian rhythm): the **hypothalamus**. This is the expected answer; the chapter text itself only says the brain controls circadian rhythms. (Exam extra: the clock sits in its suprachiasmatic nucleus.)

:::trap
Don't answer "pineal gland" for the master clock of the **CNS**. In the next chapter NCERT says the pineal's hormone, **melatonin**, helps regulate the 24-hour (diurnal) rhythm, but the pineal is an endocrine gland. The master clock of the neural system is the **hypothalamus**.
:::
- Thalamus vs hypothalamus: the thalamus relays and coordinates sensory and motor signals; the hypothalamus controls temperature, hunger and thirst and makes hormones.
- Cerebrum vs cerebellum: the cerebrum is forebrain (the largest part; motor, sensory and association areas); the cerebellum is hindbrain (input from the semicircular canals and auditory system).
