## 1. Living and non-living matter

- **Every element in the earth's crust is also found in living tissue.** The difference is in relative amounts: living matter is richer in **carbon and hydrogen** (NCERT's summary adds oxygen).
- Human body: O 65%, C 18.5%, H 9.5%. Earth's crust: O 46.6%, Si 27.7%, C only 0.03%.

## 2. How to analyse chemical composition

- Grind a tissue (a vegetable, a piece of liver) in **trichloroacetic acid** ($\mathrm{Cl_3CCOOH}$) and strain it through cheesecloth. The **filtrate** is the **acid-soluble pool** (thousands of organic compounds); the **retentate** is the **acid-insoluble fraction**.
- All carbon compounds from living tissue can be called **biomolecules**. A biologist groups them as amino acids, nucleotide bases, fatty acids and so on.
- **Inorganic** content needs a destructive test: weigh the tissue (**wet weight**), dry it (**dry weight**), then burn it. Carbon compounds leave as $\mathrm{CO_2}$ and water vapour; the **ash** left has inorganic elements (Ca, Mg).
- Inorganic constituents: $\mathrm{Na^+}$, $\mathrm{K^+}$, $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$, $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$, water, NaCl, $\mathrm{CaCO_3}$, phosphate, sulphate. Sulphate and phosphate also show up in the acid-soluble fraction.

## 3. Amino acids

- **α-amino acids** have the amino group and the carboxyl group on the **same carbon** (the α-carbon). They are **substituted methanes**: the α-carbon carries H, –COOH, –NH₂ and a variable **R group**.
- Only **20 types** occur in proteins. R = H in **glycine**, –CH₃ in **alanine**, –CH₂OH in **serine**.
- By the number of amino and carboxyl groups: **acidic** (glutamic acid), **basic** (lysine), **neutral** (valine). **Aromatic:** tyrosine, phenylalanine, tryptophan.
- The –NH₂ and –COOH groups are **ionisable**, so the structure of an amino acid changes with pH. The form with both $\mathrm{NH_3^+}$ and $\mathrm{COO^-}$ is the **zwitterionic form**.

## 4. Lipids

- Lipids are generally **water insoluble**.
- **Fatty acid:** a carboxyl group attached to an R group of 1 to 19 carbons (methyl, ethyl or more –CH₂ groups). **Palmitic acid** has 16 carbons and **arachidonic acid** 20, counting the carboxyl carbon. **Saturated** = no C=C; **unsaturated** = one or more C=C.
- **Glycerol** (trihydroxy propane) is another simple lipid. Fatty acids **esterified** with glycerol give **mono-, di- and triglycerides**, called **fats** or **oils** by melting point. Oils melt lower and stay liquid in winter (**gingelly oil**).
- **Phospholipids** have phosphorus and a phosphorylated organic compound. They are found in the **cell membrane**. Example: **lecithin**.
- Neural tissue has lipids with more complex structures. NCERT Fig. 9.1 also shows **cholesterol**.

## 5. Nitrogen bases, nucleosides and nucleotides

- Nitrogen bases are **heterocyclic** compounds: **adenine, guanine, cytosine, uracil and thymine**.
- Base + sugar = **nucleoside** (adenosine, guanosine, thymidine, uridine, cytidine).
- Base + sugar + phosphate esterified to the sugar = **nucleotide** (adenylic acid, guanylic acid, thymidylic acid, uridylic acid, cytidylic acid).
- Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are made of nucleotides only. DNA and RNA act as **genetic material**.

## 6. Primary and secondary metabolites

- NCERT calls these biomolecules **metabolites**.
- **Primary metabolites:** the compounds of Fig. 9.1 (amino acids, sugars, fatty acids, glycerol, bases, nucleotides). They have known roles in normal physiological processes. All of them are found in animal tissues.
- **Secondary metabolites:** thousands of extra compounds found in **plant, fungal and microbial** cells. Examples: alkaloids, flavonoids, rubber, essential oils, antibiotics, coloured pigments, scents, gums, spices.
- We **do not yet understand** the role of all secondary metabolites in their host. Many are useful to humans (rubber, drugs, spices, scents, pigments), and some have ecological importance.

## 7. Biomacromolecules

- Acid-soluble pool: molecular weights **18 to about 800 Da**.
- Acid-insoluble fraction: only **four** kinds of organic compounds, **proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides and lipids**. Except lipids, they are **polymers** of **10,000 Da and above**.
- So there are **micromolecules** (below **1000 Da**, also simply called biomolecules) and **macromolecules** (biomacromolecules, the acid-insoluble fraction).
- **Why lipids land in the insoluble fraction:** lipids are small (under 800 Da) but make up membranes. Grinding breaks membranes into **water-insoluble vesicles**, which separate with the insoluble pool. So lipids are **not strictly macromolecules**.
- The acid-soluble pool is roughly the **cytoplasm's** composition; macromolecules of cytoplasm and organelles make the insoluble fraction.
- **Water** is the most abundant chemical in living organisms (70–90% of cell mass). Next come proteins (10–15%).

## 8. Proteins

- Proteins are **polypeptides**: linear chains of amino acids joined by **peptide bonds**.
- With 20 types of amino acids, a protein is a **heteropolymer** (a homopolymer repeats one type of monomer n times).
- **Essential amino acids** come from dietary proteins; **non-essential** ones the body can make.
- Proteins transport nutrients, fight infection, act as hormones and enzymes, and more (Table 9.5 on the Key Terms sheet).

:::remember
**Collagen** is the most abundant protein in the **animal world**. **RuBisCO** (Ribulose bisphosphate Carboxylase-Oxygenase) is the most abundant protein in the **whole biosphere**.
:::

## 9. Polysaccharides

- Polysaccharides are long chains of monosaccharides.
- **Cellulose:** a **homopolymer** of **glucose** only. Plant cell walls, paper and cotton fibre are cellulose.
- **Starch:** a variant, the energy store of **plants**. **Glycogen:** the **animal** variant, **branched**. In a chain the **right end** is the **reducing end** and the **left end** is the **non-reducing end**.
- **Inulin:** a polymer of **fructose**.
- **Starch forms helical secondary structures** and can hold $\mathrm{I_2}$ in the helix. The starch–$\mathrm{I_2}$ complex is **blue**. Cellulose has no complex helices, so it cannot hold $\mathrm{I_2}$.
- **Complex polysaccharides** have amino-sugars and chemically modified sugars as building blocks (glucosamine, N-acetyl galactosamine). **Chitin**, in the **exoskeleton of arthropods**, is one. These are mostly homopolymers.

## 10. Nucleic acids

- Nucleic acids are **polynucleotides**. With polysaccharides and polypeptides, they make the **true macromolecular fraction** of a cell.
- Nucleotide = **heterocyclic** base + **monosaccharide** + **phosphoric acid** (phosphate).
- **Adenine and guanine** are substituted **purines**. **Cytosine, thymine and uracil** are substituted **pyrimidines**.
- The sugar is **ribose** (a pentose) in **RNA** and **2′-deoxyribose** in **DNA**.

## 11. Structure of proteins

Biologists describe protein structure at **four levels**.

- **Primary:** the **sequence** of amino acids. Pictured as a line: the left end is the first amino acid (**N-terminal**), the right end the last (**C-terminal**).
- **Secondary:** parts of the thread fold into a **helix** (like a revolving staircase); only **right-handed** helices occur in proteins. Other parts take other forms. NCERT Fig. 9.3 shows the **α-helix** and **β-pleated sheet**.
- **Tertiary:** the whole chain folds on itself like a **hollow woollen ball**, giving the 3-D shape. It is **absolutely necessary** for many biological activities. (Fig. 9.3 marks hydrogen and disulphide bonds.)
- **Quaternary:** the arrangement of two or more folded subunits. **Adult human haemoglobin**: **4 subunits, 2 α and 2 β**.

## 12. Enzymes: basics

- **Almost all enzymes are proteins.** Some nucleic acids act like enzymes: **ribozymes**.
- In the tertiary structure the chain criss-crosses itself and makes pockets. One is the **active site**, where the substrate fits.
- Inorganic catalysts work well at high temperature and pressure. Enzymes are damaged at high temperature (say **above 40°C**), except those of **thermophiles** (hot vents, sulphur springs), which work up to **80–90°C**.

## 13. Chemical reactions and their rates

- **Physical change:** shape or state changes, no bonds broken (ice melting). **Chemical reaction:** bonds broken and new bonds formed (hydrolysis of starch into glucose).
- **Rate** = amount of product formed per unit time ($\delta P/\delta t$). With a direction it is called **velocity**.
- Rule of thumb: the rate **doubles** (or halves) for every **10°C** rise (or fall).
- **Carbonic anhydrase:** without the enzyme, about 200 molecules of $\mathrm{H_2CO_3}$ form per hour. With it, about 600,000 form every second: about **10 million times** faster.
- **Metabolic pathway:** a multistep reaction where each step is catalysed by the same enzyme complex or different enzymes. Glucose → 2 pyruvic acid takes **ten** enzyme-catalysed steps.
- The same pathway gives different end products in different conditions: **pyruvic acid** (normal aerobic), **lactic acid** (skeletal muscle, anaerobic), **ethanol** (yeast fermentation).

## 14. How enzymes speed up reactions

- The substrate (S) diffuses to the active site and binds: an **ES complex** must form (short-lived). While bound, S takes an unstable **transition state structure**; then bonds break or form and the **product** (P) is released.
- Energy graph (NCERT Fig. 9.4): if P is lower than S, the reaction is **exothermic**. Either way, S must pass through the high-energy **transition state**.
- **Activation energy** = energy of the transition state minus the average energy of S.
- **Enzymes lower this energy barrier**, making S → P easier.

## 15. Nature of enzyme action

$$\mathrm{E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \rightarrow EP \rightarrow E + P}$$

The catalytic cycle:
1. The substrate binds to and fits into the active site.
2. Binding makes the enzyme **change shape** and fit more tightly around the substrate. (The idea is in NCERT; the name **induced fit** is not. Exam extra: it falls under "enzyme action" in the NEET syllabus.)
3. The active site breaks the substrate's bonds, and an **enzyme–product (EP) complex** forms.
4. The enzyme releases the product. The free enzyme is ready for another substrate molecule.

## 16. Factors affecting enzyme activity

Anything that alters the **tertiary structure** of the protein: temperature, pH, substrate concentration and specific chemicals.

- **Temperature and pH:** enzymes work in a narrow range. Each has an **optimum temperature** and **optimum pH**, with activity falling on both sides. **Low temperature** keeps the enzyme **temporarily inactive**. **High temperature** destroys activity, because heat **denatures** proteins.
- **Substrate concentration:** velocity rises at first, then reaches a **maximum velocity ($V_{max}$)**. More substrate does not raise it, because there are fewer enzyme molecules than substrate molecules and all are saturated. (On NCERT's graph, $K_m$ is the [S] at which velocity is half of $V_{max}$.)
- **Inhibitor:** a chemical whose binding shuts off enzyme activity (**inhibition**). A **competitive inhibitor** resembles the substrate and competes for its binding site: **malonate** inhibits **succinic dehydrogenase** (substrate **succinate**). Such inhibitors are used to **control bacterial pathogens**.

## 17. Classification of enzymes

Enzymes are grouped by the reaction they catalyse: **6 classes**, each with **4–13 subclasses**, named by a **four-digit number**.

- **Oxidoreductases/dehydrogenases:** oxidation–reduction between two substrates S and S′.
- **Transferases:** transfer of a group G (other than hydrogen) from S to S′.
- **Hydrolases:** hydrolysis of ester, ether, peptide, glycosidic, C–C, C–halide or P–N bonds.
- **Lyases:** remove groups by means other than hydrolysis, **leaving double bonds**.
- **Isomerases:** interconvert optical, geometric or positional isomers.
- **Ligases:** link two compounds together (C–O, C–S, C–N, P–O bonds).

## 18. Co-factors

- Some enzymes need a **non-protein co-factor** to be active; the protein part is the **apoenzyme**. (Beyond the syllabus, but a common term: apoenzyme + co-factor = **holoenzyme**. The word is not in NCERT.)
- **Prosthetic groups:** organic, **tightly bound** to the apoenzyme. **Haem** in **peroxidase** and **catalase** (which break hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen) is part of the active site.
- **Co-enzymes:** organic, bound only **transiently** (during catalysis); one serves many different enzymes. Many contain **vitamins**: **NAD** and **NADP** contain **niacin**.
- **Metal ions:** form coordination bonds with side chains at the active site and with the substrate. **Zinc** is the co-factor of the proteolytic enzyme **carboxypeptidase**.
- When the co-factor is removed, **catalytic activity is lost**. This shows co-factors are crucial.
