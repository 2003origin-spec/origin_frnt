## 1. Movement and locomotion

- **Movement** is a key feature of living beings: protoplasmic streaming in *Amoeba*, cilia, flagella, tentacles, and our limbs, jaws, eyelids and tongue.
- **Locomotion:** voluntary movement that **changes the place** (location) of the animal. Walking, running, climbing, flying and swimming are examples.
- The same structure can do both jobs:
  - *Paramoecium:* cilia move food through the cytopharynx **and** help in locomotion.
  - *Hydra:* tentacles catch prey **and** help in locomotion.
  - Humans: limbs change body posture **and** move us from place to place.

:::remember
All locomotions are movements, but **not all movements are locomotions**.
:::

- Animals move in search of food, shelter, a mate, breeding grounds or a better climate, or to escape enemies and predators.

## 2. Movements shown by human cells

Human cells show three main types of movement: **amoeboid, ciliary and muscular**.

| Type | How | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amoeboid | Pseudopodia, by streaming of protoplasm; microfilaments help | Macrophages, leucocytes |
| Ciliary | Coordinated beating of cilia | Trachea (removes dust), female reproductive tract (moves ova) |
| Muscular | Contraction of muscle | Limbs, jaws, tongue |

- **Flagellar movement** (also in NCERT): swimming of **spermatozoa**, water current in the canal system of **sponges**, and locomotion of protists like ***Euglena***.
- Locomotion needs the muscular, skeletal and neural systems working together.

## 3. Muscle: general features

- Muscle is a specialised tissue of **mesodermal** origin.
- About **40–50%** of the body weight of an adult human is muscle.
- Four properties: **excitability, contractility, extensibility and elasticity**.

## 4. Three types of muscle

Grouped by location, appearance and control.

- **Skeletal muscle:** attached to the skeleton. Striped under the microscope, so **striated**. Under voluntary control, so **voluntary**. Used for locomotion and changes of posture.
- **Visceral muscle:** in the inner walls of hollow organs such as the alimentary canal and reproductive tract. No striations, so **smooth** (non-striated). **Involuntary**. Moves food along the gut and gametes along the genital tract.
- **Cardiac muscle:** the muscle of the heart. Many cells join in a **branching** pattern. **Striated** but **involuntary**: the nervous system does not control it directly.

## 5. Structure of a skeletal muscle

From large to small:

1. **Muscle:** many **fascicles** (muscle bundles), held together by a collagenous connective tissue layer, the **fascia**.
2. **Fascicle:** contains many **muscle fibres**.
3. **Muscle fibre** (muscle cell): lined by the plasma membrane, the **sarcolemma**, which encloses the **sarcoplasm**.
4. **Myofibrils** (myofilaments): many, arranged parallel in the sarcoplasm.

- A muscle fibre is a **syncytium**: its sarcoplasm has **many nuclei**.
- Its endoplasmic reticulum, the **sarcoplasmic reticulum**, is the **store house of calcium ions**.

## 6. Bands and the sarcomere

- Each myofibril has alternating **light and dark bands**. The striations come from the arrangement of two proteins, **actin** and **myosin**.
- **I band (isotropic):** light band; contains actin.
- **A band (anisotropic):** dark band; contains myosin.
- Actin filaments are thinner (**thin filaments**); myosin filaments are thicker (**thick filaments**). Both lie parallel to each other and to the long axis of the myofibril.
- **Z line:** an elastic fibre in the centre of each I band that bisects it. Thin filaments are firmly attached to it.
- **M line:** a thin fibrous membrane in the middle of the A band that holds the thick filaments together.
- **Sarcomere:** the part between **two successive Z lines**. It is the **functional unit of contraction**.
- **H zone:** at rest, thin filaments overlap only the ends of the thick filaments; the central part they do not overlap is the H zone.

:::remember
- **Anatomical unit** of muscle: the muscle fibre.
- **Functional unit** of contraction: the sarcomere (Z line to Z line).
:::

## 7. Contractile proteins

**Thin (actin) filament**
- Two **F-actins** (filamentous) wound helically around each other.
- Each F-actin is a polymer of **G-actin** (globular) monomers.
- Two filaments of **tropomyosin** run close to the F-actins along their whole length.
- **Troponin**, a complex protein, sits at regular intervals on the tropomyosin.
- At rest, a **subunit of troponin masks** the active binding sites for myosin on actin.

**Thick (myosin) filament**
- A polymer of many monomers called **meromyosins**.
- Each meromyosin has:
  - a **globular head with a short arm** = **heavy meromyosin (HMM)**;
  - a **tail** = **light meromyosin (LMM)**.
- The head and short arm project out from the filament at regular distances and angles: the **cross arm**.
- The globular head is an active **ATPase** enzyme, with binding sites for **ATP** and active sites for **actin**.

## 8. How a muscle contracts

**Sliding filament theory:** a muscle fibre contracts by the **thin filaments sliding over the thick filaments**.

1. The CNS sends a signal through a **motor neuron** (motor neuron + its muscle fibres = **motor unit**).
2. The signal reaches the **neuromuscular junction** (motor-end plate), the junction between the motor neuron and the sarcolemma.
3. The neurotransmitter **acetylcholine** is released. It sets up an **action potential** in the sarcolemma.
4. The action potential spreads through the fibre and causes the release of **Ca++** into the sarcoplasm (from the sarcoplasmic reticulum).
5. Ca++ binds to a **subunit of troponin**. This removes the masking of the active sites on actin.
6. Using energy from **ATP hydrolysis**, the myosin head binds to the exposed sites on actin and forms a **cross bridge**.
7. The cross bridge pulls the actin filaments towards the **centre of the A band**. The Z lines attached to them are pulled inwards, so the **sarcomere shortens**: contraction.
8. Myosin releases **ADP and Pi** and goes back to its relaxed state.
9. A **new ATP binds** to the myosin head and the cross bridge **breaks**.
10. The ATP is hydrolysed again, and the cycle of making and breaking cross bridges repeats, giving more sliding.

:::remember
During contraction the **I band gets shorter**, but the **A band keeps its length**. NCERT's Fig. 17.5 also shows the **H zone narrowing** (and disappearing when the muscle is maximally contracted).
:::

## 9. Relaxation, fatigue and fibre types

- **Relaxation:** Ca++ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic cisternae. The active sites on actin are masked again, and the Z lines return to their original position.
- The reaction time of fibres differs between muscles.
- **Fatigue:** repeated activation leads to **lactic acid** build-up, from **anaerobic breakdown of glycogen**.
- **Myoglobin:** a red, oxygen-storing pigment in muscle.
- **Red fibres:** much myoglobin, so reddish. Plenty of mitochondria use the stored oxygen to make ATP. Also called **aerobic muscles**.
- **White fibres:** very little myoglobin, so pale. Few mitochondria but **a lot of sarcoplasmic reticulum**. They depend on **anaerobic** processes for energy.

## 10. Skeletal system

- A framework of **bones and a few cartilages**. Both are specialised connective tissues.
- **Bone:** very hard matrix, because of **calcium salts**.
- **Cartilage:** slightly pliable matrix, because of **chondroitin salts**.
- The human skeleton has **206 bones**, in two divisions:
  - **Axial skeleton: 80 bones** along the main axis: skull, vertebral column, sternum and ribs.
  - **Appendicular skeleton:** bones of the limbs and their girdles.

## 11. Skull

- **22 bones** in two sets:
  - **Cranial: 8.** They form the cranium, the hard protective cover of the brain.
  - **Facial: 14.** They form the front part of the skull.
- **Hyoid:** a single U-shaped bone at the base of the buccal cavity.
- **Ear ossicles:** three tiny bones in each middle ear: **malleus, incus and stapes**.
- The skull joins the top of the vertebral column by **two occipital condyles**, so it is **dicondylic**.

## 12. Vertebral column

- **26 vertebrae**, placed dorsally, from the base of the skull. The main framework of the trunk.
- Each vertebra has a central hollow, the **neural canal**, for the spinal cord.
- The **first vertebra is the atlas**; it joins the occipital condyles.
- Regions: **cervical 7, thoracic 12, lumbar 5, sacral 1 (fused), coccygeal 1 (fused)**.
- Seven cervical vertebrae are found in almost all mammals, including humans.
- Jobs: protects the spinal cord, supports the head, and gives attachment to the ribs and back muscles.

## 13. Sternum and ribs

- **Sternum:** a flat bone on the ventral midline of the thorax.
- **12 pairs of ribs.** Each rib is a thin flat bone, joined dorsally to the vertebral column and (most of them) ventrally to the sternum.
- Each rib has **two articulation surfaces** at its dorsal end: it is **bicephalic**.
- **True ribs (pairs 1–7):** joined ventrally to the sternum by **hyaline cartilage**.
- **Vertebrochondral (false) ribs (pairs 8, 9, 10):** do not join the sternum directly; they join the **seventh rib** with hyaline cartilage.
- **Floating ribs (pairs 11, 12):** not connected ventrally.
- **Rib cage** = thoracic vertebrae + ribs + sternum.

## 14. Limbs

Each limb has **30 bones**.

- **Fore limb (hand):** humerus, radius and ulna, **carpals 8** (wrist), **metacarpals 5** (palm), **phalanges 14** (digits).
- **Hind limb (leg):** **femur** (thigh bone, the **longest bone**), tibia and fibula, **tarsals 7** (ankle), **metatarsals 5**, **phalanges 14**.
- **Patella:** a cup-shaped bone covering the knee ventrally (the knee cap). It is counted in the leg's 30.

## 15. Girdles

The girdles join the limbs to the axial skeleton. Each girdle has two halves.

- **Pectoral girdle** (for the upper limb). Each half = **clavicle + scapula**.
  - **Scapula:** a large triangular flat bone on the dorsal side of the thorax, **between the 2nd and 7th ribs**. Its body has a raised ridge, the **spine**, which ends as a flat, expanded process, the **acromion**. The **clavicle joins the acromion**.
  - Below the acromion is a depression, the **glenoid cavity**, which takes the head of the humerus to form the **shoulder joint**.
  - **Clavicle:** a long slender bone with **two curvatures**; the **collar bone**.
- **Pelvic girdle** (for the lower limb): **two coxal bones**.
  - Each coxal bone is three fused bones: **ilium, ischium and pubis**.
  - Where they fuse is a cavity, the **acetabulum**, which the thigh bone (femur) joins.
  - The two halves meet ventrally at the **pubic symphysis**, which contains **fibrous cartilage**.

## 16. Joints

- **Joints** are points of contact between bones, or between bones and cartilages.
- Muscle force moves the body through joints; the joint acts as a **fulcrum**.
- Three structural types:
  - **Fibrous joints:** **no movement**. Flat skull bones fuse end to end by dense fibrous connective tissue, as **sutures**, to form the cranium.
  - **Cartilaginous joints:** bones joined by cartilage; **limited movement**. Example: between adjacent **vertebrae**.
  - **Synovial joints:** a fluid-filled **synovial cavity** between the two bones; **considerable movement**. They help in locomotion.

| Synovial joint | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Ball and socket | Humerus and pectoral girdle |
| Hinge | Knee joint |
| Pivot | Atlas and axis |
| Gliding | Between the carpals |
| Saddle | Carpal and metacarpal of the thumb |

## 17. Disorders of muscles and bones

- **Myasthenia gravis:** **autoimmune**; affects the **neuromuscular junction**, causing fatigue, weakening and paralysis of skeletal muscle.
- **Muscular dystrophy:** progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle, mostly due to a **genetic** disorder.
- **Tetany:** rapid spasms (wild contractions) in muscle due to **low Ca++** in body fluid.
- **Arthritis:** inflammation of joints.
- **Osteoporosis:** age-related; **decreased bone mass** and more fractures. A common cause is **decreased estrogen**.
- **Gout:** inflammation of joints due to accumulation of **uric acid crystals**.
