## 1. What respiration is

- All energy for life processes comes from **oxidation of food**. Ultimately, all food that is respired comes from **photosynthesis**.
- Only cells with chloroplasts photosynthesise, so food must be translocated to all non-green parts.
- **Cellular respiration:** breaking the **C–C bonds** of complex compounds by oxidation inside the cell, releasing a lot of energy.
- **Respiratory substrates:** the compounds oxidised. Usually **carbohydrates**. Proteins, fats and even organic acids are used in some plants under some conditions.
- Photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts. Breakdown of food happens in the **cytoplasm** and the **mitochondria** (mitochondria only in eukaryotes).
- The energy is released **step by step**, by enzyme-controlled reactions, and trapped as **ATP**. The cell cannot use the energy of oxidation directly; it uses ATP. So ATP is the **energy currency** of the cell.
- The carbon skeletons made during respiration are used as **precursors** to build other molecules.

## 2. Do plants breathe?

Plants need $\ce{O2}$ and give out $\ce{CO2}$, but they have **no special respiratory organs**. Gases move in and out by diffusion through **stomata** and **lenticels**.

Why they manage without respiratory organs:
1. **Each part takes care of its own gas exchange.** Very little gas moves from one part to another.
2. **Low demand.** Roots, stems and leaves respire much more slowly than animals. Large volumes of gas are exchanged only during photosynthesis, and then $\ce{O2}$ is released inside the cell itself.
3. **Short distances.** Every living cell is close to the surface. In woody stems the living cells are in thin layers inside and beneath the bark (with lenticels); the inner cells are dead and only give support.

Loosely packed **parenchyma** in leaves, stems and roots forms a connected network of **air spaces**.

## 3. Burning versus respiration

- Complete combustion of glucose gives $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$, and most of the energy comes out as **heat**:

$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2} \\ & \ce{-> 6CO2 + 6H2O} \end{aligned}$$

- The cell oxidises glucose in **many small steps**. Some steps release just enough energy to be coupled to **ATP synthesis**. That is the "story of respiration".
- The first cells probably lived without oxygen. Some present-day organisms are **facultative** anaerobes and some **obligate** anaerobes. All living organisms keep the enzymes to **partly oxidise glucose without $\ce{O2}$**: this is **glycolysis**.

## 4. Glycolysis

- Name: Greek *glycos* (sugar) + *lysis* (splitting).
- Scheme given by **Gustav Embden, Otto Meyerhof and J. Parnas**, so it is called the **EMP pathway**.
- Happens in the **cytoplasm** of **all living organisms**. In anaerobic organisms it is the **only** process of respiration.
- Glucose is **partly oxidised** to **2 molecules of pyruvic acid**, by a chain of **10 reactions**, each with its own enzyme.
- In plants the glucose comes from **sucrose** (the end product of photosynthesis) or from stored carbohydrate. **Invertase** splits sucrose into glucose and fructose. Both are phosphorylated by **hexokinase** and enter glycolysis.

Main steps:
1. Glucose → glucose-6-phosphate (**ATP used**).
2. Glucose-6-phosphate isomerises to fructose-6-phosphate.
3. Fructose-6-phosphate → fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (**ATP used**).
4. Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate splits into **dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP)** and **3-phosphoglyceraldehyde (PGAL)**.
5. PGAL → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (**BPGA**). Two hydrogen atoms (redox equivalents) go to $\ce{NAD+}$, forming **$\ce{NADH + H+}$**. PGAL is oxidised and picks up inorganic phosphate.
6. BPGA → 3-phosphoglyceric acid (**PGA**): **ATP made**.
7. PGA → 2-phosphoglycerate → phosphoenolpyruvate (**PEP**) (water is removed).
8. PEP → **pyruvic acid**: **ATP made**.

:::remember
Per glucose: **2 ATP used, 4 ATP made, net 2 ATP**, plus **2 NADH + H⁺** and **2 pyruvic acid**. Pyruvic acid is the key product of glycolysis.
:::

## 5. Three fates of pyruvic acid

What happens next depends on the cell's need:
1. **Lactic acid fermentation** (no $\ce{O2}$)
2. **Alcoholic fermentation** (no $\ce{O2}$)
3. **Aerobic respiration** (needs $\ce{O2}$): complete oxidation to $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ through Krebs' cycle.

Fermentation happens under anaerobic conditions in many **prokaryotes**, **unicellular eukaryotes** and **germinating seeds**.

## 6. Fermentation

- **Alcoholic fermentation** (e.g. yeast): pyruvic acid → **ethanol + $\ce{CO2}$**. Enzymes: **pyruvic acid decarboxylase** and **alcohol dehydrogenase**.
- **Lactic acid fermentation**: some bacteria make lactic acid from pyruvic acid. **Animal muscle** does the same during exercise when $\ce{O2}$ is short. Enzyme: **lactate dehydrogenase**.
- In both, the reducing agent is **$\ce{NADH + H+}$**, which is **re-oxidised to $\ce{NAD+}$**. This regenerated $\ce{NAD+}$ lets glycolysis go on.
- Very little energy is released: **less than 7%** of the energy in glucose, and not all of it is trapped as ATP.
- Net gain: **2 ATP per glucose** (from glycolysis only).
- The products are harmful: acid or alcohol. **Yeasts poison themselves to death** when alcohol reaches about **13%**, so naturally fermented drinks stay below about 13% alcohol.

## 7. Aerobic respiration: the plan

Happens in the **mitochondria** of eukaryotes and needs $\ce{O2}$. Pyruvate is carried from the cytoplasm into the mitochondria. Two crucial events:
1. **Complete oxidation of pyruvate** by step-by-step removal of all its hydrogen atoms, leaving **3 $\ce{CO2}$**. Site: **matrix**.
2. **Passing the electrons** (removed with the hydrogen) to molecular $\ce{O2}$, with ATP being made. Site: **inner mitochondrial membrane**.

## 8. Pyruvate to acetyl CoA

- In the matrix, pyruvate undergoes **oxidative decarboxylation**, catalysed by **pyruvate (pyruvic) dehydrogenase**. It needs several coenzymes, including **$\ce{NAD+}$ and coenzyme A**, and $\ce{Mg^2+}$.
- Pyruvic acid + CoA + $\ce{NAD+}$ → acetyl CoA + $\ce{CO2}$ + $\ce{NADH + H+}$.
- One glucose gives 2 pyruvic acids, so this step gives **2 NADH** per glucose.
- Acetyl CoA then enters the **tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle**, named **Krebs' cycle** after **Hans Krebs**, who first worked it out. It is also called the **citric acid cycle**.

## 9. Krebs' cycle (TCA cycle)

1. **Acetyl group (2C) + oxaloacetic acid (OAA, 4C) + water → citric acid (6C)**. Enzyme: **citrate synthase**. CoA is released.
2. Citrate is **isomerised to isocitrate**.
3. **Two decarboxylations in a row** give **α-ketoglutaric acid (5C)** and then **succinyl CoA (4C)**.
4. **Succinyl CoA → succinic acid** makes **one GTP**. This is **substrate-level phosphorylation**. In a coupled reaction GTP → GDP and **ADP → ATP**.
5. Succinic acid is oxidised (via malic acid) back to **OAA**, so the cycle can go on.

Per turn: **3 NAD⁺ → NADH + H⁺** and **1 FAD⁺ → FADH₂** (NCERT writes FAD⁺).

The cycle needs a steady supply of **OAA** (its first member) and regeneration of **$\ce{NAD+}$ and FAD** from NADH and $\ce{FADH2}$.

:::remember
In the mitochondria (link step + Krebs' cycle), one glucose has given $\ce{CO2}$, **8 NADH + H⁺**, **2 FADH₂** and only **2 ATP** (TCA cycle). Add the **2 NADH** from glycolysis for **10 NADH** in all. $\ce{O2}$ has not been used yet. The big ATP harvest comes next, in the ETS.
:::

## 10. Electron transport system (ETS)

The **ETS** is the chain of carriers that passes electrons from NADH and $\ce{FADH2}$ to $\ce{O2}$. It is in the **inner mitochondrial membrane**.

- **Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase)** oxidises the NADH made in the matrix and passes electrons to **ubiquinone** (inside the inner membrane).
- **Complex II** passes reducing equivalents from **$\ce{FADH2}$** (made when succinate is oxidised in Krebs' cycle) to ubiquinone. NCERT's figure names it **succinate dehydrogenase**.
- Reduced ubiquinone (**ubiquinol**) passes electrons to **cytochrome c** through **complex III (cytochrome $bc_1$)**.
- **Cytochrome c** is a small protein on the **outer surface of the inner membrane**. It is a **mobile carrier** between complex III and IV.
- **Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase)** contains **cytochromes $a$ and $a_3$** and **two copper centres**. It passes electrons to $\ce{O2}$, which forms water.
- As electrons pass from I to IV, the flow is coupled to **ATP synthase (complex V)**, which makes ATP from ADP and $\text{P}_i$.
- ATP made depends on the electron donor: **1 NADH → 3 ATP**, **1 $\ce{FADH2}$ → 2 ATP**.

**Role of oxygen.** It acts only at the **last step**, but it is vital: it removes hydrogen from the system and so drives the whole process. Oxygen is the **final hydrogen acceptor** (the ultimate electron acceptor) and is reduced to water.

## 11. Oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthase

- In **photophosphorylation**, **light** energy makes the proton gradient. In respiration, the energy of **oxidation-reduction** makes it. So ATP synthesis here is called **oxidative phosphorylation**.
- The mechanism is the **chemiosmotic hypothesis** (studied in Photosynthesis in Higher Plants).
- **ATP synthase (complex V)** has two parts:
  - **$F_1$:** the headpiece. A **peripheral** membrane protein complex. It has the **site where ATP is made** from ADP and $\text{P}_i$.
  - **$F_0$:** an **integral** membrane protein complex. It forms the **channel** through which protons cross the inner membrane.
- Protons passing through $F_0$ drive the catalytic site of $F_1$. For **each ATP, 4 H⁺** pass from the **intermembrane space to the matrix**, down the electrochemical proton gradient.

## 12. The respiratory balance sheet

The net ATP gain per glucose can be worked out only as a **theoretical exercise**, on these assumptions:
- The pathway runs in a **sequential, orderly** way: glycolysis, then TCA cycle, then ETS, each substrate forming the next.
- The **NADH made in glycolysis** is moved into the mitochondria and undergoes oxidative phosphorylation.
- **None of the intermediates** is used to make any other compound.
- **Only glucose** is respired; no other substrate enters at any stage.

In a living cell none of this is really true: all pathways run **at the same time**, substrates enter and leave as needed, ATP is used as needed, and enzyme rates are controlled in many ways. Still, on these assumptions there is a **net gain of 38 ATP** per glucose in aerobic respiration.

| Fermentation | Aerobic respiration |
| --- | --- |
| Partial breakdown of glucose | Complete breakdown to $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ |
| Net 2 ATP per glucose | Many more ATP (38 by NCERT's count) |
| NADH oxidised to $\ce{NAD+}$ rather **slowly** | NADH oxidised **very vigorously** |
| No $\ce{O2}$; cytoplasm only | Needs $\ce{O2}$; mitochondria in eukaryotes |

## 13. Amphibolic pathway

- **Glucose** is the favoured substrate. Other carbohydrates are usually first converted into glucose.
- Other substrates enter the pathway **at later points**, not at the first step:

| Substrate | Broken into | Enters as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fats | Glycerol | PGAL |
| Fats | Fatty acids | Acetyl CoA |
| Proteins | Amino acids (deaminated) | Pyruvate, acetyl CoA or a Krebs' cycle compound |

- The **same compounds** are **withdrawn** from the pathway to build these substances. For example, when the cell needs fatty acids, **acetyl CoA** is taken out of the respiratory pathway. Respiratory intermediates also link the breakdown and synthesis of proteins.
- Breaking down is **catabolism**; building up is **anabolism**. The respiratory pathway does both, so it is better called an **amphibolic pathway**, not just a catabolic one.

## 14. Respiratory quotient (RQ)

- **RQ** (respiratory ratio) = volume of $\ce{CO2}$ evolved ÷ volume of $\ce{O2}$ consumed.
- It depends on the **respiratory substrate**.
- **Carbohydrates**, completely oxidised: equal $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{O2}$, so **RQ = 1**.
- **Fats**: RQ **less than 1**. Tripalmitin: $102\,\ce{CO2} / 145\,\ce{O2} = 0.7$.
- **Proteins**: about **0.9**.
- Living organisms usually respire a **mixture** of substrates. Pure proteins or fats are never the only substrates.
