## 1. What has to be excreted

- Animals build up ammonia, urea, uric acid, $\text{CO}_2$, water and ions ($\text{Na}^+$, $\text{K}^+$, $\text{Cl}^-$, phosphate, sulphate), from metabolism or excess intake. These must be removed fully or partly.
- The three main **nitrogenous wastes** are **ammonia, urea and uric acid**. Which one an animal makes depends mainly on its **habitat** (how much water it has).

## 2. Three modes of excretion

- **Ammonotelism** (excreting ammonia): many bony fishes, aquatic amphibians, aquatic insects.
  - Ammonia is the **most toxic** and needs a **lot of water**.
  - It dissolves easily, so it leaves by **diffusion** across the body surface, or through the **gills** (in fish), as ammonium ions.
  - The kidneys play **no significant role** in removing it.
- **Ureotelism** (excreting urea): mammals, many terrestrial amphibians, marine fishes.
  - Ammonia made in metabolism is turned into **urea in the liver**, released into the blood, and filtered out by the kidneys.
  - Some animals keep some urea in the **kidney matrix** to keep a needed osmolarity.
- **Uricotelism** (excreting uric acid): reptiles, birds, land snails, insects.
  - Uric acid is the **least toxic**. It leaves as a **pellet or paste** with the least water loss.
- Life on land needed water saving, so land animals make the less toxic urea and uric acid.

## 3. Excretory structures across the animal kingdom

Most invertebrates have simple tubular structures. Vertebrates have complex tubular organs called **kidneys**.

- **Protonephridia (flame cells):** Platyhelminthes (flatworms, e.g. *Planaria*), rotifers, some annelids and the cephalochordate *Amphioxus*. Their main job is **osmoregulation** (ionic and fluid volume regulation).
- **Nephridia:** earthworms and other annelids. They remove nitrogenous wastes and keep the fluid and ionic balance.
- **Malpighian tubules:** most insects, including cockroaches. They remove nitrogenous wastes and help osmoregulation.
- **Antennal glands (green glands):** crustaceans like prawns.

## 4. Human excretory system

- A pair of **kidneys**, a pair of **ureters**, a **urinary bladder** and a **urethra**.
- Kidneys: reddish brown, bean-shaped, between the **last thoracic and third lumbar** vertebra, close to the dorsal inner wall of the abdominal cavity.
- Each adult kidney: 10–12 cm long, 5–7 cm wide, 2–3 cm thick, 120–170 g.

## 5. Inside the kidney

- **Hilum:** a notch at the centre of the inner concave side. The ureter, blood vessels and nerves enter here.
- **Renal pelvis:** a broad funnel-shaped space inside the hilum, with projections called **calyces** (singular: calyx).
- Outside is a tough **capsule**. Inside are two zones: an outer **cortex** and an inner **medulla**.
- The medulla forms a few conical **medullary pyramids** that project into the calyces.
- The cortex extends between the pyramids as renal columns called **columns of Bertini**.

## 6. The nephron

Each kidney has nearly **one million** nephrons, the **functional units**. Each nephron has two parts: the **glomerulus** and the **renal tubule**.

- **Glomerulus:** a tuft of capillaries formed by the **afferent arteriole** (a fine branch of the renal artery). Blood leaves it by the **efferent arteriole**.
- **Bowman's capsule:** a double-walled cup at the start of the tubule. It encloses the glomerulus.
- Glomerulus + Bowman's capsule = **Malpighian body** (renal corpuscle).
- Then, in order: **PCT** (proximal convoluted tubule, highly coiled) → hairpin-shaped **Henle's loop** (descending and ascending limb) → **DCT** (distal convoluted tubule, highly coiled).
- The DCTs of many nephrons open into a straight **collecting duct**. Many collecting ducts join and open into the renal pelvis through the medullary pyramids, in the calyces.

**Where each part lies:** the Malpighian corpuscle, PCT and DCT are in the **cortex**. The loop of Henle dips into the **medulla**.

| Type | Loop of Henle | How common |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cortical nephron | Short, dips only a little into the medulla | Majority |
| Juxta medullary nephron | Very long, runs deep into the medulla | Some |

**Blood vessels around the tubule**
- The efferent arteriole forms a fine capillary network around the tubule: the **peritubular capillaries**.
- A minute vessel of this network runs parallel to Henle's loop as a U-shaped **vasa recta**.
- The vasa recta is **absent or highly reduced** in cortical nephrons.

## 7. Urine formation: three steps

Glomerular filtration, reabsorption and secretion take place in different parts of the nephron.

### Glomerular filtration

- The kidneys filter **1100–1200 mL of blood per minute**, roughly 1/5 of the blood pumped out by each ventricle per minute.
- The force is the **glomerular capillary blood pressure**.
- Blood is filtered through 3 layers: the **endothelium** of the glomerular blood vessels, the **epithelium of Bowman's capsule**, and a **basement membrane** between them.
- The capsule's epithelial cells, **podocytes**, are arranged so as to leave minute spaces: **filtration slits** (slit pores).
- Almost everything in plasma except the **proteins** passes into the capsule. So this is **ultrafiltration**. It is non-selective.

:::remember
**GFR** (glomerular filtration rate) is the amount of filtrate formed by the kidneys per minute. In a healthy person it is about **125 mL/min**, that is **180 litres per day**.
:::

### The JGA keeps GFR steady

- The **juxta glomerular apparatus (JGA)** is a special sensitive region formed by cellular modifications in the **DCT** and the **afferent arteriole** where they touch.
- A fall in GFR makes the JG cells release **renin**, which brings the glomerular blood flow, and so the GFR, back to normal.

### Reabsorption

- Filtrate 180 L/day vs urine about 1.5 L/day: nearly **99%** of the filtrate must be reabsorbed by the tubules.
- **Active:** glucose, amino acids, $\text{Na}^+$, etc.
- **Passive:** nitrogenous wastes. Water is also reabsorbed passively in the initial segments.

### Secretion

- Tubular cells secrete $\text{H}^+$, $\text{K}^+$ and **ammonia** into the filtrate.
- This keeps the **ionic and acid–base balance** of body fluids.

## 8. Work of each part of the tubule

- **PCT:** lined by **simple cuboidal brush border epithelium**, which increases the surface for reabsorption.
  - Reabsorbs nearly all essential nutrients and **70–80%** of electrolytes and water.
  - Keeps pH and ionic balance: secretes $\text{H}^+$ and ammonia, absorbs $\text{HCO}_3^-$.
- **Henle's loop:**
  - **Descending limb:** permeable to water, almost impermeable to electrolytes. The filtrate gets **more concentrated** as it moves down.
  - **Ascending limb:** impermeable to water, moves electrolytes out actively or passively. The filtrate gets **diluted** as it moves up.
  - Reabsorption is **minimum** in the ascending limb, but the loop keeps the medullary interstitial fluid at **high osmolarity**.
- **DCT:** **conditional** reabsorption of $\text{Na}^+$ and water. Also reabsorbs $\text{HCO}_3^-$ and secretes $\text{H}^+$, $\text{K}^+$ and $\text{NH}_3$, keeping pH and the sodium–potassium balance of blood.
- **Collecting duct:** runs from the cortex to the inner medulla.
  - Can reabsorb **large amounts of water**, making concentrated urine.
  - Lets small amounts of **urea** pass into the medullary interstitium, to keep up the osmolarity.
  - Secretes $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{K}^+$ for pH and ionic balance.

## 9. Counter current mechanism: making concentrated urine

- Mammals make concentrated urine using **Henle's loop and the vasa recta**.
- Filtrate flows in **opposite directions** in the two limbs of Henle's loop: a **counter current**. Blood in the two limbs of the vasa recta also flows in a counter current.
- Their closeness and counter current keep the medullary interstitium more concentrated deeper in: from **300 mOsmol/L in the cortex to about 1200 mOsmol/L in the inner medulla**.
- The gradient is made mainly by **NaCl and urea**:
  - **NaCl:** moved out by the ascending limb of Henle's loop, exchanged with the descending limb of the vasa recta, and returned to the interstitium by the ascending limb of the vasa recta.
  - **Urea:** small amounts enter the **thin ascending limb** of Henle's loop and are carried back to the interstitium by the **collecting tubule**.
- This gradient lets water pass easily out of the collecting tubule, which concentrates the urine.
- Human kidneys can make urine nearly **4 times** as concentrated as the initial filtrate.

## 10. Regulation of kidney function

The kidneys are controlled by hormonal **feedback** involving the **hypothalamus**, the **JGA** and, to some extent, the **heart**.

### ADH (vasopressin)

- **Osmoreceptors** respond to changes in blood volume, body fluid volume and ion concentration.
- Too much fluid loss → osmoreceptors stimulate the hypothalamus → **ADH** is released from the **neurohypophysis**.
- ADH increases **water reabsorption** from the latter parts of the tubule, so it **prevents diuresis** (large urine output).
- More body fluid switches the osmoreceptors off and ADH release falls. This completes the feedback.
- ADH also **constricts blood vessels**. Blood pressure rises, which raises glomerular blood flow and GFR.
- If ADH synthesis or release is impaired, the kidney cannot save water, causing water loss and dehydration: **diabetes insipidus** (NCERT Chapter 19; listed under this chapter in the NEET syllabus).

### Renin–angiotensin mechanism

A fall in glomerular blood flow, glomerular blood pressure or GFR sets it off:

1. JG cells release **renin**.
2. Renin converts **angiotensinogen** (in blood) to **angiotensin I**, and then it becomes **angiotensin II**.
3. Angiotensin II is a powerful **vasoconstrictor**: it raises glomerular blood pressure and GFR.
4. Angiotensin II also makes the **adrenal cortex** release **aldosterone**.
5. Aldosterone causes reabsorption of $\text{Na}^+$ and water from the distal parts of the tubule. Blood pressure and GFR rise too.

### ANF (atrial natriuretic factor)

- More blood flow to the **atria** of the heart → **ANF** is released.
- ANF causes **vasodilation** and lowers blood pressure.
- So the ANF mechanism acts as a **check on the renin–angiotensin** mechanism.

## 11. Micturition

- Urine is stored in the **urinary bladder** until the **CNS** gives a voluntary signal.
- As the bladder fills and stretches, **stretch receptors** in its wall send signals to the CNS.
- The CNS sends motor messages: the **smooth muscles of the bladder contract** and the **urethral sphincter relaxes** at the same time, and urine is released.
- This release is **micturition**; its neural mechanism is the **micturition reflex**.

**Urine facts (adult):** 1–1.5 L per day; light yellow, watery, characteristic odour; slightly acidic (**pH 6.0**); **25–30 g urea** per day.

- **Glycosuria** (glucose in urine) and **ketonuria** (ketone bodies in urine) point to **diabetes mellitus**.

## 12. Other organs that help in excretion

- **Lungs:** remove large amounts of $\text{CO}_2$ (about **200 mL per minute**) and a lot of water every day.
- **Liver** (the largest gland): its **bile** carries bilirubin, biliverdin, cholesterol, degraded steroid hormones, vitamins and drugs. Most of these leave with the digestive wastes.
- **Skin:**
  - **Sweat glands:** sweat is watery, with NaCl, small amounts of urea, lactic acid, etc. Its main job is **cooling**; excretion is a side job.
  - **Sebaceous glands:** sebum removes sterols, hydrocarbons and waxes, and gives the skin a protective oily cover.
- **Saliva:** can remove small amounts of nitrogenous wastes.

## 13. Disorders of the excretory system

- **Uremia:** urea builds up in the blood when the kidneys malfunction; highly harmful, may lead to kidney failure.
- **Haemodialysis** (artificial kidney) removes urea:
  1. Blood is drained from a convenient **artery**, and an anticoagulant, **heparin**, is added.
  2. It is pumped into a dialysing unit with a coiled **cellophane tube** in **dialysing fluid**. The fluid is like plasma **except it has no nitrogenous wastes**.
  3. Molecules cross the porous cellophane down their **concentration gradient**, so nitrogenous wastes leave the blood.
  4. The cleared blood gets **anti-heparin** and is pumped back through a **vein**.
- **Kidney transplantation:** the ultimate correction for acute renal (kidney) failure. The donor is preferably a **close relative**, to lower the chance of rejection by the host's immune system.
- **Renal calculi:** stones, insoluble masses of crystallised salts (oxalates, etc.) formed in the kidney.
- **Glomerulonephritis:** inflammation of the glomeruli of the kidney.
