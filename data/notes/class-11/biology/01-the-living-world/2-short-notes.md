## 0. What is living? (exam extra: in the NEET syllabus, not in the current NCERT book)

NCERT asks "what is living?" but no longer answers it. The older NCERT edition did, and NEET 2026 still lists the topic. The older NCERT's answer:

| Feature | Defining property of life? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth | No | Mountains, boulders and sand mounds also grow, by material added on the surface. |
| Reproduction | No | Mules, sterile worker bees and infertile couples are alive but do not reproduce. |
| Metabolism | **Yes** | Every living organism shows it. No non-living object does. |
| Cellular organisation | **Yes** | Every living organism is made of cells. A metabolic reaction alone, in a test tube, is not life. |
| Consciousness | **Yes** | Every organism senses its surroundings and responds to stimuli. |

- **Growth:** living things grow from **inside**. Increase in mass and increase in number of individuals are the twin characteristics of growth. In plants, growth by cell division goes on all their life; in animals it is seen only up to a certain age. In unicellular organisms, growth is by cell division.
- **Reproduction:** in unicellular organisms, reproduction and growth are the same thing (both are an increase in the number of cells). Other forms: spores (fungi), budding (yeast, *Hydra*), fragmentation (filamentous algae, fungi, protonema of mosses), regeneration (*Planaria*).
- **Metabolism:** the sum of all chemical reactions in the body. Reactions done outside the body in a cell-free system (*in vitro*) are not living things, but they are living reactions.
- **Consciousness:** plants respond to light, water, temperature, other organisms and pollutants. Human beings are the only organisms aware of themselves (**self-consciousness**).
- In short, living organisms are **self-replicating, evolving and self-regulating interactive systems** that can respond to external stimuli.

## 1. Diversity and biodiversity

- Each different kind of plant, animal or other organism represents a **species**.
- Known and described species: **1.7–1.8 million**. This number is **biodiversity**: the number and types of organisms on earth.
- New organisms are still being found, in new areas and old ones.
- Local names vary from place to place, even within one country. So we need one standard name for each organism, used all over the world.

## 2. Identification, nomenclature, classification

- **Identification:** describing an organism correctly, so that we know which organism a name is attached to.
- **Nomenclature:** standardising the naming of organisms, so that one organism has the same name everywhere. Naming is possible only after correct identification.
- **Classification:** grouping things into convenient categories based on easily observable characters. "Dogs", "mammals", "wheat", "plants" are all such categories.
- These categories are called **taxa** (singular: **taxon**). Taxa can be at very different levels: a dog is a mammal and mammals are animals, so "dogs", "mammals" and "animals" are taxa at three levels.

## 3. Codes and binomial nomenclature

- **ICBN** (International Code for Botanical Nomenclature): rules for naming **plants**.
- **ICZN** (International Code of Zoological Nomenclature): rules for naming **animals**.
- Scientific names make sure that each organism has **only one name**, that people anywhere can reach the same name from its description, and that the name is not used for any other known organism.
- **Binomial nomenclature** was given by **Carolus Linnaeus**. Each name has two parts: the **generic name** and the **specific epithet**.

:::example
*Mangifera indica* (mango): *Mangifera* is the genus and *indica* is the specific epithet.

Written with the author: *Mangifera indica* Linn. ("Linn." = Linnaeus, who first described it).
:::

Rules NCERT lists:
1. Names are generally in **Latin** (or Latinised), and printed in **italics**.
2. The first word is the **genus**; the second is the **specific epithet**.
3. When handwritten, the two words are **underlined separately**.
4. The genus starts with a **capital** letter; the specific epithet with a **small** letter.
- The **author's name** comes at the end, after the specific epithet, in **short form**.

## 4. Taxonomy and systematics

- **Taxonomy:** classifying living organisms into different taxa based on their characteristics.
- Basis of modern taxonomy: external and internal structure, cell structure, development process and ecological information.
- The four basic processes of taxonomy: **characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature**.
- The **earliest classifications** were based on the **uses** of organisms (for food, clothing and shelter).
- **Systematics:** the study of the diversity of organisms and the **relationships among them**. From the Latin *systema* (systematic arrangement). Linnaeus called his book ***Systema Naturae***.
- The scope of systematics was later enlarged to include identification, nomenclature and classification. Systematics takes **evolutionary relationships** into account.
- Taxonomic studies are useful in agriculture, forestry, industry and in knowing our bio-resources.

| | Taxonomy | Systematics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main job | Classify into taxa by characters | Study diversity and relationships |
| Key point | Based on characteristics | Takes evolutionary relationships into account |

## 5. Taxonomic categories and hierarchy

- Classification is **not a single step**. It is a hierarchy of steps; each step is a **rank** or **category**.
- A category that is part of the taxonomic arrangement is a **taxonomic category**. All categories together make the **taxonomic hierarchy**.
- Each category is a **unit of classification**, and is called a **taxon**.
- Example: insects share features like **three pairs of jointed legs**, so they form a real, recognisable group with a rank.
- Taxonomic groups are **distinct biological entities**, not just morphological aggregates.
- The common categories: **kingdom, phylum (division for plants), class, order, family, genus, species**.
- To place an organism, you need to know its **characters**: how it is similar to, and different from, other organisms.

## 6. The categories one by one

**Species**
- A group of individuals with **fundamental similarities**. One species should be distinguishable from closely related species by **distinct morphological differences**.
- The **lowest** category for all organisms.
- Examples of specific epithets: *indica*, *tuberosum* (potato), *leo* (lion). Human beings: *Homo sapiens*.
- One genus can have one or more species: *Panthera* has *leo* and *tigris*; *Solanum* has *tuberosum*, *nigrum* and *melongena*.
- (Exam extra: the NEET syllabus lists "concept of species". Ernst Mayr, named in NCERT, pioneered the accepted **biological species** definition: groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups. NCERT's own text defines species by fundamental and morphological similarities.)

**Genus**
- A group of **related species** with more characters in common than with species of other genera.
- Potato and brinjal are different species of *Solanum*. Lion (*P. leo*), leopard (*P. pardus*) and tiger (*P. tigris*) are species of *Panthera*. *Panthera* differs from *Felis* (cats).

**Family**
- A group of **related genera**, with fewer similarities than genus and species.
- Plant families are characterised by both **vegetative and reproductive** features.
- *Solanum*, *Petunia* and *Datura* are in **Solanaceae**. *Panthera* and *Felis* are in **Felidae**. Cat and dog are in different families: **Felidae** and **Canidae**.

**Order**
- An assemblage of **families** that share a few similar characters. Order and higher categories are identified based on **aggregates of characters**.
- Convolvulaceae and Solanaceae are in the order **Polymoniales**, mainly on **floral** characters. (This is NCERT's spelling; most botany books write **Polemoniales**.)
- The animal order **Carnivora** includes Felidae and Canidae.

**Class**
- A group of related **orders**.
- Order **Primata** (monkey, gorilla, gibbon) and order **Carnivora** (tiger, cat, dog) are both in class **Mammalia**. (NCERT writes "Primata"; the standard name is **Primates**.)

**Phylum (Division in plants)**
- A group of related **classes**.
- Fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals are placed in phylum **Chordata**, based on the **notochord** and the **dorsal hollow neural system**.
- In plants, classes with a few similar characters form a **Division**.

**Kingdom**
- The **highest** category. All animals of various phyla form **Kingdom Animalia**. All plants of various divisions form **Kingdom Plantae**.

## 7. Trends in the hierarchy

- From species up to kingdom, the number of **common characteristics decreases**.
- **Lower** the taxon, **more** characters its members share.
- **Higher** the category, **harder** it is to work out its relationship with other taxa at the same level. So classification gets more complex as you go up.
- Taxonomists have also made **sub-categories** in the hierarchy, for a more exact placement of taxa.

## 9. Ernst Mayr

- NCERT prints his dates as **1904–2004**. Most other sources give his death as February 2005, aged 100; use NCERT's dates in the exam.

- Harvard evolutionary biologist, called **"the Darwin of the 20th century"**.
- Made the origin of species diversity the central question of evolutionary biology.
- Pioneered the currently accepted definition of a **biological species**.
- Won the "triple crown" of biology: Balzan Prize (1983), International Prize for Biology (1994), Crafoord Prize (1999).
