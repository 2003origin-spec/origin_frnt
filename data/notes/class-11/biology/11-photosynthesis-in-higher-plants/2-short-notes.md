## 1. What photosynthesis is

- **Photosynthesis:** a physico-chemical process in which green plants use **light energy** to make organic compounds. Such plants are **autotrophs**; organisms that depend on them are **heterotrophs**.
- It is the **primary source of all food** on earth, and it **releases oxygen** into the air.
- School experiments show that **chlorophyll, light and CO₂** are all needed:
  - A **variegated leaf**, or a leaf partly covered with black paper, makes starch only in its green, lit parts.
  - Half a leaf shut in a tube with **KOH-soaked cotton** (KOH absorbs CO₂) makes no starch; the half in air does.

## 2. Early experiments

| Scientist | Work | Conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Joseph Priestley (1770) | Candle, mouse and mint plant in a bell jar | Plants restore to the air what breathing animals and burning candles remove |
| Jan Ingenhousz | Same set-up in light and dark; bubbles from an aquatic plant | Sunlight is needed; only **green parts** release O₂ |
| Julius von Sachs (1854) | Growing plants make glucose | Made in green parts, stored as **starch**; chlorophyll is in chloroplasts |
| T.W. Engelmann | Prism-split light on the alga *Cladophora* with aerobic bacteria | Bacteria gathered in **blue and red** light: the first **action spectrum** |
| Cornelius van Niel | Purple and green bacteria | O₂ given out by plants comes from **water**, not CO₂ |
| Melvin Calvin | Used radioactive $^{14}$C in algae | First product of CO₂ fixation is **PGA**; worked out the Calvin cycle |

- Priestley also discovered oxygen, in **1774**.
- Van Niel: hydrogen from an oxidisable compound reduces CO₂ to carbohydrate. With H₂S as the donor (purple and green sulphur bacteria), sulphur or sulphate forms, not O₂.
- Calvin worked with J.A. Bassham and won the **Nobel Prize in 1961** for mapping the path of carbon.

## 3. Where photosynthesis happens

- Mainly in green **leaves**, but also in other green parts.
- Leaf **mesophyll cells** have many chloroplasts, which line up along the cell walls to get the best amount of light.
- The chloroplast shows a clear **division of labour**:
  - **Membrane system** (grana and stroma lamellae): traps light and makes **ATP and NADPH**. These are the **light reactions** (photochemical reactions).
  - **Stroma**: enzymes make sugar, which then forms starch. These are the **dark reactions** (carbon reactions).

:::remember
"Dark reaction" does **not** mean it happens in the dark. It is not directly driven by light, but needs ATP and NADPH from the light reaction. When light goes off, it continues for a short time and then stops. So the name is a misnomer.
:::

## 4. Pigments

Paper chromatography of leaf pigments shows **four** pigments:

| Pigment | Colour on the chromatogram |
| --- | --- |
| Chlorophyll a | Bright or blue green |
| Chlorophyll b | Yellow green |
| Xanthophylls | Yellow |
| Carotenoids | Yellow to yellow-orange |

- **Absorption spectrum:** how much light a pigment absorbs at each wavelength. Chlorophyll a absorbs most in the **blue and red** regions.
- **Action spectrum:** the rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength. It is also highest in blue and red. So **chlorophyll a is the chief pigment**.
- The two spectra do not overlap one-to-one: some photosynthesis happens at other wavelengths too.
- **Accessory pigments** (chlorophyll b, xanthophylls, carotenoids):
  - absorb light and pass the energy to chlorophyll a, so a **wider range of wavelengths** can be used;
  - **protect chlorophyll a from photo-oxidation**.

## 5. Light reaction (photochemical phase)

The light reaction includes: light absorption, splitting of water, release of oxygen, and formation of **ATP and NADPH**.

- Pigments are grouped into **light harvesting complexes (LHC)** inside **Photosystem I** and **Photosystem II**.
- Each has hundreds of pigment molecules bound to proteins. All except one chlorophyll a molecule form the **antenna**, which absorbs many wavelengths.
- The single chlorophyll a molecule is the **reaction centre**. It is different in the two photosystems:
  - PS I: **P700** (absorption peak at 700 nm).
  - PS II: **P680** (absorption maximum at 680 nm).

## 6. Electron transport: the Z scheme

1. PS II's reaction centre absorbs 680 nm red light. Its electrons are excited and passed to an **electron acceptor**.
2. They move **downhill** (on the redox potential scale) through an electron transport system of **cytochromes**. Electrons are not used up; they are passed on to PS I.
3. At the same time PS I's reaction centre absorbs 700 nm light. Its electrons are excited and passed to another acceptor with a greater redox potential.
4. They move downhill again to **NADP+**, which is reduced to **NADPH + H+**.

The carriers, placed in order on a redox potential scale, form a "Z" shape: hence the **Z scheme**.

**Splitting of water.** PS II must get back the electrons it lost. They come from splitting water: $\ce{2H2O -> 4H+ + O2 + 4e-}$. The water-splitting complex is linked to PS II, on the **inner side** of the thylakoid membrane, so protons and O₂ are released into the **lumen**.

## 7. Cyclic and non-cyclic photophosphorylation

- **Phosphorylation:** making ATP (in mitochondria and chloroplasts). **Photophosphorylation:** making ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate **in the presence of light**.
- **Non-cyclic:** PS II and PS I work in series, joined by the electron transport chain (the Z scheme). It makes **ATP and NADPH + H+**, and water is split.
- **Cyclic:** only **PS I** works. The excited electron does not go to NADP+; it cycles back to PS I through the electron transport chain. It makes **ATP only**.
  - Likely site: the **stroma lamellae**. Their membranes lack **PS II** and **NADP reductase**. Grana lamellae have both PS I and PS II.
  - It also happens when only light of wavelength **beyond 680 nm** is available.
  - The Calvin cycle uses 3 ATP per 2 NADPH; cyclic flow probably makes up the extra ATP.

## 8. Chemiosmotic hypothesis (how ATP is made)

ATP synthesis is linked to a **proton gradient** across the **thylakoid membrane**. Protons pile up **inside**, in the **lumen** (in respiration, in the mitochondrial intermembrane space).

Three things build the gradient:
1. **Water splitting** happens on the inner side, so its protons stay in the lumen.
2. The **primary electron acceptor** is on the outer (stroma) side. It passes its electron to an **H carrier**, which takes a proton from the **stroma** and releases it into the **lumen**. (Beyond the syllabus, not in NCERT: this H carrier is plastoquinone.)
3. **NADP reductase** is on the stroma side. Reducing NADP+ uses protons from the **stroma**.

Result: fewer protons in the stroma, more in the lumen, and a measurable **fall in pH in the lumen**.

**ATP synthase** has two parts:
- **CF0**: embedded in the thylakoid membrane. It forms a channel for **facilitated diffusion** of protons back to the stroma.
- **CF1**: sticks out on the **stroma side**. The protons' flow causes a conformational change in it, and it makes ATP.

:::remember
Chemiosmosis needs four things: a **membrane**, a **proton pump**, a **proton gradient** and **ATP synthase**.
:::

## 9. Where ATP and NADPH are used: the Calvin cycle

- Of the light reaction products, O₂ diffuses out; **ATP and NADPH** drive sugar synthesis in the stroma (the **biosynthetic phase**).
- First product of CO₂ fixation: **3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA, 3C)** in **C3 plants**; **oxaloacetic acid (OAA, 4C)** in **C4 plants**.
- The CO₂ acceptor is a **5-carbon** ketose sugar, **ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP)**. Scientists first expected a 2-carbon acceptor and spent years looking for one.
- The **Calvin cycle runs in all photosynthetic plants**, C3 or C4. It is cyclic because RuBP is regenerated.

Three stages:
1. **Carboxylation:** CO₂ is fixed onto RuBP to give **2 molecules of 3-PGA**. Enzyme: **RuBP carboxylase**. It can also act as an oxygenase, so its full name is **RuBP carboxylase-oxygenase (RuBisCO)**. This is the most crucial step.
2. **Reduction:** a series of steps that form glucose. Uses **2 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂**. Triose phosphate is formed.
3. **Regeneration:** RuBP is made again so the cycle can continue. Uses **1 ATP per CO₂**.

So each CO₂ needs **3 ATP + 2 NADPH**, and one glucose (6 CO₂, **6 turns**) needs **18 ATP + 12 NADPH**.

## 10. The C4 pathway (Hatch and Slack pathway)

- Found in plants adapted to **dry tropical regions**, such as **maize and sorghum**.
- The **Calvin cycle** is still their main biosynthetic pathway.
- They have **Kranz anatomy**, tolerate **higher temperatures**, respond to **high light**, **lack photorespiration** and have **greater biomass productivity**.

**Kranz anatomy.** "Kranz" means **wreath**. Large **bundle sheath cells** surround the vascular bundles, sometimes in several layers. They have:
- a large number of chloroplasts;
- **thick walls impervious to gas exchange**;
- **no intercellular spaces**.

Seeing this Kranz bundle sheath in a vertical section of the leaf identifies a C4 plant.

**The pathway:**
- **Mesophyll cells:** the primary acceptor **PEP** (phosphoenol pyruvate, 3C) takes up CO₂, catalysed by **PEP carboxylase (PEPcase)**. **OAA** forms, then other C4 acids such as **malic acid or aspartic acid**. Mesophyll cells **lack RuBisCO**.
- The C4 acids move to the **bundle sheath cells**. There they are broken down to release CO₂ and a 3-carbon molecule.
- The 3-carbon molecule returns to the mesophyll and is turned back into PEP. So the pathway is cyclic.
- The released CO₂ enters the **Calvin cycle**, which in C4 plants runs **only in the bundle sheath** (in C3 plants, in all mesophyll cells). Bundle sheath cells are **rich in RuBisCO** but **lack PEPcase**.

## 11. Photorespiration

- RuBisCO is the **most abundant enzyme in the world**. Its active site binds **both CO₂ and O₂**; the binding is **competitive**, so their relative concentration decides which binds. When the two are nearly equal, it has a much greater affinity for CO₂.
- In **C3 plants** some O₂ still binds. Then RuBP + O₂ gives **one phosphoglycerate + one phosphoglycolate (2C)** instead of two PGA. This is **photorespiration**.
- In photorespiration there is **no sugar, no ATP and no NADPH** made. CO₂ is released and ATP is used. Its biological function is **not known yet**. NCERT calls it a wasteful oxygenation reaction.
- **C4 plants do not photorespire.** Breaking down C4 acids in the bundle sheath raises the CO₂ concentration at RuBisCO, so it works as a carboxylase and its oxygenase activity is minimised. This is why C4 plants are more productive. They also tolerate higher temperatures.

## 12. Factors affecting photosynthesis

- **Internal (plant) factors:** number, size, age and orientation of leaves, mesophyll cells and chloroplasts; internal CO₂ concentration; amount of chlorophyll.
- **External factors:** sunlight, temperature, CO₂ concentration and water.

:::remember
**Blackman's Law of Limiting Factors (1905):** if a chemical process is affected by more than one factor, its rate is decided by the factor **nearest to its minimal value**. Changing that factor directly changes the rate.
:::

Example: a leaf with optimal light and CO₂ may not photosynthesise at a very low temperature. Warm it to the optimum and it starts.

**Light**
- Light quality, intensity and duration all matter.
- At **low** intensity the rate rises **linearly** with light. At higher intensity it levels off, because other factors become limiting.
- **Light saturation** happens at about **10% of full sunlight**. So light is rarely limiting in nature, except for plants in shade or dense forests.
- Too much light breaks down chlorophyll and lowers photosynthesis.

**Carbon dioxide**
- CO₂ is the **major limiting factor**. Air has only **0.03–0.04%**.
- Raising it up to **0.05%** increases the rate; higher levels can be damaging over long periods.
- At low light neither C3 nor C4 plants respond to more CO₂; at high light both do.
- **C4** plants saturate at about **360 µL/L**. **C3** plants keep responding and saturate only **beyond 450 µL/L**. So today's CO₂ level limits C3 plants.
- Greenhouse crops such as **tomatoes and bell pepper** (C3 plants) are grown in CO₂-enriched air for higher yields.

**Temperature**
- Dark reactions are enzymatic, so they are temperature-controlled. Light reactions are affected much less.
- C4 plants have a **higher** temperature optimum than C3 plants.
- **Tropical** plants have a higher optimum than **temperate** plants.

**Water**
- Water is a reactant, but its effect is mostly **indirect**, through the plant:
  - water stress **closes stomata**, so less CO₂ gets in;
  - leaves **wilt**, so leaf area and metabolic activity fall.
