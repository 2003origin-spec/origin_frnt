## 1. Seed germination: where it starts

- A mature plant forms from the zygote through a precise, highly ordered series of events. The first step is **seed germination**.
- A seed germinates only when conditions are favourable. Otherwise it goes into **suspended growth or rest**, and resumes metabolic activity and growth when good conditions return.

## 2. What is growth?

- **Growth:** an **irreversible, permanent** increase in size of an organ, its parts, or even a single cell.
- It is usually accompanied by metabolic processes (both anabolic and catabolic) that use energy.
- Expansion of a leaf is growth. A piece of wood swelling in water is not: the swelling is reversible and is not driven by metabolism.

### Plant growth is generally indeterminate

- Plants keep the capacity for **unlimited growth** all their life because of **meristems**, whose cells divide and self-perpetuate; their products stop dividing and form the plant body. New cells are always being added: the **open form of growth**.
- **Root and shoot apical meristems:** primary growth, mainly elongation along the axis.
- **Lateral meristems** (vascular cambium and cork cambium): appear later in **dicots and gymnosperms** and increase girth. This is **secondary growth**.

## 3. Growth is measurable

- Growth is mainly an increase in **protoplasm**, which is hard to measure, so we measure fresh weight, dry weight, length, area, volume or cell number. Examples: maize root apical meristem, **17,500+ new cells per hour** (cell number); watermelon cells, up to **3,50,000 times** bigger (cell size); pollen tube (length); dorsiventral leaf (surface area).

## 4. Phases of growth

- **Meristematic phase:** constantly dividing cells at the root and shoot apex. Rich in protoplasm, large conspicuous nuclei; thin, cellulosic **primary walls** with abundant plasmodesmata.
- **Elongation phase:** the cells just behind the meristematic zone. Increased **vacuolation**, cell enlargement and new cell wall deposition.
- **Maturation phase:** farther back. Cells reach their maximum size in wall thickening and protoplasmic changes.

:::remember
Zones of elongation are found by the **parallel line technique** (Figure 13.3): the zones just behind the apex elongate the most.
:::

## 5. Growth rates

**Growth rate:** the increase in growth per unit time.

### Arithmetic growth

- After mitosis, **only one** daughter cell goes on dividing; the other matures. Example: a root elongating at a constant rate.
- Length against time is a **straight line**: $L_t = L_0 + rt$.

### Geometric growth

- After mitosis, **both** daughter cells go on dividing.
- Slow at first (**lag phase**), then exponential (**log phase**); with limited nutrients it slows to a **stationary phase**.
- The result is a **sigmoid (S-shaped) curve**, typical of all plant cells, tissues and organs, and of living organisms in a natural environment.
- $W_1 = W_0 e^{rt}$, where $r$ is the **relative growth rate** (**efficiency index**). The final size depends on $W_0$.
- An embryo shows both: an early **geometric phase** (all cells divide) and a later **arithmetic phase** (Figure 13.4).

### Absolute and relative growth rate

- **Absolute:** total growth per unit time. **Relative:** growth per unit time per unit initial size.
- NCERT's leaves (Figure 13.7): A 5 → 10 cm², B 50 → 55 cm². Same absolute growth, but A's relative growth (100%) is ten times B's (10%).

## 6. Conditions for growth

- **Water:** cells grow by enlargement, which needs water. **Turgidity** helps extension growth. Water is also the medium for enzyme action.
- **Oxygen:** helps release the metabolic energy needed for growth.
- **Nutrients** (macro and micro essential elements): needed to make protoplasm and as a source of energy.
- **Temperature:** each plant has an **optimum range**; going outside it can harm survival.
- **Light and gravity:** environmental signals that affect certain phases or stages of growth.

## 7. Differentiation, dedifferentiation, redifferentiation

- **Differentiation:** cells from apical meristems and cambium mature to do specific jobs; walls and protoplasm change. Example: a **tracheary element** loses its protoplasm and builds a strong, elastic, **lignocellulosic secondary wall** to carry water over long distances, even under extreme tension.
- **Dedifferentiation:** living differentiated cells that had lost the power to divide regain it. Example: **interfascicular cambium** and **cork cambium** form from fully differentiated **parenchyma** cells.
- **Redifferentiation:** cells made by such tissue again stop dividing and mature to do specific jobs. Example (products of redifferentiation): secondary xylem, secondary phloem and cork in a woody dicot.

### Differentiation is open too

- Cells from the **same meristem** mature differently, also depending on **position**. Cells pushed away from the root apical meristem become **root-cap** cells; those pushed to the periphery mature as **epidermis**.

## 8. Development

- **Development:** all the changes in the life cycle, from seed germination to **senescence**.
- Plant cell (Figure 13.8; also true for tissues and organs): meristematic cell → plasmatic growth → expansion (elongation) → differentiation → maturation → mature cell → senescence → death. Cell division sends cells back to the meristematic state.

### Plasticity and heterophylly

- **Plasticity:** plants follow different pathways, in response to the environment or the phase of life, to form different kinds of structures.
- **Heterophylly** (different leaf shapes on one plant) is an example:

| Plant | Cause of the different leaves |
| --- | --- |
| Cotton, coriander, larkspur | Phase of life: juvenile leaves differ from adult leaves |
| Buttercup | Environment: leaves in air differ from leaves in water |

Development is controlled by **intrinsic** factors, intracellular (genetic) and intercellular (chemicals such as **PGRs**), and **extrinsic** factors (light, temperature, water, oxygen, nutrition).

## 9. Plant growth regulators: general

- PGRs (plant growth substances, plant hormones, phytohormones) are **small, simple molecules** of diverse chemical nature: indole compounds (**IAA**), adenine derivatives (**kinetin**, N⁶-furfurylamino purine), carotenoid derivatives (**ABA**), terpenes (**GA₃**) and gases (**ethylene**, C₂H₄).

- **Promoters** (cell division and enlargement, pattern formation, tropic growth, flowering, fruiting, seed formation): **auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins**.
- **Inhibitors** (responses to wounds and biotic and abiotic stress; dormancy, abscission): **abscisic acid**.
- **Ethylene** could fit either group, but it is **largely an inhibitor**.

## 10. Discovery of PGRs

The discovery of each of the five major groups was **accidental**.

- **Auxin:** Charles and Francis Darwin saw **canary grass** coleoptiles bend towards one-sided light (phototropism); the **tip** was the source of the influence. **F.W. Went** isolated auxin from **oat** coleoptile tips.
- **Gibberellin:** **'bakanae' (foolish seedling)** disease of rice, caused by the fungus ***Gibberella fujikuroi***. **E. Kurosawa (1926)** produced the symptoms with sterile fungal filtrates.
- **Cytokinin:** **F. Skoog**'s tobacco internode callus grew only if, besides auxin, the medium had vascular tissue extract, yeast extract, **coconut milk** or DNA. **Miller et al. (1955)** crystallised the substance: **kinetin**.
- **ABA:** mid-1960s, **inhibitor-B, abscission II and dormin** proved to be one chemical.
- **Ethylene:** **H.H. Cousins (1910)**: a gas from ripe **oranges** hastened ripening of stored **bananas**.

## 11. Auxins

- Name from Greek *auxein*, "to grow". First isolated from **human urine**.
- Made mainly at the **growing apices** of stems and roots, then moved to where they act.
- **Natural:** IAA and indole butyric acid (**IBA**). **Synthetic:** **NAA** (naphthalene acetic acid) and **2,4-D** (2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid).
- Effects and uses:
  - start **rooting in stem cuttings** (plant propagation);
  - promote **flowering**, e.g. in **pineapple**;
  - prevent fruit and leaf drop at early stages, but promote abscission of **older, mature** leaves and fruits;
  - **apical dominance:** the growing apical bud suppresses the lateral (axillary) buds. Removing the shoot tip (**decapitation**) lets the lateral buds grow. Used in **tea plantations** and **hedge-making**;
  - induce **parthenocarpy**, e.g. in tomato;
  - **herbicides:** 2,4-D kills **dicot weeds** but does not affect mature monocots. Used for weed-free lawns;
  - control **xylem differentiation** and help in cell division.

## 12. Gibberellins

- More than **100** gibberellins are known, from fungi and higher plants: GA₁, GA₂, GA₃ and so on. **GA₃** was one of the first found and is the most studied.
- **All GAs are acidic.**
- Effects and uses:
  - increase axis length: used to lengthen **grape stalks**;
  - make fruits like **apple** elongate and improve their shape;
  - **delay senescence**, so fruits can stay on the tree longer and the market period is extended;
  - GA₃ speeds up **malting** in the brewing industry;
  - spraying **sugarcane** lengthens the stem and raises the yield by as much as **20 tonnes per acre**;
  - spraying **juvenile conifers** hastens maturity, so seeds are produced early;
  - promote **bolting** (internode elongation just before flowering) in **beet, cabbage** and many rosette plants.

## 13. Cytokinins

- Specific effects on **cytokinesis** (cell division).
- First found as **kinetin** (a modified adenine, a purine) from **autoclaved herring sperm DNA**. Kinetin does **not** occur naturally in plants.
- **Zeatin**, a natural cytokinin, was isolated from **corn kernels** and **coconut milk**.
- Natural cytokinins are made where cells divide fast: **root apices**, developing shoot buds, young fruits.
- Effects:
  - help produce new leaves, **chloroplasts** in leaves, lateral shoot growth and **adventitious shoots**;
  - help **overcome apical dominance**;
  - promote **nutrient mobilisation**, which **delays leaf senescence**.

## 14. Ethylene

- A simple **gaseous** PGR, made in large amounts by tissues undergoing **senescence** and by **ripening fruits**.
- Effects on seedlings: **horizontal growth** of seedlings, swelling of the axis, and **apical hook** formation in dicot seedlings.
- Promotes **senescence and abscission**, especially of leaves and flowers.
- **Fruit ripening:** highly effective. It raises the respiration rate during ripening; this rise is called the **respiratory climactic** (NCERT's word; the usual term is "climacteric").
- **Breaks seed and bud dormancy**: starts germination in **peanut** seeds and sprouting of **potato tubers**.
- Rapid internode and petiole elongation in **deep-water rice**, which keeps the leaves and upper shoot above water.
- Promotes **root growth and root hair** formation, which increases the absorbing surface.
- Starts **flowering** and synchronises **fruit-set in pineapple**; induces flowering in **mango**.
- Ethylene is one of the **most widely used PGRs** in agriculture. **Ethephon**, the most used source of ethylene, is absorbed and transported readily and releases ethylene slowly. It:
  - hastens fruit ripening in **tomato** and **apple**;
  - speeds abscission of flowers and fruits (**thinning** of cotton, cherry, walnut);
  - promotes **female flowers in cucumber**, raising the yield.

## 15. Abscisic acid (ABA)

- Discovered for its role in **abscission and dormancy**. A **general growth inhibitor** and metabolism inhibitor; **inhibits seed germination**.
- Stimulates **closure of stomata** and raises tolerance to many kinds of stress, so it is called the **stress hormone**.
- Important in seed development, maturation and **dormancy**. By inducing dormancy, it helps seeds withstand desiccation and other unfavourable conditions.
- In most situations ABA is an **antagonist of GAs**.

## 16. PGRs work together

- Every phase of growth and development involves some PGR. Roles can be **complementary or antagonistic**, **individualistic or synergistic**.
- Several PGRs interact in events such as dormancy of seeds and buds, abscission, senescence and apical dominance.
- PGRs are only **one** kind of intrinsic control, alongside genomic control and extrinsic factors. Many extrinsic factors (temperature, light) act **through PGRs**, for example in vernalisation, flowering, dormancy, seed germination and plant movements.
