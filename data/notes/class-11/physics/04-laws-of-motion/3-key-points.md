## First law and inertia

- **No net force does not mean no motion.** It means no **change** in motion: the body is at rest or moves at constant velocity.
- A body moving at constant velocity has zero net force on it, even if it moves fast.
- Mass is the measure of inertia. A heavier body is harder to start, stop or turn.

:::trap
"A force is needed to keep a body moving" is Aristotle's idea, and it is **wrong**. A force is needed to **change** the motion. On a real road you need a force only to beat friction.
:::

## Second law and impulse

:::trap
$\vec F$ in $\vec F = m\vec a$ is the **net external** force, the vector sum of all forces on the body. Do not put just one of the forces in.
:::

- Velocity and acceleration need not point the same way. The acceleration is always along the **net force**, not along the velocity.
- When a ball hits at speed $u$ and bounces straight back at speed $v$, the size of the change in momentum is $m(v + u)$, **not** $m(v - u)$. The direction reverses.
- A force at $90^\circ$ to the velocity changes only the direction, not the speed (as in uniform circular motion).
- Same impulse, longer time, smaller force. That is why we have air bags, crumple zones, sand pits for long jump, and why a cricketer pulls his hands back.

:::tip
(Exam extra, not in NCERT) Area under a force–time graph = impulse = change in momentum.
:::

## Third law

:::trap
Action and reaction **never cancel**, because they act on **different** bodies. Forces cancel only when they act on the **same** body.
:::

:::trap
The weight of a book and the normal force from the table on it are **not** an action–reaction pair. Both act on the book. The partner of the book's weight is the book's pull on the **Earth**.
:::

- **Horse and cart:** the cart pulls back on the horse as hard as the horse pulls it. The system moves because the ground pushes the horse forward (friction) more than friction holds the cart back.
- A rocket does not need air to push on. It pushes its exhaust gas backward, and the gas pushes the rocket forward.

## Conservation of momentum

- It needs **no net external force** on the system. If an external force acts only along one axis, momentum is still conserved along the other axes.
- In explosions and recoil the total momentum stays the same, but kinetic energy increases (it comes from stored energy: chemical in a gun, nuclear when a nucleus splits).
- When a body at rest splits into **two** pieces, they fly apart in opposite directions and the lighter piece moves faster: speeds are in the **inverse** ratio of masses.

## Normal force and friction

:::trap
$N = mg$ only on a level surface with no other vertical force and no vertical acceleration (not in an accelerating lift). On an incline, with nothing else pressing on the block, $N = mg\cos\theta$. On a level floor, pulling with force $F$ at angle $\alpha$ above the horizontal makes $N = mg - F\sin\alpha$. Pushing down at that angle makes $N = mg + F\sin\alpha$.
:::

:::tip
**Pull, don't push** a lawn roller. Pulling at an angle lifts part of the weight, so $N$ and friction become smaller.
:::

:::trap
Static friction is **not** always $\mu_s N$. That is only its **maximum**. If a 10 N push does not move a block with $\mu_s N = 25$ N, the friction is just **10 N**.
:::

- Horizontal push on a level floor: first compare the push with $\mu_s N$. Smaller: no motion, $f = $ push. Larger: it slides, and you switch to $f_k = \mu_k N$.
- Friction opposes **relative** motion, not motion itself. When you walk, or when a car speeds up, the static friction on the feet or tyres points **forward**.
- Friction does not depend on the area of contact (for the same $N$).
- A block placed at rest on an incline stays at rest if $\tan\theta \le \mu_s$. The largest such angle is $\theta_{max}$, with $\tan\theta_{max} = \mu_s$ (many books call it the **angle of repose**). Just above it, the block starts to slide.

## Lifts and connected bodies

- A weighing scale reads the **normal force**, not your true weight.
- Lift going **up and speeding up**, or going **down and slowing down**: you feel heavier, $R = m(g + a)$.
- Lift going **up and slowing down**, or going **down and speeding up**: you feel lighter, $R = m(g - a)$.
- In free fall (cable snaps) $R = 0$: you feel weightless, though gravity still acts.

:::tip
The direction of **acceleration** decides the reading, not the direction of motion. "Up-acceleration = heavier."
:::

- In a light, inextensible string over a light, smooth pulley, the tension is the **same** on both sides, and both blocks have the same size of acceleration.
- For a group of blocks moving together, first treat them as one body: $a = F_{net}/(\text{total mass})$. Then use one block's free-body diagram to find a tension or a contact force.

## Circular motion

:::trap
Centripetal force is **not** a separate force. Never add "$mv^2/r$" as an extra arrow in a free-body diagram drawn in the ground frame. It is the **net** inward force made by real forces.
:::

:::trap
On a banked road, friction points **down** the slope above $v_o$ and **up** the slope below $v_o$. At exactly $v_o = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta}$ there is no friction.
:::

- On a level road, $v_{max} = \sqrt{\mu_s rg}$ uses **static** friction, because the tyres do not slip sideways.
- These speeds do not depend on the mass of the car.
- If a string whirling a stone breaks, the stone flies off along the **tangent**, not outward along the radius.

## NCERT lines to know

:::remember
- The state of rest and the state of uniform linear motion are equivalent.
- Newton's second law is a local relation.
- Forces always occur in pairs; action and reaction act on different bodies.
- The total momentum of an isolated system of interacting particles is conserved.
- Static friction opposes impending motion. Kinetic friction opposes relative motion.
- Rolling friction is much smaller than static or sliding friction.
:::
