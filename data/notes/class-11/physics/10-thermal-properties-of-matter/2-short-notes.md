## 1. Temperature and heat

- **Temperature** is a measure of how hot or cold a body is. SI unit: kelvin (K). Degree Celsius (°C) is also common.
- **Heat** is energy that flows between two systems (or a system and its surroundings) **because of a temperature difference**. SI unit: joule (J).
- Heat flows from the hotter body to the colder one until both reach the same temperature.

:::remember
Any energy transfer that does not involve a temperature difference is **not** heat (NCERT, Points to Ponder).
:::

## 2. Measuring temperature

- A thermometer uses a **thermometric property** that changes with temperature, for example the volume of mercury or alcohol in a liquid-in-glass thermometer.
- A scale needs **two fixed points**. The old ones: the ice point and the steam point of pure water at standard pressure.

| Scale | Ice point | Steam point | Divisions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Celsius | 0 °C | 100 °C | 100 |
| Fahrenheit | 32 °F | 212 °F | 180 |
| Kelvin | 273.15 K | 373.15 K | 100 |

- Liquid-in-glass thermometers made with different liquids agree only at the fixed points, because liquids expand differently. A **gas thermometer** gives the same reading whatever gas is used (at low density).

## 3. Ideal-gas equation and absolute zero

- Boyle's law: $PV$ = constant at fixed $T$. Charles' law: $V/T$ = constant at fixed $P$. Together: $PV = \mu RT$.
- In a **constant-volume gas thermometer**, $P \propto T$, so the temperature is read from the pressure.
- Extending the $P$–$T$ straight line of any low-density gas to $P = 0$ gives the same point: **−273.15 °C**, called **absolute zero**. This is the zero (0 K) of the Kelvin scale.
- Kelvin and Celsius degrees are the same size: $T = t_C + 273.15$.
- Modern fixed point: the **triple point of water**, given the exact value **273.16 K**.

## 4. Thermal expansion

The increase in the size of a body when its temperature rises. It can be in length (linear), area or volume.

- For a small $\Delta T$: $\Delta l/l = \alpha_l\Delta T$ and $\Delta V/V = \alpha_V\Delta T$.
- For a solid that expands equally in all directions: $\alpha_A = 2\alpha_l$ and $\alpha_V = 3\alpha_l$.
- $\alpha_V$ is **not** strictly constant. It depends on temperature (NCERT shows copper's $\alpha_V$ rising and levelling off at high temperature).
- Metals expand more than glass: copper about 5 times more than pyrex glass. Invar (an iron–nickel alloy) and pyrex expand very little.
- Among liquids, alcohol (ethanol) expands more than mercury.
- Gases expand far more than solids and liquids. For an ideal gas at constant pressure, $\alpha_V = 1/T$, so it **decreases** as the temperature rises.

:::example{title="Fitting an iron ring (NCERT Example 10.2)"}
An iron ring of diameter 5.231 m at 27 °C must fit a rim of 5.243 m. With $\alpha_l = 1.20 \times 10^{-5}$ K⁻¹: $5.243 = 5.231\,[1 + 1.2 \times 10^{-5}(T_2 - 27)]$, so $T_2 \approx 218$ °C.
:::

**Thermal stress.** If a rod is clamped so it cannot expand, the supports squeeze it. The stress is $Y\alpha_l\Delta T$. NCERT example: a steel rail ($A$ = 40 cm²) held fixed while warming by 10 °C feels a force of about $10^5$ N, enough to bend rails.

## 5. Anomalous expansion of water

- Water **contracts** when heated from 0 °C to 4 °C, and expands above 4 °C.
- So water has its **maximum density at 4 °C**.
- In winter, surface water cools to 4 °C, becomes denser and sinks. Below 4 °C the top water is lighter, stays on top and freezes. Lakes freeze **from the top down**, and fish survive in the 4 °C water below.

## 6. Specific heat capacity

The heat needed depends on the **mass**, the **rise in temperature** and the **nature of the substance**.

| Quantity | Meaning | SI unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat capacity $S$ | $\Delta Q/\Delta T$ for the body | J K⁻¹ |
| Specific heat $s$ | per unit mass | J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ |
| Molar specific heat $C$ | per mole | J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ |

- $s$ and $C$ depend on the substance and its temperature.
- For a gas, the heat needed depends on the conditions. $C_p$ (pressure fixed) is more than $C_v$ (volume fixed). In NCERT Table 10.4, $C_p - C_v$ is about 8.3 to 8.5 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ for every gas listed, close to $R$ = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
- **Water** has the highest specific heat of the substances in NCERT Table 10.3 (4186 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹). So it is used as a coolant in car radiators and in hot-water bags. The sea warms and cools more slowly than land, so sea winds cool coastal areas. Deserts heat up fast by day and cool fast by night.

## 7. Calorimetry

- **Calorimetry** = measuring heat. In an **isolated** system, heat lost by the hot part = heat gained by the cold part.
- A **calorimeter**: a metal vessel and stirrer of the same metal (copper or aluminium), kept in a wooden jacket with insulation (glass wool). A thermometer goes in through an opening.

:::example{title="Specific heat of aluminium (NCERT Example 10.3)"}
0.047 kg of aluminium at 100 °C goes into a 0.14 kg copper calorimeter holding 0.25 kg water at 20 °C. Final temperature 23 °C. Heat lost by Al = heat gained by water and copper: $0.047\,s_{Al}(77) = (0.25 \times 4180 + 0.14 \times 386)(3)$, giving $s_{Al} \approx 911$ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
:::

## 8. Change of state

| Change | Name |
| --- | --- |
| Solid → liquid | Melting (fusion) |
| Liquid → solid | Freezing |
| Liquid → vapour | Vaporisation (boiling) |
| Solid → vapour directly | Sublimation |

- While the state changes, the **temperature stays constant** and both states coexist in equilibrium. The heat goes into changing the state.
- **Melting point** depends on pressure. At standard atmospheric pressure it is the **normal** melting point.
- **Regelation:** a loaded wire passes through a slab of ice without cutting it in two. Under the wire the high pressure melts the ice at a lower temperature; above the wire the water freezes again. Skating works because pressure makes a thin layer of water under the skates, which acts as a lubricant.
- **Boiling point rises with pressure**: a pressure cooker cooks faster. It **falls when pressure falls**: cooking is hard on hills.
- **Sublimation**: dry ice (solid CO₂) and iodine.
- **Triple point**: the one temperature and pressure where solid, liquid and vapour coexist. On the $P$–$T$ phase diagram, the sublimation, fusion and vaporisation curves meet there. Water: 273.16 K and about 611 Pa. (NCERT prints the pressure as $6.11 \times 10^{-3}$ Pa; the accepted value is 611.657 Pa $\approx 6.11 \times 10^{2}$ Pa.)

**Latent heat** $L$ is the heat per unit mass needed to change the state at constant temperature: $Q = mL$. $L_f$ for melting, $L_v$ for boiling. For water, $L_f = 3.33 \times 10^5$ J kg⁻¹ and $L_v = 22.6 \times 10^5$ J kg⁻¹. On a temperature–heat graph for water, the flat parts are the changes of state. The sloping parts have different slopes because ice, water and steam have different specific heats.

## 9. Heat transfer

| Mode | How | Medium? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conduction | Between neighbouring parts, by molecular collisions; no flow of matter | Needed |
| Convection | Bulk flow of the fluid itself | Fluids only |
| Radiation | Electromagnetic waves, at the speed of light | Not needed |

**Conduction.** In the steady state, $H = KA(T_C - T_D)/L$. Metals are good conductors (silver 406, copper 385 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹). Wood, glass wool and air are poor. Gases conduct poorly; liquids are in between. Plastic foam insulates because of its trapped air. Copper-bottomed pots spread heat evenly.

**Convection.**
- **Natural:** caused by gravity and buoyancy. The heated fluid becomes lighter and rises; colder fluid sinks to take its place.
- **Forced:** a pump moves the fluid. Examples: home air heaters, the human blood circulation (the heart is the pump), a car engine's cooling system.
- **Sea breeze** (day): land heats faster, warm air over land rises, cooler air from the sea moves in. At night the cycle reverses (land breeze).
- **Trade winds:** steady surface winds blowing from the north-east towards the equator. The earth's rotation makes the air come down at about 30° N instead of at the poles.

**Radiation.**
- All bodies emit thermal radiation. Black bodies absorb **and** emit better than light-coloured ones. So we wear white clothes in summer and dark clothes in winter, and cooking pots have blackened bottoms.
- **Thermos (Dewar) flask:** double glass walls, silvered to reflect radiation, with a vacuum between them to reduce conduction and convection, supported on an insulator such as cork.
- **Blackbody spectrum:** continuous over all wavelengths. The peak wavelength $\lambda_m$ moves to **shorter** wavelengths as $T$ rises (Wien's law). This is why heated iron glows dull red, then reddish yellow, then white.
- The curves depend **only on temperature**, not on the size, shape or material of the blackbody.
- Wien's law estimates star temperatures. The sun's surface: $\lambda_m$ = 4753 Å gives about 6060 K. The moon: $\lambda_m \approx$ 14 µm gives about 200 K.
- **Stefan–Boltzmann law:** power radiated $H = Ae\sigma T^4$. Emissivity: $e = 1$ for a perfect radiator; lamp black is close to 1, a tungsten lamp about 0.4.

## 10. Newton's law of cooling

- The rate of loss of heat is proportional to the **excess temperature** of the body over the surroundings: $-dQ/dt = k(T_2 - T_1)$.
- Valid only for a **small** temperature difference.
- Cooling is fast at first and slows down as the body nears room temperature (exponential decay of the excess temperature).
- Verified with a copper calorimeter of hot water inside a double-walled vessel: a graph of $\ln(T_2 - T_1)$ against $t$ is a straight line with a negative slope.
- It is a good approximation for a cup of tea cooling on a table, heat loss through the wall of a room, and heat transfer from a radiator to a room.
