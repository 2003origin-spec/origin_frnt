## 1. Ecology and its levels

- **Ecology** studies the interactions among organisms, and between organisms and their physical (abiotic) environment.
- It deals with four levels of organisation: **organisms, populations, communities and biomes**. This chapter is about the **population** level.
- Biology asks two kinds of questions. **"How"** questions ask for the mechanism (how does the bulbul sing? Its voice box). **"Why"** questions ask for the significance (why does it sing? To communicate with its mate in the breeding season).
- **Ramdeo Misra** (1908–1998) is the Father of Ecology in India. He built ecology teaching and research at Banaras Hindu University.

## 2. What is a population?

- A group of individuals of one species that live in a well-defined area, share or compete for similar resources, and can interbreed.
- A group formed by **asexual** reproduction is also treated as a population in ecology.
- NCERT examples: cormorants in a wetland, rats in an abandoned house, teakwood trees in a forest tract, bacteria in a culture plate, lotus plants in a pond.
- **Natural selection acts at the population level.** So population ecology links ecology to population genetics and evolution.

## 3. Population attributes

An individual is born and dies. Only a population has these attributes:

- **Birth rate and death rate**, given **per capita** (per member). 8 new lotus plants added to 20 gives a birth rate of 8/20 = 0.4 per lotus per year.
- **Sex ratio**, for example 60% females and 40% males.
- **Age distribution**: the percentage of individuals in each age group. Plotted, it is an **age pyramid**. For humans it shows males and females side by side.

| Pyramid | Shape of base | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expanding | Broadest | Growing |
| Stable | About equal to reproductive | Stable |
| Declining | Narrower than reproductive | Declining |

The age groups are pre-reproductive (bottom), reproductive and post-reproductive (top).

## 4. Population size (density, N)

- The effect of anything (a competitor, a predator, a pesticide) is judged by the **change in population size**.
- Size can be under 10 (Siberian cranes at Bharatpur wetlands in a year) or millions (*Chlamydomonas* in a pond).
- Population size is technically called **population density (N)**. It need not be a head count.

| Measure | When it is used | NCERT example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total number | Usually the best | Most populations |
| Per cent cover or biomass | Number is misleading | 1 banyan vs 200 *Parthenium* plants |
| Relative density | Absolute value not needed | Fish caught per trap |
| Indirect signs | Animals can't be seen or counted | Tiger census: pug marks, fecal pellets |

- A huge banyan with a large canopy matters far more than its count of "1" suggests. So per cent cover or biomass is better there.
- A dense bacterial culture in a Petri dish is too large to count.

## 5. Population growth: four processes

- **Natality (B):** births added in a period. Increases N.
- **Immigration (I):** individuals of the **same species** coming in from elsewhere. Increases N.
- **Mortality (D):** deaths in a period. Decreases N.
- **Emigration (E):** individuals leaving the habitat. Decreases N.

$$N_{t+1} = N_t + [(B + I) - (D + E)]$$

- Normally **births and deaths** matter most.
- Immigration and emigration matter only in special cases. In a **newly colonised** habitat, immigration may add more than births.

## 6. Exponential growth

- Happens when resources (food and space) are **unlimited**. Each species can then fully realise its **innate potential** to grow, as Darwin observed.
- Growth is exponential or geometric.

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = (b - d) \times N$$

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = rN$$

- $b$ and $d$ are **per capita** birth and death rates, not total numbers.
- $r = b - d$ is the **intrinsic rate of natural increase**. It is used to judge how any biotic or abiotic factor affects population growth.
- Integral form: $N_t = N_0 e^{rt}$.
- Plot of N against time: a **J-shaped curve**.
- Even a slow breeder like the **elephant** could reach huge numbers without checks (Darwin).
- **Chessboard story:** grains doubled on each of 64 squares. By half the board, the whole kingdom's wheat was not enough. A *Paramecium* doubling every day for 64 days would also reach an enormous number, if food and space were unlimited.

## 7. Logistic growth

- No natural population has unlimited resources. Individuals **compete**, and the "fittest" survive and reproduce.
- A habitat can support only a maximum number: its **carrying capacity (K)**.
- Growth with limited resources goes through: **lag phase → acceleration → deceleration → asymptote** (when N reaches K).
- Plot of N against time: a **sigmoid curve**. This is **Verhulst-Pearl logistic growth**.

$$\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(\frac{K - N}{K}\right)$$

- When N is small, $(K - N)/K$ is close to 1, so growth is nearly exponential. As N nears K, the term nears 0 and growth stops.
- The logistic model is **more realistic**, because resources for most animal populations are finite and become limiting sooner or later.
- In either case, growth is finally limited by the carrying capacity (NCERT summary).

| Feature | Exponential | Logistic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resources | Unlimited | Limited |
| Equation | $dN/dt = rN$ | $dN/dt = rN(K-N)/K$ |
| Curve | J-shaped | Sigmoid (S) |
| K involved? | No | Yes |

## 8. Life history variation

- Populations evolve to maximise **reproductive fitness**, also called **Darwinian fitness (high r value)**.
- Breed **once** in a lifetime: Pacific salmon fish, bamboo. Breed **many times**: most birds and mammals.
- **Many small** offspring: oysters, pelagic fishes. **Few large** offspring: birds, mammals.
- These traits evolved under the limits set by the abiotic and biotic parts of the habitat.

## 9. Population interactions

- No habitat has a single species. Even a plant needs soil microbes (to return nutrients) and animals (for pollination).
- **Interspecific interactions** happen between populations of **two different species**.
- Predation, parasitism and commensalism share one feature: the two species **live closely together**.

## 10. Predation (+, –)

- Nature's way of passing energy fixed by plants to **higher trophic levels**. A sparrow eating seeds is also a predator. Herbivores are, broadly, predators of plants.
- **Predators control prey numbers.** Without them, prey could reach very high densities and destabilise the ecosystem.
- **Invasive species** spread fast where their natural predators are absent. The **prickly pear cactus** in Australia (early 1920s) spread over millions of hectares. It was controlled only after a **cactus-feeding moth** from its native habitat was brought in.
- **Biological control** of pests is based on the ability of predators to regulate prey.
- Predators **maintain species diversity** by reducing competition among prey. When the starfish *Pisaster* was removed from an intertidal area (American Pacific Coast), **more than 10 invertebrate species** became extinct within a year.
- Predators are **"prudent"**. Overexploiting prey would wipe out the prey and then the predator.

**Prey defences:**
- Camouflage (cryptic colour): some insects and frogs.
- Being poisonous. The **Monarch butterfly** is distasteful to birds because of a chemical it takes in as a **caterpillar** from a poisonous weed.

**Plant defences** (plants cannot run away; nearly **25%** of insects are phytophagous):
- Morphological: **thorns** (*Acacia*, cactus), the most common.
- Chemical: *Calotropis* makes highly poisonous **cardiac glycosides**, so cattle and goats don't browse it.
- Nicotine, caffeine, quinine, strychnine and opium are made by plants as defences against grazers and browsers.

## 11. Competition (–, –)

- Darwin saw interspecific competition as a strong force in evolution.
- Not only closely related species compete. **Unrelated** species can too: **flamingoes and resident fishes** compete for zooplankton in shallow South American lakes.
- Resources need not be limiting. In **interference competition**, one species lowers the feeding efficiency of another just by its presence, even when food and space are plentiful.
- **Best definition:** competition is a process in which the **fitness (r)** of one species is significantly lower in the presence of another.
- Herbivores and plants seem more affected by competition than carnivores.

**Evidence from nature:**
- **Abingdon tortoise** (Galapagos) became extinct within a decade after **goats** were introduced, apparently due to the goats' better browsing.
- **Competitive release:** a species restricted by a superior competitor spreads widely when that competitor is removed. **Connell** (rocky coasts of Scotland): the larger barnacle ***Balanus*** dominates the intertidal zone and excludes the smaller ***Chathamalus*** (NCERT's spelling; usually spelt *Chthamalus*).

:::remember
**Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle:** two closely related species competing for the same resources cannot co-exist indefinitely; the competitively inferior one is eliminated. This may hold if resources are limiting, but not otherwise.
:::

- **Resource partitioning** lets competitors co-exist: different feeding times or foraging patterns. **MacArthur:** five closely related **warbler** species lived on the same tree by foraging differently.

## 12. Parasitism (+, –)

- Free lodging and meals, so it evolved in many groups, from plants to higher vertebrates.
- Many parasites are **host-specific**, and host and parasite **co-evolve**.
- Adaptations: loss of unnecessary sense organs, adhesive organs or **suckers**, loss of digestive system, **high reproductive capacity**.
- Complex life cycles with intermediate hosts or vectors. **Human liver fluke** (a trematode): two intermediate hosts, a **snail and a fish**. **Malarial parasite**: a vector, the **mosquito**.
- Most parasites harm the host: lower survival, growth, reproduction and population density, and more risk of predation.

| Type | Lives | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ectoparasite | On the surface | Lice on humans, ticks on dogs, copepods on marine fish, *Cuscuta* |
| Endoparasite | Inside (liver, kidney, lungs, RBCs) | Liver fluke, malarial parasite |
| Brood parasite | Lays eggs in host's nest | Cuckoo (koel) in a crow's nest |

- *Cuscuta* grows on hedge plants. It has **lost its chlorophyll and leaves**.
- Endoparasites have very complex life cycles, simplified body structure and high reproductive potential.
- In brood parasitism, the parasite's eggs evolved to look like the host's eggs in **size and colour**.
- The **female mosquito is not considered a parasite**, though it needs our blood for reproduction. NCERT asks why but gives no answer. A common answer (beyond NCERT): it only visits the host briefly to feed and does not live on or in it.

## 13. Commensalism (+, 0)

- **Orchid** growing as an epiphyte on a **mango** branch.
- **Barnacles** on the back of a **whale**.
- **Cattle egret** and grazing cattle: the moving cattle flush out insects for the egrets.
- **Clown fish** among the stinging tentacles of a **sea anemone**: the fish gets protection; the anemone gets no apparent benefit.

## 14. Amensalism (–, 0)

One species is harmed and the other is unaffected. NCERT defines it but gives no example in this chapter.

## 15. Mutualism (+, +)

- **Lichens:** a fungus with photosynthesising algae or cyanobacteria.
- **Mycorrhizae:** fungi with roots of higher plants. Fungi help absorb nutrients; the plant gives carbohydrates.
- Plants "pay fees" to animals: pollen and nectar for pollinators, juicy fruits for seed dispersers. The system must be guarded against **cheaters** (nectar thieves), so mutualists often **co-evolve**.
- **Fig and wasp:** in many fig species, a tight **one-to-one** relationship: a fig species is pollinated only by its partner wasp species. The female wasp pollinates the fig inflorescence while looking for egg-laying sites, and uses the fruit to lay eggs. Some developing seeds feed its larvae.
- **Orchids** have diverse flowers that attract the right pollinator (bees, bumblebees).
- ***Ophrys*** (Mediterranean orchid) uses **sexual deceit**. One petal looks like the **female bee**. The male bee "pseudocopulates" with it, gets dusted with pollen, and carries it to another flower. If the female bee's pattern changes, the orchid must co-evolve to keep pollination working.
