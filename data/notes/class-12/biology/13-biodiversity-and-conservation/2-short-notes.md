## 1. What is biodiversity?

- Diversity (heterogeneity) exists at **all levels** of biological organisation, from macromolecules in cells to biomes.
- **Biodiversity** is the combined diversity at all these levels. The term was popularised by the sociobiologist **Edward Wilson**.
- Some striking counts: more than 20,000 species of ants, 3,00,000 of beetles, 28,000 of fishes and nearly 20,000 of orchids.

The three most important levels:

- **Genetic diversity:** one species varies genetically over its range. *Rauwolfia vomitoria* in different Himalayan ranges differs in the potency and concentration of **reserpine**. India has more than **50,000** genetically different strains of rice and **1,000** varieties of mango.
- **Species diversity:** the Western Ghats have greater **amphibian** species diversity than the Eastern Ghats.
- **Ecological diversity:** India, with deserts, rain forests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, estuaries and alpine meadows, has more ecosystem diversity than a Scandinavian country like **Norway**.

Evolution took millions of years to build this diversity. At present rates of loss, we could lose it in **less than two centuries**.

## 2. How many species are there?

- **IUCN (2004):** slightly more than **1.5 million** plant and animal species have been described.
- Most undiscovered species are in the **tropics**. Species lists are more complete in temperate countries.
- **How the total is estimated:** compare the temperate–tropical species richness of an insect group that is well studied, then apply (extrapolate) this ratio to other groups of animals and plants.
- Extreme estimates: 20 to 50 million. **Robert May's** conservative estimate: about **7 million**.

Among recorded species:

- **Animals:** more than 70%. **Plants** (including algae, fungi, bryophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms): no more than 22%.
- **Insects** are the most species-rich group: more than 70% of all animals.
- **Fungi** have more species than fishes, amphibians, reptiles and mammals put together.
- **Prokaryotes** are not counted. Normal taxonomic methods do not suit microbes, and many cannot be cultured in the lab. Using biochemical or molecular criteria, their diversity alone might run into millions.

## 3. Biodiversity in India

- India has only **2.4%** of the world's land area but **8.1%** of global species diversity.
- So it is one of the **12 mega diversity countries**.
- About **45,000** species of plants and **twice as many** animals have been recorded.
- By May's estimate only 22% of species are recorded. Applied to India, more than **1,00,000 plant** and **3,00,000 animal** species are still to be discovered.
- Many may go extinct before they are found. NCERT: "Nature's biological library is burning" before we catalogue its books.

## 4. Pattern 1: latitudinal gradient

- Species diversity generally **decreases** from the equator towards the poles.
- With very few exceptions, the **tropics** (23.5° N to 23.5° S) have more species than temperate or polar areas.

| Place | Bird species |
| --- | --- |
| Colombia (near equator) | nearly 1,400 |
| India (mostly tropical) | more than 1,200 |
| New York (41° N) | 105 |
| Greenland (71° N) | 56 |

- A tropical forest in **Ecuador** (NCERT prints "Equador") has up to 10 times the vascular plant species of an equal-sized temperate forest in the Midwest USA.
- The **Amazon rain forest** has the greatest biodiversity on earth: more than 40,000 plant species, 3,000 fishes, 1,300 birds, 427 mammals, 427 amphibians, 378 reptiles and more than 1,25,000 invertebrates. At least two million insect species there may still be undiscovered.

:::remember
**Why are the tropics so rich?** Three hypotheses:
1. **More evolutionary time.** Speciation is generally a function of time. Temperate regions had frequent glaciations in the past; the tropics stayed relatively undisturbed for millions of years.
2. **Constant environment.** The tropics are less seasonal, more constant and predictable. This promotes **niche specialisation**.
3. **More solar energy.** This gives higher productivity, which may indirectly support more species.
:::

## 5. Pattern 2: species–area relationship

- **Alexander von Humboldt** (German naturalist and geographer), exploring South American jungles, saw that species richness **increases with area, but only up to a limit**.
- For many taxa (angiosperms, birds, bats, freshwater fishes) the curve of species richness against area is a **rectangular hyperbola**.
- On a **logarithmic scale** it becomes a straight line:

$$\log S = \log C + Z \log A$$

Here $S$ = species richness, $A$ = area, $Z$ = slope of the line (regression coefficient) and $C$ = Y-intercept.

- For most regions and groups, $Z$ is **0.1 to 0.2**: plants in Britain, birds in California and molluscs in New York state all give similar slopes.
- For **very large areas** like whole continents, the line is much **steeper**: $Z$ = **0.6 to 1.2**.
- Example: frugivorous (fruit-eating) birds and mammals in tropical forests of different continents, $Z$ = **1.15**.
- A steeper slope means species richness rises much faster with area.

## 6. Why species diversity matters to the ecosystem

- Ecologists have **no definitive answer** yet. For decades they believed that more species make a community more stable.
- A **stable community**: shows little year-to-year variation in productivity, is resistant or resilient to occasional disturbances, and resists invasion by alien species.
- **David Tilman** (long-term experiments with outdoor plots): plots with more species showed **less year-to-year variation in total biomass**, and higher diversity gave **higher productivity**.

:::remember
**Rivet popper hypothesis (Paul Ehrlich, Stanford):**
- Airplane = ecosystem. Rivets = species.
- Passengers popping rivets = species going extinct. At first flight safety (ecosystem function) is not affected, but over time the plane becomes dangerously weak.
- **Which** rivet goes matters too. Rivets on the **wings** (key species that drive major ecosystem functions) matter more than those on seats or windows.
:::

## 7. Loss of biodiversity

- New species are hardly being added by speciation, but losses continue, mainly due to **human activities**.
- Human colonisation of tropical **Pacific Islands** led to the extinction of more than **2,000** native bird species.
- **IUCN Red List (2004):** **784** extinctions in the last 500 years (338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates, 87 plants). The NCERT summary rounds this to "nearly 700"; if asked for the IUCN 2004 figure, use 784.
- Recent extinctions: **dodo** (Mauritius), **quagga** (Africa), **thylacine** (Australia), **Steller's sea cow** (Russia), and three subspecies of tiger (**Bali, Javan, Caspian**).
- The last twenty years alone: **27** species lost.
- Extinctions are **not random**. Some groups, like **amphibians**, are more vulnerable.
- More than **15,500** species face extinction now (more than 650 of them from India, says the NCERT summary): 12% of birds, 23% of mammals, 32% of amphibians and 31% of gymnosperms.
- Life (over 3 billion years) has seen **five** mass extinctions. The **Sixth Extinction** now in progress differs in its **rate**: 100 to 1,000 times faster than in pre-human times, and caused by humans.
- If trends continue, nearly **half** of all species might be wiped out in the next **100 years**.

**Effects of biodiversity loss in a region:**
- decline in plant production;
- lower resistance to environmental disturbances such as drought;
- more variability in ecosystem processes like plant productivity, water use, and pest and disease cycles.

## 8. Causes: the "Evil Quartet"

**(i) Habitat loss and fragmentation** — the **most important** cause.
- Tropical rain forests once covered more than **14%** of earth's land surface; now no more than **6%**.
- The **Amazon** ("lungs of the planet") is being cleared for **soya beans** and for grasslands to raise **beef cattle**.
- Pollution also degrades habitats.
- When large habitats break into small fragments, mammals and birds needing large territories, and migratory animals, are badly hit.

**(ii) Over-exploitation** — when "need" turns to "greed".
- **Steller's sea cow** and the **passenger pigeon** were lost this way.
- Many marine fish populations are over-harvested today.

**(iii) Alien species invasions** — introduced species turn invasive.
- **Nile perch** in **Lake Victoria** (east Africa) led to the extinction of more than **200** species of **cichlid** fish.
- Invasive weeds: carrot grass (*Parthenium*), *Lantana* and water hyacinth (*Eichhornia*; NCERT prints "Eicchornia", the correct spelling is *Eichhornia*).
- **African catfish** *Clarias gariepinus*, illegally introduced for aquaculture, threatens our native catfishes.

**(iv) Co-extinctions** — a species dies out, and species tied to it in an **obligatory** way die with it.
- A host fish goes extinct, and its unique parasites go too.
- In a **coevolved plant–pollinator mutualism**, extinction of one leads to extinction of the other.

## 9. Why conserve biodiversity?

| Argument | Main idea |
| --- | --- |
| Narrowly utilitarian | Direct economic benefits |
| Broadly utilitarian | Ecosystem services |
| Ethical | Every species has intrinsic value |

- **Narrowly utilitarian:** food (cereals, pulses, fruits), firewood, fibre, construction material, industrial products (tannins, lubricants, dyes, resins, perfumes) and medicines. More than **25%** of drugs sold worldwide come from plants. **25,000** plant species are used in traditional medicine. **Bioprospecting** = exploring molecular, genetic and species-level diversity for useful products.
- **Broadly utilitarian:** the Amazon produces about **20%** of the oxygen in earth's atmosphere by photosynthesis. Pollination by bees, bumblebees, birds and bats. Also aesthetic pleasures (woods, spring flowers, a bulbul's song).
- **Ethical:** a moral duty to care for the species we share the planet with, and to pass on our biological legacy in good order.

## 10. How we conserve: in situ and ex situ

| Feature | In situ | Ex situ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | On site | Off site |
| Protects | Whole ecosystem | Threatened species |
| Examples | Hotspots, national parks, sacred groves | Zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks |

**In situ** — "save the entire forest to save the tiger".
- **Biodiversity hotspots:** regions with very high species richness and high **endemism** (species found only there). They are also regions of fast habitat loss.
- 25 hotspots at first, 9 more added: **34** in all. Together they cover **less than 2%** of land, yet strict protection could reduce ongoing mass extinctions by almost **30%**.
- India's three: **Western Ghats and Sri Lanka**, **Indo-Burma**, **Himalaya**.
- India's legally protected areas: **14 biosphere reserves, 90 national parks, 448 wildlife sanctuaries** (the summary says "> 450" sanctuaries). These are NCERT's figures; use them in NEET.
- **Sacred groves:** forest tracts set aside by religious and cultural traditions. All the trees and wildlife in them are venerated and given total protection. Found in Khasi and Jaintia Hills (Meghalaya), Aravalli Hills (Rajasthan), Western Ghats of Karnataka and Maharashtra, and Sarguja, Chanda and Bastar (Madhya Pradesh). In **Meghalaya** they are the last refuges of many rare and threatened plants.

**Ex situ** — for **endangered or threatened** species (very high risk of extinction in the wild in the near future).
- Zoological parks, botanical gardens and wildlife safari parks. Many animals extinct in the wild are still kept in zoological parks.
- **Cryopreservation** of gametes (kept viable and fertile for long periods).
- **In vitro fertilisation** of eggs and **tissue culture** propagation of plants.
- **Seed banks** for genetic strains of commercially important plants.

## 11. International efforts

- **Convention on Biological Diversity ("The Earth Summit")**, **Rio de Janeiro, 1992**: all nations to conserve biodiversity and use its benefits sustainably.
- **World Summit on Sustainable Development**, **Johannesburg** (South Africa), **2002**: **190** countries pledged to significantly reduce the rate of biodiversity loss by **2010**.
