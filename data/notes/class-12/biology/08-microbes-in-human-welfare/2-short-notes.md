## 1. Microbes are everywhere

- Microbes live in soil, water, air, and inside our bodies and those of other animals and plants.
- They live even where no other life can: deep inside **geysers (thermal vents)** at up to **100 °C**, deep in the soil, under thick snow, and in highly acidic places.
- They are diverse: **protozoa, bacteria, fungi, microscopic plant and animal viruses, viroids and prions** (prions are proteinaceous infectious agents).
- Examples of viruses in NCERT: the **bacteriophage**, the **adenovirus** (causes respiratory infections) and the rod-shaped **Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV)**.
- Bacteria and many fungi grow on nutritive media as **colonies** that can be seen with the naked eye.
- Many microbes cause disease, but **not all microbes are harmful**. Many are very useful to humans.

## 2. Microbes in household products

**Curd**
- *Lactobacillus* and other **lactic acid bacteria (LAB)** grow in milk and turn it into curd.
- The acids they make **coagulate and partly digest** milk proteins.
- A little curd added to fresh milk acts as the **inoculum or starter**. Its LAB multiply at a suitable temperature.
- Curd is more nutritious: it has more **vitamin B12**.
- In our stomach, LAB help by **checking disease-causing microbes**.

**Dough**
- Dough for **idli and dosa** is fermented by **bacteria**.
- Dough for **bread** is fermented by **baker's yeast**, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*.
- The dough puffs up because of **CO₂** made during fermentation.

**Traditional foods and drinks**
- **Toddy**, a drink of parts of south India, is made by fermenting **sap from palms**.
- Microbes are used to ferment **fish, soyabean and bamboo shoots** into foods.

**Cheese** (one of the oldest foods made with microbes)
- Each variety gets its texture, flavour and taste from the microbes used.
- **Swiss cheese:** the large holes come from a lot of CO₂ made by the bacterium *Propionibacterium sharmanii*.
- **Roquefort cheese:** ripened by growing a specific **fungus** on it, which gives its flavour.

## 3. Microbes in industrial products

Industrial production needs microbes grown in very large vessels called **fermentors**.

### Fermented beverages
- Yeasts have been used since ancient times for wine, beer, whisky, brandy and rum.
- The same *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* used for bread is called **brewer's yeast** here.
- It ferments **malted cereals and fruit juices** to make **ethanol**.
- The drink depends on the raw material and on the processing:

| Processing | Drinks |
| --- | --- |
| Without distillation | Wine, beer |
| With distillation of the fermented broth | Whisky, brandy, rum |

### Antibiotics
- "Anti" means against and "bio" means life. Antibiotics are against the life of disease-causing organisms, but "pro life" for humans.
- **Definition:** chemicals made by some microbes that can kill or slow the growth of other (disease-causing) microbes.
- They are among the most important discoveries of the twentieth century.

:::remember
**Penicillin** was the **first** antibiotic found, and it was a **chance discovery**.
- Alexander Fleming was working on *Staphylococci* bacteria.
- In an unwashed culture plate, a mould was growing, and *Staphylococci* could not grow around it.
- A chemical from the mould caused this. He named it penicillin, after the mould ***Penicillium notatum***.
- **Ernest Chain and Howard Florey** later showed its full potential as an antibiotic.
- It was widely used to treat American soldiers wounded in **World War II**.
- Fleming, Chain and Florey got the **Nobel Prize in 1945**.
:::

- Antibiotics greatly improved treatment of deadly diseases: **plague, whooping cough (kali khansi), diphtheria (gal ghotu) and leprosy (kusht rog)**. The NCERT summary also names **pneumonia**.

### Chemicals, enzymes and other bioactive molecules

**Organic acids and alcohol**

| Product | Microbe |
| --- | --- |
| Citric acid | *Aspergillus niger* (fungus) |
| Acetic acid | *Acetobacter aceti* (bacterium) |
| Butyric acid | *Clostridium butylicum* (bacterium) |
| Lactic acid | *Lactobacillus* (bacterium) |
| Ethanol (commercial) | *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (yeast) |

**Enzymes**
- **Lipases** are used in **detergents**. They help remove oily stains from laundry.
- **Pectinases and proteases** **clarify** bottled fruit juices. That is why bottled juice is clearer than home-made juice.
- **Streptokinase** is made by the bacterium ***Streptococcus*** and modified by genetic engineering. It is used as a **"clot buster"** to remove clots from the blood vessels of patients who have had a **myocardial infarction** (heart attack).

**Bioactive molecules**
- **Cyclosporin A:** an **immunosuppressive** agent for **organ-transplant** patients. Made by the fungus ***Trichoderma polysporum***.
- **Statins:** lower **blood cholesterol**. Made by ***Monascus purpureus*** (NCERT calls it a yeast). They act by **competitively inhibiting** the enzyme that makes cholesterol.

## 4. Microbes in sewage treatment

- Cities make huge amounts of waste water every day. A major component is **human excreta**. This municipal waste water is **sewage**.
- Sewage has a lot of organic matter and microbes, many of them **pathogenic**. So it cannot go straight into rivers.
- It is treated in **sewage treatment plants (STPs)** by the **heterotrophic microbes** already present in the sewage.

### Primary treatment (physical)
- Removes particles, large and small, by **filtration and sedimentation**, in stages:
  1. Floating debris is removed by **sequential filtration**.
  2. **Grit** (soil and small pebbles) is removed by **sedimentation**.
- The solids that settle form the **primary sludge**. The liquid on top (supernatant) is the **effluent**.
- The primary effluent goes on to secondary treatment.

### Secondary treatment (biological)
1. The primary effluent goes into large **aeration tanks**. It is stirred mechanically and **air is pumped in**.
2. Useful **aerobic** microbes grow fast as **flocs** (bacteria with fungal filaments, forming a mesh).
3. These microbes eat most of the organic matter, so the **BOD falls** sharply. Treatment goes on until BOD is low.
4. The effluent then goes to a **settling tank**, where the flocs settle. This sediment is the **activated sludge**.
5. A **small part** of the activated sludge is pumped back into the aeration tank as **inoculum**.
6. The **major part** goes to **anaerobic sludge digesters**. There, **anaerobic** bacteria digest the bacteria and fungi in the sludge.
7. This digestion gives **biogas** (methane, hydrogen sulphide, CO₂), which burns and can be used as fuel.
8. The effluent from secondary treatment is usually released into rivers and streams.

### BOD
- **BOD** is the oxygen that would be used if all the organic matter in **one litre** of water were oxidised by bacteria.
- The BOD test measures the **rate of oxygen uptake** by microbes in a water sample. So BOD is an **indirect** measure of organic matter.
- Greater BOD means greater **polluting potential**.

### The bigger picture
- Microbial sewage treatment has been used for **more than a century**, almost everywhere. No man-made technology has matched it.
- Sewage now grows faster than treatment plants are built. Untreated sewage in rivers causes pollution and water-borne diseases.
- The **Ministry of Environment and Forests** started the **Ganga Action Plan** and **Yamuna Action Plan**. They plan many new STPs so that only treated sewage reaches these rivers.

## 5. Microbes in biogas production

- **Biogas** is a mixture of gases, **mostly methane**, made by microbes. It can be used as fuel.
- The gas a microbe makes depends on the microbe and its substrate. In dough, cheese and drinks, the main gas is **CO₂**.
- Some bacteria growing **anaerobically on cellulose** make a lot of **methane, along with CO₂ and H₂**. They are called **methanogens**. A common one is ***Methanobacterium***.
- Where methanogens live:
  - in the **anaerobic sludge** of sewage treatment;
  - in the **rumen** (part of the stomach) of cattle, where they help **break down cellulose** and so help cattle nutrition.
- So cattle dung (**gobar**) is rich in methanogens. Biogas made from dung is called **gobar gas**.

**The biogas plant**
- A **concrete tank, 10–15 feet deep**, collects bio-wastes and is fed a **slurry of dung**.
- A **floating cover** over the slurry rises as gas is made.
- One outlet pipes **biogas** to nearby houses for **cooking and lighting**.
- Another outlet removes the **spent slurry**, which can be used as **fertiliser**.
- Plants are mostly in **rural areas**, where cattle dung is plentiful.
- The technology was developed in India mainly by **IARI** (Indian Agricultural Research Institute) and **KVIC** (Khadi and Village Industries Commission).

## 6. Microbes as biocontrol agents

- **Biocontrol:** using biological methods to control plant diseases and pests.
- Chemical insecticides, pesticides and weedicides are toxic to humans and animals. They pollute soil, ground water, fruits, vegetables and crops.

**The organic farmer's view**
- Biodiversity furthers health. The more variety a landscape has, the more sustainable it is.
- Pests are **not wiped out**. They are kept at **manageable levels** by natural checks and balances.
- Wiping them out is undesirable: the useful predatory and parasitic insects that feed on or live in them would also die.
- The farmer must know the field's life forms (predators and pests), their life cycles, feeding patterns and preferred habitats.

**Examples**
- **Ladybird** (beetle with red and black markings) controls **aphids**. **Dragonflies** control **mosquitoes**.
- ***Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt)** controls **butterfly caterpillars**.
  - Sold in sachets as **dried spores**. These are mixed with water and sprayed on plants such as **brassicas and fruit trees**.
  - Insect larvae eat them. The **toxin is released in the larval gut** and kills the larvae.
  - Other insects are not harmed.
  - Bt toxin genes have been put into plants by genetic engineering, making them pest-resistant. Example: **Bt-cotton**, grown in some Indian states.
- ***Trichoderma*:** free-living fungi, very common in **root ecosystems**. They are effective biocontrol agents of several **plant pathogens** (organisms that cause plant diseases).
- **Baculoviruses:** pathogens of **insects and other arthropods**.
  - Most used in biocontrol belong to the genus ***Nucleopolyhedrovirus***.
  - They are **species-specific** and **narrow spectrum**.
  - No harm to plants, mammals, birds, fish or non-target insects.
  - Useful in **integrated pest management (IPM)** and in ecologically sensitive areas.

## 7. Microbes as biofertilisers

- Overuse of chemical fertilisers pollutes the environment. So there is a push towards **organic farming** with biofertilisers.
- **Biofertilisers** are organisms that enrich the nutrient quality of the soil. Main sources: **bacteria, fungi and cyanobacteria**.

**Bacteria (fix nitrogen)**
- ***Rhizobium***: **symbiotic**, in the root nodules of **leguminous** plants. It fixes atmospheric nitrogen into organic forms used by the plant.
- ***Azospirillum*** and ***Azotobacter***: **free-living** in the soil. They enrich soil nitrogen.

**Fungi (mycorrhiza)**
- Fungi form symbiotic associations with plant roots called **mycorrhiza**. Many members of the genus ***Glomus*** form them.
- The fungus **absorbs phosphorus** from the soil and passes it to the plant.
- Other benefits to the plant: **resistance to root-borne pathogens**, **tolerance to salinity and drought**, and better overall growth.

**Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae)**
- **Autotrophic** microbes in water and on land. Many fix atmospheric nitrogen: ***Anabaena, Nostoc, Oscillatoria***.
- An important biofertiliser in **paddy (rice) fields**.
- They also add **organic matter** to the soil.

Many biofertilisers are now sold in India. Farmers use them to replenish soil nutrients and depend less on chemical fertilisers.
