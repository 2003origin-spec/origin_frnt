## Ratios

:::trap
**Phenotype ratio is not genotype ratio.** A monohybrid F2 is 3 : 1 by phenotype but 1 : 2 : 1 by genotype. Read which one the question asks for.
:::

- In a monohybrid F2 (Rr × Rr) with incomplete dominance or co-dominance, the heterozygote looks different from both homozygotes, so the phenotype ratio equals the genotype ratio: **1 : 2 : 1**.
- Among the **tall** plants of a monohybrid F2, only **1 in 3** is pure (TT). The other 2 are Tt.
- In a dihybrid F2, only 4 of the 16 boxes are pure-breeding for both traits (RRYY, RRyy, rrYY, rryy).

:::tip
Ratios to know by heart: **3 : 1** (monohybrid F2), **1 : 2 : 1** (incomplete dominance), **1 : 1** (test cross), **9 : 3 : 3 : 1** (dihybrid F2), **1 : 1 : 1 : 1** (dihybrid test cross).
:::

:::trap
Incomplete dominance does **not** mean blending. Pink F1 snapdragons give red and white plants again in the F2, so the alleles stayed separate. Mendel's law of segregation still holds.
:::

## Dominance

- **Co-dominance** (AB blood group): both alleles show **fully**. **Incomplete dominance** (pink snapdragon): the heterozygote is **in between**.
- Dominance is not a property of the allele alone. It depends on the trait you look at: for the pea starch gene (B/b), B is dominant for seed shape (Bb seeds are round) but shows incomplete dominance for starch-grain size (Bb grains are medium-sized).
- An A × B couple can have children of **all four** blood groups if both parents are heterozygous ($I^Ai \times I^Bi$).

## Linkage

- Linkage is the main exception to Mendel's law of independent assortment: linked genes tend to travel together.
- The closer two genes are, the **less** they recombine. 1% recombination = 1 map unit.
- Morgan's dihybrid crosses didn't give 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 because both genes were on the **same** chromosome (here the X). They were linked, so parental combinations far outnumbered the recombinants.

## Many genes or many effects?

:::trap
Don't swap these two:
- **Polygenic:** many genes, one trait (skin colour). A smooth range of shades.
- **Pleiotropy:** one gene, many effects (phenylketonuria).
:::

## Sex determination

:::remember
- In **humans** the **father** decides the child's sex (his sperm carries X or Y).
- In **birds** the **mother** decides (her egg carries Z or W).
- In the **grasshopper** (XO), males have **one chromosome fewer** than females.
:::

:::trap
A male honeybee comes from an **unfertilised** egg. So he has **no father** and can't have sons, but he has a grandfather and can have grandsons.
:::

## Sex-linked disorders

- X-linked recessive disorders (haemophilia, colour blindness) are far more common in **males**, who have only one X.
- A man passes his X to **all his daughters** and to **none of his sons**.
- So a colour-blind man and a woman who is not a carrier have **no** colour-blind children: all the sons are normal, and all the daughters are carriers.
- A carrier mother and a normal father: each son has a 1 in 2 chance of being affected, and each daughter a 1 in 2 chance of being a carrier. No daughter is affected.

:::trap
Sickle-cell anaemia and thalassemia are **autosomal**, not sex-linked. Boys and girls get them equally often.
:::

## Chromosomal disorders

:::trap
**Klinefelter** (47, XXY) is a **male**. **Turner** (45, X0) is a **female**. Both are sterile. Don't swap them.
:::

- **Down syndrome** is an **autosome** disorder: trisomy of chromosome 21. Klinefelter and Turner are **sex chromosome** disorders.
- Aneuploidy (one or more chromosomes extra or missing, not a whole set) comes from chromatids failing to separate. Polyploidy (whole extra sets) comes from cytokinesis failing. Polyploidy is common in plants.

:::tip
**Count the chromosomes:** Down 47, Klinefelter 47, Turner 45. Only Turner has fewer than 46.
:::

## Sickle-cell anaemia in one line

:::remember
Glutamic acid → valine at position **6** of the **β**-globin chain, from GAG → GUG. It is a **point mutation**.
:::

## Names and dates

:::remember
- Mendel's experiments: **1856–1863**. Published: **1865**. Rediscovered: **1900** (de Vries, Correns, von Tschermak).
- Chromosomal theory of inheritance: **Sutton and Boveri** (1902).
- Linkage and recombination in fruit flies: **Thomas Hunt Morgan**.
- First gene maps from recombination: **Alfred Sturtevant**.
- X body: **Henking** (1891).
- Down syndrome first described: **Langdon Down** (1866).
:::
