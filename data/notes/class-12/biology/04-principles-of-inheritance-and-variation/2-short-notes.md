## 1. Heredity and variation

- **Inheritance:** characters pass from parents to offspring. It is the basis of heredity.
- **Variation:** how much offspring differ from their parents.
- People used this long before genetics: by selective breeding they made new crops and animal breeds, such as the Sahiwal cow of Punjab.

## 2. Mendel's experiments

- Gregor Mendel crossed garden peas for **7 years (1856–1863)**. His work was published in **1865** but ignored.
- In **1900** it was rediscovered, independently, by **de Vries, Correns and von Tschermak**.
- He succeeded because he used **true-breeding** lines, followed one or two traits at a time, counted large numbers of plants, and used statistics.
- He studied **7 pairs** of contrasting traits:

| Trait | Dominant | Recessive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stem height | Tall | Dwarf |
| Flower colour | Violet | White |
| Flower position | Axial | Terminal |
| Pod shape | Inflated | Constricted |
| Pod colour | Green | Yellow |
| Seed shape | Round | Wrinkled |
| Seed colour | Yellow | Green |

## 3. Words to know

- **Gene** (Mendel's "factor"): the unit that controls a trait.
- **Alleles:** different forms of one gene, such as T and t.
- **Homozygous:** both alleles the same (TT or tt). **Heterozygous:** different (Tt).
- **Genotype:** the alleles an organism has. **Phenotype:** the trait that is actually expressed and can be seen or tested (tall, round seed, blood group A).
- **Punnett square:** the grid for working out a cross, made by Reginald C. Punnett.

## 4. One trait at a time: Mendel's first two laws

A cross of tall (TT) with dwarf (tt) gives all tall plants in the F1. Selfing them gives 3 tall : 1 dwarf in the F2. The dwarf trait was hidden, not lost.

:::remember
- **Law of dominance:** traits are controlled by units (factors) that come in pairs. When the two differ, one (dominant) shows and the other (recessive) is hidden.
- **Law of segregation:** the two alleles never blend. They separate when gametes form, so each gamete gets **only one**.
:::

## 5. Dominance is not always complete

- **Incomplete dominance:** the heterozygote is in between. In snapdragon (dog flower, *Antirrhinum*), red (RR) × white (rr) gives **pink** (Rr). The F2 is 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.
- **Why one allele is dominant:** often the normal allele makes a working enzyme. A changed allele may make no enzyme or a useless one. One working copy makes enough enzyme, so the unmodified allele is dominant and the modified one is recessive.
- **Co-dominance:** both alleles show fully. In AB blood group, both A and B sugars are on the red blood cells.
- **Multiple alleles:** the ABO gene has 3 alleles ($I^A$, $I^B$, $i$), but one person carries only 2.
- **Dominance depends on the trait you look at.** In pea, BB makes large starch grains (round seeds) and bb small grains (wrinkled seeds). Bb seeds are round, but their starch grains are medium-sized. For seed shape, B is dominant. For grain size, it is incomplete dominance.

## 6. Two traits at a time: independent assortment

- Round yellow (RRYY) × wrinkled green (rryy) gives all round yellow in the F1, and **9 : 3 : 3 : 1** in the F2.
- Each trait on its own still gives 3 : 1 (12 round : 4 wrinkled, 12 yellow : 4 green), and 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 is just (3 : 1) × (3 : 1).
- New combinations (round green, wrinkled yellow) appear at the full 3/16 each. So the two gene pairs assort independently.

:::remember
**Law of independent assortment:** when two pairs of traits are combined in a hybrid, one pair segregates independently of the other.
:::

## 7. Genes are on chromosomes

- **Sutton and Boveri** (1902) noticed that chromosomes behave like Mendel's factors: they come in pairs and separate at meiosis. This is the **chromosomal theory of inheritance**.
- **Thomas Hunt Morgan** proved it with the fruit fly *Drosophila melanogaster*.
- Fruit flies are ideal: they can be grown on a simple synthetic medium in the lab, finish their life cycle in about **two weeks**, give many offspring from one mating, males and females look clearly different, and many inherited variations can be seen with a low-power microscope.

## 8. Linkage and recombination

- Morgan's dihybrid crosses gave far more parental types than 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 predicts, because the genes were on the **same chromosome** (the X).
- **Linkage:** genes on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together.
- **Recombination:** new, non-parental combinations, made by crossing over.
- Tightly linked genes rarely recombine: white eye and yellow body showed only **1.3%**. Genes far apart recombine often: white eye and miniature wing showed **37.2%**.
- **Alfred Sturtevant** used recombination frequencies to work out the positions of genes on a chromosome: the first gene maps.

## 9. Many genes, one trait; one gene, many traits

- **Polygenic inheritance:** a trait controlled by three or more genes, like human **skin colour** or height. The phenotype shows the effect of every allele added together, and the environment also plays a part. The result is a smooth range, not separate classes.
- **Pleiotropy:** one gene affects several traits. In **phenylketonuria**, a mutation in the single gene for the enzyme **phenylalanine hydroxylase** causes mental retardation and less pigment in the hair and skin.

## 10. Sex determination

- **Henking (1891)** traced a nuclear structure through sperm formation in a few insects: 50% of the sperm received it and 50% did not. He named it the **X body** but could not explain its significance. It was later shown to be the X chromosome.
- **Male heterogamety** (the male makes two kinds of sperm):
  - XO type: grasshopper. The female is XX, the male has only one X.
  - XY type: humans and fruit flies.
- **Female heterogamety:** birds. The female is ZW, the male ZZ.
- **Honeybee:** a fertilised egg becomes a female (diploid, 32 chromosomes) and an unfertilised egg becomes a male (haploid, 16 chromosomes) by parthenogenesis. This is the **haplodiploid** system. The female is a queen or a worker; the male (drone) is already haploid, so he makes sperm by **mitosis**.

## 11. Mutation

- A **mutation** is a change in the DNA sequence, which can change the phenotype.
- A **point mutation** changes a single base pair, as in sickle-cell anaemia.
- Inserting or deleting one or two base pairs shifts the reading frame (**frameshift mutation**). Inserting or deleting three, or a multiple of three, adds or removes whole codons, and the frame after it stays the same.
- **Mutagens** cause mutations: for example UV radiation and some chemicals.

## 12. Pedigree analysis

- A family tree that tracks a trait over several generations.
- Squares are males, circles are females, and filled symbols are affected people. A double line between two partners shows a marriage between relatives.
- It shows whether a trait is dominant or recessive, and whether it is autosomal or sex-linked.

## 13. Mendelian disorders (one gene)

- **Haemophilia** (X-linked recessive): one protein in the blood-clotting chain is faulty, so a small cut keeps bleeding. Each son of a carrier mother has a 50% chance of being haemophilic. A haemophilic female is extremely rare: her mother must be at least a carrier and her father haemophilic. It famously ran in Queen Victoria's descendants.
- **Colour blindness** (X-linked recessive): a fault in the red or green cones. About 8% of men but only about 0.4% of women have it. A daughter is colour-blind only if her father is colour-blind and her mother is at least a carrier.
- **Sickle-cell anaemia** (autosomal recessive): the faulty haemoglobin has **valine instead of glutamic acid at position 6** of the β-globin chain. It comes from a single base change in the sixth codon of the β-globin gene. NCERT writes this as **GAG → GUG**, and that is the form to use in the exam. (Strictly, GUG is the mRNA codon; on the DNA coding strand the change is GAG → GTG.) At low oxygen the red blood cells become sickle-shaped. Only $\text{Hb}^S\text{Hb}^S$ people show the disease; $\text{Hb}^A\text{Hb}^S$ people are carriers.
- **Phenylketonuria** (autosomal recessive; an inborn error of metabolism): the enzyme that converts phenylalanine into tyrosine is missing. Phenylalanine builds up and is converted into **phenylpyruvic acid** and other derivatives. These collect in the brain, causing mental retardation, and are also passed out in the urine.
- **Thalassemia** (autosomal recessive): too little globin is made.
  - α-thalassemia: two closely linked genes, HBA1 and HBA2, on chromosome **16** of each parent (four copies in all). It follows mutation or deletion of one or more of the four; the more copies affected, the less α-globin is made.
  - β-thalassemia: one gene, HBB, on chromosome **11** of each parent; mutation of one or both copies.

:::remember
**Thalassemia** is a problem of **quantity** (too few globin chains are made). **Sickle-cell anaemia** is a problem of **quality** (the globin made is faulty).
:::

## 14. Chromosomal disorders

- **Aneuploidy:** gain or loss of one or more chromosomes (not a whole set), when chromatids fail to separate during cell division. Examples: trisomy 21, and 45, X0.
- **Polyploidy:** whole extra sets of chromosomes, when cytokinesis fails after telophase. Common in plants.
- **Down syndrome:** an extra copy of chromosome **21** (trisomy 21). Short stature, a small round head, a furrowed tongue, a partly open mouth, a broad palm with a typical crease, and delayed physical and mental development. First described by Langdon Down in 1866.
- **Klinefelter syndrome:** **47, XXY**. A male who also shows some female features (breast development, called **gynaecomastia**) and is sterile.
- **Turner syndrome:** **45, X0** (one X missing). A female with rudimentary ovaries, who is sterile and lacks other secondary sexual characters.
