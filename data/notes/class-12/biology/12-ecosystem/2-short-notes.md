## 1. What is an ecosystem?

- An **ecosystem** is a functional unit of nature, where living organisms interact among themselves and with their physical environment.
- Size varies greatly: from a small pond to a large forest or a sea.
- Many ecologists treat the whole **biosphere** as a **global ecosystem**: all the local ecosystems on Earth put together.
- Two basic types: **terrestrial** and **aquatic**. Crop fields and an aquarium may also be treated as **man-made** ecosystems.

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Terrestrial | Forest, grassland, desert |
| Aquatic | Pond, lake, wetland, river, estuary |
| Man-made | Crop fields, aquarium |

## 2. Structure of an ecosystem

- Biotic and abiotic components interact to give each ecosystem its own **physical structure**.
- **Species composition:** identifying and counting (enumerating) the plant and animal species.
- **Stratification:** the vertical distribution of species in different layers. In a forest, **trees** form the top layer, **shrubs** the second, and **herbs and grasses** the bottom layers.

:::remember
The components of an ecosystem work as a unit in four ways: **productivity, decomposition, energy flow and nutrient cycling**.
:::

## 3. The pond: a simple example

A pond is a shallow water body that is fairly self-sustaining. It shows all four functions clearly.

| Component | In a pond |
| --- | --- |
| Abiotic | Water with dissolved inorganic and organic substances; rich soil at the bottom |
| Autotrophs | Phytoplankton, some algae; floating, submerged and marginal plants |
| Consumers | Zooplankton; free-swimming and bottom-dwelling forms |
| Decomposers | Fungi, bacteria and flagellates, mostly at the bottom |

- Solar input, the cycle of temperature, day-length and other climatic conditions regulate the rate of function of the whole pond.
- What the pond does, like any ecosystem:
  - autotrophs turn inorganic matter into organic matter using the sun's radiant energy;
  - heterotrophs eat the autotrophs;
  - dead matter is decomposed and mineralised, and the nutrients are reused by autotrophs.
- Energy moves **one way (unidirectional)** to the higher trophic levels and is lost as **heat**.

## 4. Productivity

- A constant input of **solar energy** is the basic need of every ecosystem.
- **Primary production:** the amount of biomass or organic matter made per unit area over a time period by plants during photosynthesis. Units: g m⁻² or kcal m⁻².
- **Productivity:** the **rate** of biomass production. Units: g m⁻² yr⁻¹ or kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹. These units let us compare ecosystems.
- **Gross primary productivity (GPP):** rate of production of organic matter during photosynthesis.
- Plants use a considerable part of GPP in respiration ($R$). What is left is the **net primary productivity (NPP)**:

$$\text{GPP} - R = \text{NPP}$$

- NPP is the biomass available to **heterotrophs** (herbivores and decomposers).
- **Secondary productivity:** the rate of formation of new organic matter by **consumers**.

**What primary productivity depends on:**
- the plant species living in the area;
- environmental factors;
- availability of nutrients;
- the photosynthetic capacity of the plants.

So it differs between ecosystems.

:::remember
- Annual NPP of the whole biosphere: about **170 billion tons** (dry weight) of organic matter.
- Oceans cover about **70%** of the surface but give only **55 billion tons**. The rest is from land.
:::

NCERT asks you to discuss why the ocean is so unproductive. The usual answer (beyond NCERT, not stated in the text): light reaches only the upper layers of water, and the open ocean is poor in nutrients.

## 5. Decomposition

- **Decomposition:** decomposers break down complex organic matter into inorganic substances like **carbon dioxide, water and nutrients**.
- The earthworm is the "farmer's friend": it breaks down organic matter and loosens the soil.
- **Detritus** is the raw material: dead leaves, bark, flowers, dead animal remains and fecal matter.

**The five steps** (they all go on **at the same time** on the detritus):

1. **Fragmentation:** detritivores (e.g. earthworm) break detritus into smaller particles.
2. **Leaching:** water-soluble inorganic nutrients go down into the soil horizon and are precipitated as **unavailable salts**.
3. **Catabolism:** bacterial and fungal **enzymes** degrade detritus into simpler inorganic substances.
4. **Humification:** a dark, amorphous substance called **humus** builds up. Humus is highly **resistant** to microbes and decomposes extremely slowly. Being **colloidal**, it is a reservoir of nutrients.
5. **Mineralisation:** some microbes degrade humus further and release inorganic nutrients.

Humification and mineralisation happen in the **soil**.

**What controls the rate:**
- Decomposition is largely an **oxygen-requiring** (aerobic) process.
- **Chemical composition of detritus:**
  - **slower** if rich in **lignin and chitin**;
  - **faster** if rich in **nitrogen** and water-soluble substances like **sugars**.
- **Climate:** temperature and soil moisture matter most, because they control soil microbes.
  - Warm and moist: decomposition is fast.
  - Low temperature and anaerobiosis (no oxygen): decomposition is slow, and organic matter builds up.

## 6. Energy flow

- Except for the **deep-sea hydrothermal ecosystem**, the **sun** is the only source of energy for all ecosystems.
- **PAR** (photosynthetically active radiation) is **less than 50%** of the incident solar radiation.
- Plants capture only **2–10% of the PAR**. This small amount sustains the whole living world.
- Energy flows **one way**: sun → producers → consumers.
- Ecosystems obey the **second law of thermodynamics**. They need a constant supply of energy to build molecules and to fight the universal tendency towards disorder.

**Producers and consumers:**
- **Producers:** green plants. On land, mainly herbaceous and woody plants. In water, phytoplankton, algae and higher plants.
- **Consumers (heterotrophs):** all animals depend on plants, directly or indirectly.
  - **Primary consumers** eat plants: they are **herbivores**. Examples: insects, birds and mammals on land; **molluscs** in water.
  - **Secondary consumers** eat herbivores: **primary carnivores**.
  - Animals that eat primary carnivores: **secondary carnivores**.

:::remember
NCERT's grazing food chain: **Grass** (producer) → **Goat** (primary consumer) → **Man** (secondary consumer).
:::

## 7. Food chains and food webs

| | Grazing food chain (GFC) | Detritus food chain (DFC) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starts with | Living plants (producers) | Dead organic matter |
| Main organisms | Herbivores, carnivores | Decomposers: fungi, bacteria |
| Main path in | **Aquatic** ecosystems | **Terrestrial** ecosystems |

- No energy stays in an organism for ever: it is passed to a consumer, or the organism dies. **Death is the start of the detritus food chain.**
- **Decomposers** are heterotrophs, mainly fungi and bacteria, also called **saprotrophs** (*sapro*: to decompose). They secrete digestive enzymes onto dead matter, break it into simple inorganic materials, and then absorb them.
- On land, a **much larger fraction** of energy flows through the DFC than through the GFC.
- The DFC joins the GFC at some levels: some DFC organisms are prey of GFC animals. Omnivores like **cockroaches and crows** link chains too.
- These natural links between food chains make a **food web**.

## 8. Trophic levels and standing crop

- **Trophic level:** the specific place an organism takes in the food chain, based on its source of food.
- Producers are the 1st level, herbivores the 2nd, carnivores the 3rd (and top carnivores the 4th in NCERT Fig. 12.2).
- Energy **decreases** at each higher trophic level. Each level depends on the one below it.
- When any organism dies, it becomes detritus, an energy source for decomposers.
- **Standing crop:** the mass of living material at a trophic level at a particular time. It is measured as **biomass** or as **number** per unit area.
- Biomass is given as fresh or dry weight. **Dry weight is more accurate** (NCERT asks why; the usual answer, beyond NCERT: water content varies a lot between organisms and with time).

:::remember
**10 per cent law:** only **10%** of the energy passes from one trophic level to the next. That is why a grazing food chain has only a few levels (producer, herbivore, primary carnivore, secondary carnivore).
:::

## 9. Ecological pyramids

- The relationship between trophic levels can be drawn as a pyramid of **number, biomass or energy**.
- **Base:** producers (1st trophic level). **Apex:** tertiary or top consumers.
- Counts must include **all** organisms at a trophic level, not just a few.
- A trophic level is a **functional level**, not a species. One species can be at more than one level at the same time. Example: a **sparrow** is a primary consumer when it eats seeds, fruits and peas, and a secondary consumer when it eats insects and worms.

**Upright or inverted?**
- In most ecosystems all three pyramids are **upright**.
- **Pyramid of numbers** can be inverted: count the many insects feeding on one big tree (one producer, many herbivores). NCERT then asks you to add the small birds that eat the insects, and the larger birds that eat them, and draw the shape you get.
- **Pyramid of biomass in the sea** is generally **inverted**: the biomass of fishes far exceeds that of phytoplankton. In NCERT Fig. 12.4 c, phytoplankton 4 support zooplankton 21.
- **Pyramid of energy** is **always upright**. It can never be inverted, because some energy is always lost as **heat** at each step. Each bar shows the energy at that level in a given time, or annually, per unit area.

**Limitations of ecological pyramids:**
- They don't take into account the same species belonging to two or more trophic levels.
- They assume a simple food chain, which almost never exists in nature, and do not accommodate a food web.
- **Saprophytes** (decomposers) get no place in them, though they play a vital role.

## 10. Lines found only in NCERT's summary

The current NCERT main text has no sections on these (it only lists nutrient cycling as one of the four functions), but the chapter summary mentions them:
- **Nutrient cycling:** storage and movement of nutrient elements through the ecosystem, so they are used again and again. Two types: **gaseous** (reservoir: atmosphere or hydrosphere; e.g. **carbon**) and **sedimentary** (reservoir: Earth's crust; e.g. **phosphorus**).
- **Ecosystem services:** products of ecosystem processes, e.g. purification of air and water by forests.
- The summary also words two things differently from the main text: decomposition has **three** processes (fragmentation, leaching, catabolism), and secondary productivity is the "rate of assimilation of food energy by the consumers". Learn the main-text versions (five steps; rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers).
