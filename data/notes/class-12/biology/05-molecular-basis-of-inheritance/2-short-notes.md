## 1. Nucleic acids and the polynucleotide chain

- **DNA** is the genetic material in most organisms; **RNA** in some viruses. RNA mostly works as a **messenger**, also as an **adapter**, a **structural** molecule and sometimes a **catalyst**.
- DNA length = number of nucleotides or base pairs (bp); sizes, sugars, bases and bonds are in the sheet.
- Nucleotides join by **3'–5' phosphodiester** bonds. The chain has a free phosphate at the **5' end** and a free 3'-OH at the **3' end**. Sugar–phosphate forms the **backbone**; bases project from it.
- RNA has an extra **2'-OH** on every ribose. Thymine is **5-methyl uracil**.

## 2. The double helix

- **Friedrich Meischer (1869)** found DNA as an acidic substance in the nucleus: **"nuclein"**.
- **Watson and Crick (1953)** proposed the **double helix** from the X-ray diffraction data of **Wilkins** and **Franklin**. Its hallmark is **base pairing**. It also used **Chargaff's** finding that in double-stranded DNA, A/T and G/C are constant and equal to **one**.
- Features:
  - Two chains, sugar–phosphate backbone outside, bases inside, **anti-parallel** (5'→3' and 3'→5').
  - **A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C (3 H-bonds).** A purine always faces a pyrimidine, so the strands stay an almost uniform distance apart.
  - **Right-handed**; pitch **3.4 nm**, about **10 bp per turn**, about **0.34 nm** per bp.
  - Base pairs **stack**; stacking plus H-bonds give stability. The strands are **complementary**.

:::remember
**Central dogma** (Francis Crick): DNA → RNA → protein. In some viruses information flows from RNA to DNA (reverse transcription).
:::

## 3. Packaging of DNA

- Mammalian cell: $6.6 \times 10^9$ bp × 0.34 nm ≈ **2.2 m** of DNA, in a nucleus about $10^{-6}$ m across.
- **Prokaryotes** (*E. coli*): no defined nucleus. Negative DNA is held by positive proteins in the **nucleoid**, in large loops.
- **Eukaryotes:**
  - **Histones**: positive, basic proteins rich in **lysine** and **arginine**. Eight form a **histone octamer**.
  - DNA wraps the octamer to form a **nucleosome** (typically **200 bp**). Nucleosomes repeat along **chromatin** and look like **"beads-on-string"** in the electron microscope.
  - Chromatin fibres condense into chromosomes at **metaphase**; higher packing needs **non-histone chromosomal (NHC) proteins**.
  - **Euchromatin:** loose, stains light, transcriptionally **active**. **Heterochromatin:** dense, stains dark, **inactive**.

## 4. Search for the genetic material

**Griffith (1928), *Streptococcus pneumoniae*:** S strain: mucous (polysaccharide) coat, smooth colonies, **virulent**. R strain: no coat, rough, harmless.

| Injected into mice | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Live S | Mice die |
| Live R | Mice live |
| Heat-killed S | Mice live |
| Heat-killed S + live R | Mice die; live S recovered |

A **"transforming principle"** from heat-killed S changed R into S. Its chemical nature was not found.

**Avery, MacLeod and McCarty (1933–44):** before them, genetic material was thought to be **protein**. DNA alone from S transformed R; **proteases** and **RNases** did not stop it, **DNase** did. Not all biologists were convinced.

**Hershey and Chase (1952), the unequivocal proof:**
- Phages grown with **³²P** got radioactive **DNA**; with **³⁵S**, radioactive **protein**.
- Infect *E. coli* → **blend** (removes viral coats) → **centrifuge**. ³²P was **inside** the bacteria; ³⁵S was not. So DNA enters the cell: DNA is the genetic material.

## 5. Properties of genetic material

It must: (1) **replicate**; (2) be chemically and structurally **stable**; (3) allow slow changes (**mutation**) for evolution; (4) express itself as **Mendelian characters**.

| Point | DNA | RNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stability | More stable | 2'-OH makes it labile |
| Reactivity | Less | Catalytic, reactive |
| Mutation | Slower | Faster |
| Expression | Needs RNA | Codes protein directly |

- Proteins fail test 1. Griffith's heat-killed cells showed DNA's stability: heat-separated strands rejoin. Thymine (not uracil) adds stability.
- DNA is better for **storing** information; RNA for **transmitting** it. RNA genomes: Tobacco Mosaic Virus, QB bacteriophage.

## 6. RNA world

**RNA was the first genetic material**; metabolism, translation and splicing evolved around it. Being catalytic, RNA was reactive and unstable, so **DNA evolved from RNA** with stabilising changes; double-stranded DNA also resists change by **repair**.

## 7. Replication

Watson and Crick proposed **semiconservative** replication: each new DNA has one parental and one new strand.

**Meselson and Stahl (1958), *E. coli*:** grown for many generations in $\ce{^{15}NH4Cl}$ (¹⁵N is heavy, **not radioactive**), then moved to $\ce{^{14}NH4Cl}$. DNA was separated by density in a **CsCl gradient**. *E. coli* divides every **20 minutes**.

| Time in ¹⁴N | DNA found |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | All heavy |
| 20 min (gen I) | All hybrid |
| 40 min (gen II) | Half hybrid, half light |

**Taylor and colleagues (1958)** showed the same in *Vicia faba* chromosomes using **radioactive thymidine**.

**Machinery:**
- **DNA-dependent DNA polymerase**, fast and accurate: *E. coli* ($4.6 \times 10^6$ bp) replicates in **18 minutes**, about **2000 bp/s** (NCERT's figure).
- **dNTPs** (deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates) are both **substrate** and **energy source**.
- Replication occurs at a small opening, the **replication fork**.
- Polymerase works only **5'→3'**: **continuous** on the template with polarity **3'→5'**, **discontinuous** on the template with polarity **5'→3'**. **DNA ligase** joins the pieces.
- Polymerase cannot initiate on its own; replication begins at a definite **origin of replication**. So cloned DNA needs a **vector**, which supplies the origin.
- Eukaryotes replicate in **S phase**. Replication without cell division gives **polyploidy**.

## 8. Transcription

- One strand of a DNA segment is copied into RNA; A pairs with **U**. Copying both would give two different proteins from one segment, and the two RNAs would pair into untranslatable double-stranded RNA.

**Transcription unit:** **promoter**, **structural gene**, **terminator**.
- RNA polymerase also works 5'→3', so the strand with polarity **3'→5'** is the **template strand**.
- The other strand (5'→3') has the **same sequence as RNA** (T for U): the **coding strand**, though it codes for nothing. All reference points use it.
- **Promoter:** upstream (5' end of the coding strand); binds RNA polymerase and defines which strand is the template. **Terminator:** downstream (3' end); marks the end.

**Gene and cistron:**
- **Cistron:** DNA coding for one polypeptide. **Monocistronic:** mostly eukaryotes; **polycistronic:** mostly bacteria.
- Eukaryotic genes are **split**: **exons** (kept in mature RNA) are interrupted by **introns** (removed).
- Regulatory sequences are loosely called **regulatory genes**, though they make no RNA or protein.

**In bacteria:** **mRNA** (template), **tRNA** (brings amino acids, reads the code), **rRNA** (structural, catalytic). **One** RNA polymerase makes all; it only **elongates**, joining **σ** to initiate and **ρ** to terminate. No processing and no nuclear membrane, so **transcription and translation can be coupled**.

**In eukaryotes:**
- At least three nuclear RNA polymerases: **I** (28S, 18S, 5.8S rRNA), **II** (**hnRNA**, precursor of mRNA), **III** (tRNA, 5S rRNA, snRNAs).
- hnRNA is processed: **splicing** (introns out, exons joined in order); **capping** (methyl guanosine triphosphate at the **5' end**); **tailing** (**200–300 adenylates** at the **3' end**, template-independent). Then mRNA leaves the nucleus.
- Split genes are probably an **ancient** feature; splicing reflects the dominance of the **RNA world**.

## 9. Genetic code

**George Gamow** (a physicist) argued 4 bases for 20 amino acids need a **triplet** code ($4^3 = 64$). **Khorana** (chemical RNA synthesis), **Nirenberg** (cell-free system) and **Ochoa** (polynucleotide phosphorylase) helped decipher it.

**Features:**
- **Triplet:** 61 codons for amino acids; 3 **stop** codons (UAA, UAG, UGA).
- **Degenerate:** some amino acids have several codons.
- Read **contiguously**, with **no punctuation**.
- **Nearly universal** (UUU = phenylalanine, bacteria to humans); exceptions in mitochondria and some protozoans.
- **AUG** = methionine and the **initiator** codon.

**Mutations:**
- **Point mutation:** one base-pair change. In β-globin, glutamate → valine gives **sickle-cell anaemia**.
- Insertion or deletion of **1 or 2 bases** shifts the frame: **frameshift** (RAM HAS RED CAP → RAM HAS BRE DCA P).
- Insertion or deletion of **3 bases** (or a multiple) adds or removes whole codons; the frame after it is unchanged.

**tRNA, the adapter** (postulated by **Crick**; once called **sRNA**): **anticodon loop** + **amino acid acceptor end**; specific for each amino acid; a special **initiator tRNA**; **none for stop codons**. **Clover-leaf** secondary structure; **inverted L** in 3D.

## 10. Translation

- Amino acids join by **peptide bonds** in mRNA order. First, **charging (aminoacylation)**: each amino acid is activated by **ATP** and linked to its own tRNA.
- **Ribosome:** structural RNAs + about **80 proteins**, in large and small subunits. Translation begins when the small subunit meets mRNA. The large subunit has **two sites** for amino acids. **23S rRNA** (bacteria) makes the peptide bond: a **ribozyme**.
- **Translational unit:** start codon (AUG) to stop codon. **UTRs** at both 5' and 3' ends are not translated but needed for efficient translation.
- **Initiation** at AUG (read only by the initiator tRNA) → **elongation** (charged tRNAs pair by anticodon; ribosome moves codon by codon) → **termination** (a **release factor** binds the stop codon, polypeptide released).

## 11. Regulation of gene expression

- Eukaryotes: at **transcription**, **processing** (splicing), **mRNA transport** out of the nucleus, and **translation**.
- Prokaryotes: mainly the **rate of transcription initiation**. Accessory proteins act as **activators** (positive) or **repressors** (negative). A repressor binds the **operator**, next to the promoter; each operon has its own operator and repressor.
- **Operon:** polycistronic structural gene with a common promoter and regulatory genes (lac, trp, ara, his, val).

**Lac operon (Jacob and Monod):**
- **i** (from "inhibitor") makes the **repressor**, **constitutively**. Structural genes **z, y, a** (products in the sheet).
- **No inducer:** repressor binds the operator, so RNA polymerase cannot transcribe the operon.
- **Lactose, no glucose:** lactose enters by permease (a very low level of the operon is always expressed). The **inducer** (lactose or **allolactose**) inactivates the repressor; transcription proceeds.
- Regulation of enzyme synthesis by its **substrate**. Repressor control is **negative regulation**; NCERT notes positive regulation too, without details.

## 12. Human Genome Project (HGP)

- **1990–2003**, coordinated by the US **Department of Energy** and **National Institute of Health**; **Wellcome Trust** (UK) a major partner, with Japan, France, Germany, China and others.
- A **mega project**: estimated **9 billion US dollars** (3 dollars per bp); one cell's sequence would fill **3300 books**. It drove **bioinformatics**.
- **Goals:** identify the approximately **20,000–25,000 genes**; sequence the **3 billion** bp; store the data; improve analysis tools; transfer technology to industry; address **ELSI** (ethical, legal, social issues).
- **Methods:** **ESTs** (Expressed Sequence Tags) find genes expressed as RNA; **sequence annotation** sequences everything, then assigns functions.
- Random fragments are cloned in bacteria and yeast (**BAC**, **YAC** vectors), read by automated sequencers (**Sanger's** method) and aligned by computer from overlaps. **Chromosome 1** finished last (**May 2006**). Maps used restriction-site polymorphism and **microsatellites**.
- **Salient features** (numbers in the sheet): about **30,000** genes (earlier estimates 80,000–1,40,000); over 50% of functions unknown; **repeated sequences** form a large part, with no direct coding role, but shed light on chromosome structure and evolution; **SNPs** ("snips") help locate disease genes and trace human history.
- Also sequenced: bacteria, yeast, *Caenorhabditis elegans*, *Drosophila*, rice, *Arabidopsis*.

## 13. DNA fingerprinting

- Compares two people's DNA quickly, without full sequencing, using **repetitive DNA**.
- In density gradient centrifugation, bulk DNA forms the major peak; small peaks are **satellite DNA** (micro-, mini-satellites): normally non-coding, a large part of the genome, highly **polymorphic**.
- **Polymorphism** arises by mutation: a variant at a locus with population frequency **greater than 0.01**. It is more likely in non-coding DNA.
- **Alec Jeffreys** used **VNTR** (Variable Number of Tandem Repeats), a **mini-satellite**, as the probe. VNTR size: **0.1 to 20 kb**.

**Steps (Southern blot hybridisation):** isolate DNA → digest with **restriction endonucleases** → **electrophoresis** → **blot** onto nitrocellulose or nylon → **hybridise** with labelled VNTR probe → detect by **autoradiography**.

- Band patterns differ between people, except **monozygotic (identical) twins**, and are the same in every tissue (blood, hair follicle, skin, bone, saliva, sperm). With **PCR**, a **single cell** is enough.
- Uses: **forensics**, **paternity** tests (polymorphisms are inherited), population and genetic **diversity** studies.
