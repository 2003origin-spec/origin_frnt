## 1. What biotechnology does

- Biotechnology is the **industrial-scale** production of biopharmaceuticals and biologicals using genetically modified microbes, fungi, plants and animals.
- Its uses include therapeutics, diagnostics, GM crops, processed food, bioremediation, waste treatment and energy production.
- **Three critical research areas:**
  1. the best catalyst: an improved organism (usually a microbe) or a pure enzyme;
  2. optimal conditions, made by engineering, for the catalyst to act;
  3. downstream processing to purify the protein or organic compound.

## 2. Ways to grow more food

- Three options: **agro-chemical based**, **organic**, and **genetically engineered crop-based** agriculture.
- The **Green Revolution** tripled the food supply, but it was still not enough for the growing population.
- Higher yields came partly from improved varieties, but **mainly** from better management and **agrochemicals** (fertilisers and pesticides).
- For farmers in developing countries, agrochemicals are often too costly. Conventional breeding could not raise yields of existing varieties any further.

## 3. Plant tissue culture

- In the **1950s** scientists learnt that whole plants can be regenerated from **explants**: any plant part grown in a test tube, under sterile conditions, in a special nutrient medium.
- **Totipotency:** the capacity of any cell or explant to form a whole plant.
- The **medium** must give a carbon source (such as **sucrose**), inorganic salts, vitamins, amino acids, and growth regulators such as **auxins and cytokinins**.

### Micro-propagation

- Producing **thousands of plants** in a very short time by tissue culture.
- All the plants are genetically identical to the original plant: they are **somaclones**.
- Grown on a commercial scale for **tomato, banana, apple**, etc.

### Virus-free plants

- Even in a virus-infected plant, the **meristem (apical and axillary)** is free of virus.
- So the meristem is removed and grown **in vitro** to get virus-free plants.
- Done for **banana, sugarcane, potato**, etc.

### Somatic hybridisation

- Single plant cells are isolated and their **cell walls digested**. This gives **naked protoplasts**, surrounded only by the plasma membrane.
- Protoplasts of two varieties, each with a useful character, are **fused** to make hybrid protoplasts. These are grown into a new plant, a **somatic hybrid**.
- Example: tomato + potato protoplasts gave the **pomato**. It did **not** have all the desired characters for commercial use.

## 4. Genetically modified organisms

- **GMOs:** plants, bacteria, fungi and animals whose genes have been altered by manipulation.
- GM plants have:
  1. become more tolerant to **abiotic stresses** (cold, drought, salt, heat);
  2. reduced reliance on **chemical pesticides** (pest-resistant crops);
  3. helped to reduce **post-harvest losses**;
  4. used **minerals more efficiently** (so soil fertility is not used up early);
  5. improved **nutritional value**, e.g. **golden rice** (vitamin A enriched rice).
- GM is also used to make tailor-made plants that supply industries with starches, fuels and pharmaceuticals.

## 5. Bt crops

- **Bt toxin** comes from the soil bacterium ***Bacillus thuringiensis***. Its gene was cloned and expressed in plants, which then resist insects **without insecticides**. In effect, this is a **bio-pesticide**.
- Examples: **Bt cotton, Bt corn**, rice, tomato, potato, soyabean.
- Some strains kill **lepidopterans** (tobacco budworm, armyworm), **coleopterans** (beetles) and **dipterans** (flies, mosquitoes).

### How the toxin works

1. The bacterium forms **protein crystals** in a particular phase of growth. They hold the toxin as an **inactive protoxin**, so the bacterium itself is not harmed.
2. An insect eats the crystals. The **alkaline pH** of its gut dissolves them and turns the protoxin into the **active toxin**.
3. The active toxin binds to the surface of **midgut epithelial cells** and makes **pores**.
4. The cells swell and **lyse**, and the insect dies.

### Cry genes

- The toxin is coded by genes called ***cry***. Most Bt toxins are **insect-group specific**, so the gene is chosen to suit the crop and the pest.
- *cryIAc* and *cryIIAb*: control **cotton bollworms**.
- *cryIAb*: controls **corn borer**.

## 6. Pest-resistant plants by RNAi

- The nematode ***Meloidegyne incognitia*** (NCERT's spelling; usually written *Meloidogyne incognita*) infects **tobacco roots** and greatly cuts yield.
- **RNA interference (RNAi)** happens in **all eukaryotes** as a method of **cellular defence**. A complementary **dsRNA** binds a specific mRNA and stops its translation (**silencing**).
- In nature, the complementary RNA may come from **RNA viruses** or from **transposons** that copy themselves through an RNA intermediate.
- Method:
  - nematode-specific genes were put into tobacco using ***Agrobacterium*** vectors;
  - the plant made both **sense and anti-sense RNA** of these genes;
  - the two strands paired to form **dsRNA**, which started RNAi and silenced the **nematode's** mRNA;
  - the parasite could not survive in the transgenic host, so the plant was protected.

## 7. Recombinant therapeutics

- rDNA technology allows mass production of **safe and more effective** drugs.
- Recombinant therapeutics are identical to human proteins, so they **do not cause unwanted immune responses**, and they carry no risk of infection. Products taken from non-human sources can cause both.
- About **30** recombinant therapeutics are approved worldwide; **12** are marketed in India.

## 8. Genetically engineered insulin

- Insulin is taken at regular intervals to manage **adult-onset diabetes** (NCERT's term).
- Earlier, insulin came from the **pancreas of slaughtered cattle and pigs**. Some patients had **allergy** or other reactions to this foreign protein.
- Insulin has two short polypeptide chains, **A and B**, linked by **disulphide bridges**.
- In mammals it is made as a **pro-hormone (pro-insulin)** with an extra stretch, the **C peptide**. The C peptide is removed during maturation and is not in mature insulin.
- The main challenge was getting insulin assembled into its **mature form**.
- **1983, Eli Lilly** (an American company):
  1. prepared two DNA sequences for chains **A and B** of human insulin;
  2. put them into **plasmids of *E. coli***;
  3. made chains A and B **separately**, extracted them, and joined them by **disulphide bonds** to form human insulin.
- Insulin made in bacteria has a structure **identical** to the natural molecule.

## 9. Gene therapy

- A set of methods to correct a gene defect diagnosed in a **child or embryo**. Genes are inserted into a person's cells and tissues to treat a disease.
- A **normal gene** is delivered to take over the function of, and make up for, the non-functional gene.

### ADA deficiency

- **First clinical gene therapy: 1990**, to a **4-year-old girl** with **adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency**.
- ADA is crucial for the **immune system**. The disorder is caused by **deletion** of the ADA gene.
- Other treatments: **bone marrow transplantation** or **enzyme replacement therapy** (functional ADA given by injection). Neither is completely curative.
- Gene therapy steps:
  1. **lymphocytes** from the patient's blood are grown in culture outside the body;
  2. a functional **ADA cDNA** is put into them using a **retroviral vector**;
  3. the cells are returned to the patient.
- These lymphocytes are **not immortal**, so the patient needs **periodic infusions**.
- If the ADA gene from marrow cells is put into cells at **early embryonic stages**, it could be a **permanent cure**.
- NCERT summary: viruses that put their genetic material into host cells are used as **vectors** to carry healthy genes (or parts of genes).

## 10. Molecular diagnosis

- Normal tests (serum, urine) cannot detect a disease early. A pathogen is usually suspected only after symptoms appear, when its numbers are already high.
- Early diagnosis uses **rDNA technology, PCR and ELISA**.
- **PCR:** amplifies the nucleic acid of a pathogen present in **very low** amounts, before symptoms show. Used routinely to detect **HIV** in suspected AIDS patients, and to find **mutations** in suspected cancer patients and other genetic disorders.
- **Probe:** a single-stranded DNA or RNA tagged with a **radioactive** molecule. It hybridises with its complementary DNA in a clone of cells, then is detected by **autoradiography**. A clone with the **mutated** gene does **not** show on the film, because the probe is not complementary to it.
- **ELISA:** based on **antigen–antibody interaction**. It detects either the pathogen's **antigens** (proteins, glycoproteins) or the **antibodies** made against it.

## 11. Transgenic animals

- Animals whose DNA has been manipulated to carry and express an extra (**foreign**) gene.
- Transgenic rats, rabbits, pigs, sheep, cows and fish exist, but **over 95%** of all transgenic animals are **mice**.

| Use | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Normal physiology and development | Insulin-like growth factor |
| Study of disease (models) | Cancer, cystic fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis, Alzheimer's |
| Biological products | α-1-antitrypsin (emphysema); Rosie's milk |
| Vaccine safety | Polio vaccine, tested in mice |
| Chemical safety testing | Toxicity tests |

- **Biological products:** attempts are also being made for PKU and cystic fibrosis. In **1997**, **Rosie**, the first transgenic cow, gave milk with human protein (**2.4 g per litre**). It had human **alpha-lactalbumin** and was nutritionally more balanced for babies than natural cow milk.
- **Vaccine safety:** if reliable, transgenic mice could **replace monkeys** for testing batches of polio vaccine.
- **Chemical safety:** the animals carry genes that make them **more sensitive** to toxic substances, so results come in **less time**.

## 12. Ethical issues

- Manipulating living organisms needs **regulation** and ethical standards. GM organisms can have **unpredictable** results when released into the ecosystem.
- India set up **GEAC (Genetic Engineering Approval Committee)**. It decides on the validity of GM research and the safety of introducing GM organisms for public services.

### Patents and biopiracy

- There is public anger that companies get patents on products that use genetic materials and bio-resources long developed and used by **farmers and indigenous people**.
- **Basmati:** India has about **200,000** rice varieties and **27** documented Basmati varieties. In **1997** an **American company** got a patent on Basmati through the **US Patent and Trademark Office**. Its "new" variety came from Indian farmers' varieties crossed with **semi-dwarf** varieties. The patent covers functional equivalents, so others selling Basmati could be restricted.
- Attempts have also been made to patent uses of Indian herbal medicines, e.g. **turmeric, neem**.
- **Biopiracy:** use of bio-resources by multinational companies and other organisations **without proper authorisation** from the countries and people concerned, and **without compensatory payment**.
- Industrialised nations are rich in money but **poor in biodiversity** and traditional knowledge. Developing and underdeveloped nations are **rich** in biodiversity and traditional knowledge.
- Some nations are making laws against such exploitation. The **Indian Parliament** has cleared the **second amendment of the Indian Patents Bill**, which takes these issues into account.
