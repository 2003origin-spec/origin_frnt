## 1. The carbonyl group

- **Aldehyde:** C=O bonded to one carbon (or H) **and** one H, $\ce{RCHO}$. **Ketone:** C=O bonded to two carbons, $\ce{RCOR'}$. **Carboxylic acid:** C=O bonded to –OH, $\ce{RCOOH}$.
- The carbonyl carbon is **sp²**. It and the three atoms on it lie in one plane, with bond angles of about 120°. The π bond lies above and below that plane.
- Oxygen is more electronegative, so C=O is **polar**: the carbon is an electrophile (Lewis acid), the oxygen a nucleophile (Lewis base). Carbonyl compounds are more polar than ethers.

## 2. Naming

- **Common names:** aldehydes are named after the matching acid (formic acid → formaldehyde). Substituent positions use Greek letters: α is the carbon next to C=O, then β, γ. Ketones name the two groups: methyl n-propyl ketone. Alkyl phenyl ketones end in -phenone (acetophenone).
- **IUPAC:** replace the -e of the alkane by **-al** (aldehyde) or **-one** (ketone). Aldehydes are numbered from the CHO carbon (C-1). Ketones are numbered from the end nearer to C=O.
- A CHO group on a ring: add **-carbaldehyde** to the ring's name. $\ce{C6H5CHO}$ is benzenecarbaldehyde; IUPAC also accepts **benzaldehyde**.
- **Acids:** replace -e by **-oic acid**; the COOH carbon is C-1.

| Formula | Common name | IUPAC name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ce{CH2=CHCHO}$ | Acrolein | Prop-2-enal |
| $\ce{(CH3)2CHCHO}$ | Isobutyraldehyde | 2-Methylpropanal |
| $\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCOCH3}$ | Mesityl oxide | 4-Methylpent-3-en-2-one |
| $\ce{HOOC-COOH}$ | Oxalic acid | Ethanedioic acid |
| $\ce{HOOC(CH2)4COOH}$ | Adipic acid | Hexanedioic acid |

## 3. Preparing aldehydes and ketones

| Starting material | Reagent | Gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1° alcohol | PCC, or Cu/Ag (hot) | Aldehyde |
| 2° alcohol | $\ce{CrO3}$, or Cu/Ag (hot) | Ketone |
| Acyl chloride | $\ce{H2}$, Pd–$\ce{BaSO4}$ (Rosenmund) | Aldehyde |
| Nitrile | $\ce{SnCl2}$/HCl, then $\ce{H3O+}$ (Stephen) | Aldehyde |
| Nitrile or ester | DIBAL-H, then $\ce{H2O}$ | Aldehyde |
| Toluene | $\ce{CrO2Cl2}$ (Etard) | Benzaldehyde |
| Benzene | CO + HCl, anhyd. $\ce{AlCl3}$/CuCl | Benzaldehyde |
| Acyl chloride | $\ce{R2Cd}$ | Ketone |
| Nitrile | RMgX, then $\ce{H3O+}$ | Ketone |
| Benzene | RCOCl, anhyd. $\ce{AlCl3}$ | Aryl ketone |

Also: ozonolysis of alkenes (both), hydration of alkynes (only ethyne gives an aldehyde), and toluene → benzaldehyde by $\ce{CrO3}$ in acetic anhydride or via benzal chloride (commercial).

## 4. Physical properties

- Methanal is a gas, ethanal a volatile liquid; the rest are liquids or solids at room temperature.
- **Boiling points:** higher than hydrocarbons and ethers of similar mass (dipole–dipole attraction), lower than alcohols (no hydrogen bonding between their own molecules).
- NCERT's set (mass 58–60): n-butane 273 K < methoxyethane 281 K < propanal 322 K < acetone 329 K < propan-1-ol 370 K.
- Methanal, ethanal and propanone mix with water in all proportions (they hydrogen-bond **with water**). Solubility falls fast as the chain grows.
- Small aldehydes smell sharp; bigger ones smell more fragrant.

## 5. Nucleophilic addition

- The nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon from roughly **perpendicular** to the plane of its sp² orbitals. The carbon becomes **sp³**, giving a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate, which then picks up $\ce{H+}$.
- **Aldehydes react faster than ketones**, for two reasons:
  - steric: two groups on a ketone block the nucleophile more;
  - electronic: two alkyl groups push in more electron density, making the carbon less positive.
- Benzaldehyde is **less** reactive than propanal: resonance with the ring reduces the polarity of C=O.

## 6. Addition reactions to know

- **HCN** gives cyanohydrins (base catalysed). Useful synthetic intermediates.
- **$\ce{NaHSO3}$** gives a crystalline addition compound, used to **separate and purify aldehydes**.
- **Grignard reagent**, then hydrolysis: methanal gives a 1° alcohol, other aldehydes 2°, ketones 3° (Unit 7).
- **Alcohols** (dry HCl): aldehyde → hemiacetal → acetal. Ketone + ethylene glycol → cyclic ketal. Dry HCl protonates the carbonyl oxygen and makes the carbon more electrophilic.
- **Ammonia derivatives** ($\ce{H2N-Z}$): addition, then loss of water, gives $\ce{>C=N-Z}$ (oxime, hydrazone, semicarbazone and so on).

## 7. Reduction

- $\ce{NaBH4}$, $\ce{LiAlH4}$ or $\ce{H2}$/catalyst: aldehyde → 1° alcohol; ketone → 2° alcohol.
- C=O → $\ce{CH2}$: **Clemmensen** (Zn–Hg, conc. HCl) or **Wolff–Kishner** ($\ce{NH2NH2}$, then KOH in ethylene glycol, heat).

## 8. Oxidation and tests

- Aldehydes oxidise easily to acids, even with **mild** reagents. Ketones need strong reagents and heat, and their C–C bonds break.

| Test | Positive result | Given by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tollens' reagent | Silver mirror | Aldehydes (incl. aromatic) |
| Fehling's solution | Red-brown $\ce{Cu2O}$ | Aliphatic aldehydes |
| $\ce{I2}$ + NaOH | Yellow $\ce{CHI3}$ | $\ce{CH3CO}$–, $\ce{CH3CH(OH)}$– |
| 2,4-DNP | Yellow/orange/red solid | Aldehydes and ketones |

## 9. Reactions due to α-hydrogen

- α-H atoms are **acidic**: C=O pulls electrons away, and the anion left behind (the conjugate base) is stabilised by resonance.
- **Aldol:** with **dilute** alkali, an aldehyde or ketone with at least one α-H gives a β-hydroxy aldehyde (aldol) or β-hydroxy ketone (ketol). On heating it loses water to give an **α,β-unsaturated** carbonyl compound. The whole process is aldol condensation.
- **Cross aldol:** two different carbonyl compounds. If **both** have α-H, you get **four** products (ethanal + propanal gives but-2-enal, 2-methylpent-2-enal, 2-methylbut-2-enal and pent-2-enal). A ketone can be one partner (benzaldehyde + acetophenone → benzalacetophenone).

## 10. Cannizzaro and ring substitution

- **Cannizzaro:** an aldehyde with **no α-H** + **concentrated** alkali, heated. Half is reduced to the alcohol, half oxidised to the acid salt (disproportionation). Examples: HCHO, $\ce{C6H5CHO}$.
- In aromatic aldehydes and ketones, C=O **deactivates** the ring and directs **meta** (benzaldehyde → m-nitrobenzaldehyde).

## 11. Uses (aldehydes and ketones)

- **Formalin** (40% methanal): preserves biological specimens; methanal also makes bakelite and urea–formaldehyde glues.
- Ethanal: starting material for acetic acid, ethyl acetate, vinyl acetate, polymers, drugs.
- Benzaldehyde: perfumes, dyes. Acetone and ethyl methyl ketone: industrial solvents.

## 12. Carboxylic acids: structure and preparation

- Fatty acids ($\ce{C12}$–$\ce{C18}$) occur in fats as glycerol esters.
- Bonds to the carboxyl carbon lie in one plane, about 120° apart. The carboxyl carbon is **less electrophilic** than a carbonyl carbon, because the –OH lone pair is in resonance with C=O.
- **Preparation:**
  - 1° alcohol + $\ce{KMnO4}$ or $\ce{CrO3}$/$\ce{H2SO4}$ (Jones reagent); aldehydes with mild oxidants;
  - alkylbenzene + $\ce{KMnO4}$ or chromic acid → benzoic acid (whole side chain);
  - nitriles → amides → acids (acid or base);
  - Grignard reagent + $\ce{CO2}$ (dry ice), then acid;
  - hydrolysis of acyl halides, anhydrides and esters (basic hydrolysis gives the carboxylate; acidify to get the acid).

## 13. Physical properties of acids

- Up to $\ce{C9}$: colourless liquids with unpleasant smells. Higher acids: waxy, nearly odourless solids.
- They boil **higher than alcohols** of similar mass, because their hydrogen bonding is more extensive. Most exist as **dimers** in the vapour phase and in aprotic solvents.
- Up to four carbons: miscible with water. Benzoic acid is nearly insoluble in cold water.

## 14. Acidity

- Carboxylic acids are weaker than mineral acids but stronger than alcohols and most simple phenols. They release $\ce{CO2}$ from carbonates and hydrogencarbonates; simple phenols such as phenol don't.
- **Why stronger than phenol:** the carboxylate ion has two **equivalent** resonance structures with the negative charge on two **oxygen** atoms. Phenoxide's structures are not equivalent and put the charge on less electronegative **carbon** atoms.
- **Electron-withdrawing groups** stabilise the carboxylate ion and **raise** acidity. **Electron-donating groups** lower it.
- Effect of the group (increasing acidity): Ph < I < Br < Cl < F < CN < $\ce{NO2}$ < $\ce{CF3}$. More halogens, and halogens closer to COOH, give stronger acids.
- Phenyl or vinyl attached **directly** to COOH raises acidity, because the **sp²** carbon is more electronegative. So benzoic acid ($\text{p}K_a$ 4.19) is stronger than acetic acid (4.76).
- On a benzene ring, acid strength: 4-nitrobenzoic acid ($\text{p}K_a$ 3.41) > benzoic acid (4.19) > 4-methoxybenzoic acid (4.46).

NCERT's order of decreasing acid strength (selected):

$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CF3COOH > CCl3COOH > CHCl2COOH} \\ & \ce{> FCH2COOH > ClCH2COOH} \\ & \ce{> BrCH2COOH > HCOOH} \\ & \ce{> C6H5COOH > CH3COOH} \\ & \ce{> CH3CH2COOH} \end{aligned}$$

## 15. Reactions of carboxylic acids

- **O–H bond breaks:** salts with metals ($\ce{H2}$ given off), alkalis, carbonates and hydrogencarbonates.
- **C–OH bond breaks:**
  - anhydride: heat with $\ce{H2SO4}$ or $\ce{P2O5}$;
  - ester: alcohol or phenol + conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ or HCl gas (nucleophilic acyl substitution);
  - acid chloride: $\ce{PCl5}$, $\ce{PCl3}$ or $\ce{SOCl2}$ (best, because its by-products are gases);
  - amide: $\ce{NH3}$ gives the ammonium salt, which loses water on strong heating. Phthalic acid gives phthalamide, then phthalimide.
- **–COOH group:** reduced to a 1° alcohol by $\ce{LiAlH4}$ or, better, $\ce{B2H6}$ (not by $\ce{NaBH4}$). Decarboxylation with soda lime gives an alkane with one carbon fewer. Kolbe electrolysis gives an alkane with twice the alkyl carbons.
- **Hydrocarbon part:** HVZ α-halogenation ($\ce{Cl2}$ or $\ce{Br2}$, red P). On a ring, COOH deactivates and directs **meta**. Aromatic acids do **not** undergo Friedel–Crafts: COOH deactivates the ring and binds the $\ce{AlCl3}$.

## 16. Uses (acids)

- Methanoic acid: rubber, textile, dyeing, leather, electroplating. Ethanoic acid: solvent, vinegar.
- Hexanedioic (adipic) acid: nylon-6,6. Sodium benzoate: food preservative. Benzoate esters: perfumes. Higher fatty acids: soaps and detergents.
