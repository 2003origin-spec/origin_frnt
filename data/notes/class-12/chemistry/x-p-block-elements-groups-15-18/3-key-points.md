## Trends that break the pattern

:::trap
**Electron gain enthalpy:** the first member is **not** the most negative. Cl is more negative than F, and S more negative than O. The small F and O atoms are crowded, so an incoming electron is repelled more.
:::

:::trap
**Halogen bond enthalpy:** $\ce{F2}$ is **not** the strongest. The order is $\ce{Cl2}$ > $\ce{Br2}$ > $\ce{F2}$ > $\ce{I2}$. The lone pairs on the two small F atoms repel each other.
:::

- Group 15 has a **higher** ionisation enthalpy than group 16 in the same period (N > O, P > S): a half-filled $p^3$ shell is extra stable.
- From As to Bi the atomic radius grows only a little: poorly shielding d and f electrons hold the outer shell in.
- $\ce{NH3}$ boils **higher** than $\ce{PH3}$ and $\ce{AsH3}$, but **lower** than $\ce{SbH3}$ and $\ce{BiH3}$. Hydrogen bonding lifts it above its neighbours, but the big heavy hydrides still win.
- $\ce{H2O}$, by contrast, has the **highest** boiling point of the group 16 hydrides, and HF the highest of the HX.

## The first member is different

:::remember
- **N:** no d orbitals, so maximum covalency 4, no $\ce{NCl5}$ or $\ce{NF5}$. Strong $p\pi$–$p\pi$ bonds, so $\ce{N2}$ is a gas while phosphorus is $\ce{P4}$.
- **O:** hydrogen bonding makes water a liquid while $\ce{H2S}$ is a gas. O shows a positive state only with F ($\ce{OF2}$ +2, $\ce{O2F2}$ +1).
- **F:** only −1, only one oxoacid (HOF), HF is a liquid and a **weak** acid in water.
:::

:::trap
**N–N single bond is weaker than P–P**, even though N is smaller. The lone pairs on the two close N atoms repel. But the N≡N **triple** bond is very strong, which is why $\ce{N2}$ is so unreactive.
:::

## Hydrides

- Basicity: $\ce{NH3}$ > $\ce{PH3}$ > $\ce{AsH3}$ > $\ce{SbH3}$ ≥ $\ce{BiH3}$. Reducing power goes the **other** way: $\ce{BiH3}$ is the strongest reducing agent.
- Bond angle falls down the group: $\ce{NH3}$ (107.8°) is far above $\ce{PH3}$ (93.6°). The same with $\ce{H2O}$ (104.5°) and $\ce{H2S}$ (92°).
- Acid strength **rises** down the group: $\ce{H2Te}$ is the strongest acid among $\ce{H2E}$, and HI among HX. The H–E bond weakens down the group; electronegativity is not the deciding factor here.

:::trap
HF is a **weak** acid in water although F is the most electronegative element. The H–F bond is very strong. Don't rank HX acid strength by electronegativity.
:::

## Oxoacids: basicity and structure

:::trap
$\ce{H3PO3}$ is **dibasic**, not tribasic, and $\ce{H3PO2}$ is **monobasic**. Only H atoms on oxygen (P–OH) ionise. The H on P (P–H) does not.
:::

:::tip
Each P carries one P=O. In an acid with **one** P atom, every other O is a P–OH. $\ce{H3PO2}$ has 2 O, so 1 P–OH; the other 2 H sit on P. $\ce{H3PO3}$: 3 O, so 2 P–OH and 1 P–H.
:::

- Acids with P–H bonds ($\ce{H3PO2}$, $\ce{H3PO3}$) are good reducing agents; $\ce{H3PO4}$, with no P–H, is not. $\ce{H3PO2}$ (two P–H) reduces silver nitrate to silver.
- $\ce{H4P2O6}$ has a **P–P** bond; $\ce{H4P2O7}$ has a **P–O–P** bridge. Both are tetrabasic. Don't confuse them.
- $\ce{H2S2O8}$ (peroxodisulphuric acid) has an **O–O** bridge. S is still +6; the two peroxide O atoms are −1.
- Oxoacids of chlorine: **more O atoms, stronger acid**. $\ce{HClO4}$ is the strongest. The negative charge on the anion is spread over more O atoms.

## Nitric acid and the oxides of nitrogen

:::trap
Copper gives **NO** with **dilute** $\ce{HNO3}$ and **$\ce{NO2}$** with **concentrated** $\ce{HNO3}$. Zinc with dilute $\ce{HNO3}$ gives $\ce{N2O}$. Never H₂ with copper.
:::

- Al and Cr do **not** dissolve in conc. $\ce{HNO3}$: they become passive (oxide film).
- $\ce{NO2}$ (brown, paramagnetic, odd electron) pairs up into $\ce{N2O4}$ (colourless, diamagnetic) on cooling.
- $\ce{N2O}$ and NO are **neutral** oxides; the others are acidic.
- Brown ring: the brown compound is $\ce{[Fe(H2O)5(NO)]^2+}$, formed at the junction of the solution and conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$.

## Phosphorus compounds

- White P is more reactive than red P because the 60° bond angle in $\ce{P4}$ is strained.
- $\ce{PCl5}$ in the **solid** is $\ce{[PCl4]+[PCl6]-}$ (tetrahedral + octahedral), not trigonal bipyramidal molecules.
- In gaseous $\ce{PCl5}$ the two **axial** bonds are **longer** (weaker) than the three equatorial ones.
- Partial hydrolysis of $\ce{PCl5}$ gives $\ce{POCl3}$; complete hydrolysis gives $\ce{H3PO4}$. $\ce{PCl3}$ hydrolyses to $\ce{H3PO3}$.

## Oxygen, ozone and sulphur

- $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{S2}$ (vapour at high temperature) are both **paramagnetic**.
- $\ce{O3}$ is **bent**, not linear, with two **equal** O–O bonds.
- Rhombic S is stable **below** 369 K, monoclinic **above** it. Both contain $\ce{S8}$ rings.

:::trap
In the contact process $\ce{SO3}$ is dissolved in conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ to give oleum, $\ce{H2S2O7}$, **not** in water. (Dissolving it straight in water gives an acid mist.)
:::

- Catalyst: $\ce{V2O5}$. Dust and arsenic compounds are removed from $\ce{SO2}$ first, as they poison it.
- To dilute conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$, always add **acid to water**, never water to acid.

## Halogens and interhalogens

- Cold dilute NaOH with $\ce{Cl2}$ gives **NaOCl**; hot concentrated NaOH gives **$\ce{NaClO3}$**. Both are disproportionations (Cl from 0 to −1 and +1, or −1 and +5).
- Excess $\ce{NH3}$ with $\ce{Cl2}$ gives $\ce{N2}$ + $\ce{NH4Cl}$. Excess $\ce{Cl2}$ gives explosive $\ce{NCl3}$.
- Moist $\ce{Cl2}$ bleaches by **oxidation**, so its bleaching is permanent. Dry $\ce{Cl2}$ does not bleach.

:::trap
In an interhalogen XX′ₙ, X is the **larger** halogen and sits in the centre. On hydrolysis the **smaller** halogen gives the halide ion and the larger one the oxoacid: ICl gives HCl and HOI, not HI and HOCl.
:::

- Interhalogens are more reactive than the halogens (except $\ce{F2}$): the X–X′ bond is weaker.
- $\ce{ClF3}$ is **T-shaped**, not trigonal planar: 3 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs.

## Noble gases

:::remember
- Shapes: $\ce{XeF2}$ linear, $\ce{XeF4}$ square planar, $\ce{XeF6}$ distorted octahedral, $\ce{XeO3}$ pyramidal, $\ce{XeOF4}$ square pyramidal.
- Lone pairs on Xe: $\ce{XeF2}$ 3, $\ce{XeF4}$ 2, $\ce{XeF6}$ 1, $\ce{XeO3}$ 1, $\ce{XeOF4}$ 1.
- Bartlett's first noble-gas compound: $\ce{Xe+PtF6-}$, suggested by $\ce{O2+PtF6-}$ (Xe and $\ce{O2}$ have almost the same ionisation enthalpy).
:::

:::trap
$\ce{XeF6}$ is **not** a regular octahedron. Xe has 6 bond pairs **and** 1 lone pair (7 electron pairs), so the octahedron is distorted.
:::

- Complete hydrolysis of $\ce{XeF6}$ gives $\ce{XeO3}$ (explosive); partial hydrolysis gives $\ce{XeOF4}$ and then $\ce{XeO2F2}$.
- Xe in $\ce{XeO3}$, $\ce{XeOF4}$ and $\ce{XeF6}$ is +6 in all three.
- He has the lowest boiling point of any substance (4.2 K). He–O₂ mixtures are used in diving because He dissolves very little in blood.
