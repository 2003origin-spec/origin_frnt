## 1. Group 15: N, P, As, Sb, Bi

- Outer shell $ns^2np^3$: a **half-filled** p subshell, which is extra stable. So their ionisation enthalpy is **higher** than that of group 16 in the same period.
- Atomic radius grows down the group: a big jump from N to P, then only small steps from As to Bi (filled d and f subshells shield poorly).
- **Non-metal to metal:** N and P are non-metals, As and Sb metalloids, Bi a metal.
- Every element except N shows allotropy.
- **Oxidation states:** −3, +3, +5. Down the group −3 gets less common (Bi hardly forms it) and +3 gets more stable than +5 (inert pair effect).
- N also shows +1, +2 and +4 with oxygen. In acid solution nitrogen's states from +1 to +4 tend to **disproportionate**:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{3HNO2} \\ & \ce{-> HNO3 + H2O + 2NO} \end{aligned}$$
- **Oxides** $\ce{E2O3}$ and $\ce{E2O5}$: the higher oxidation state is more acidic. Acidity falls down the group: $\ce{E2O3}$ of N and P is acidic, of As and Sb amphoteric, of Bi mainly basic.
- **Halides** $\ce{EX3}$ and $\ce{EX5}$: N forms no pentahalide (no d orbitals). Pentahalides are more covalent than trihalides. Of nitrogen's trihalides, only $\ce{NF3}$ is stable. $\ce{BiF3}$ is ionic; the other trihalides are mainly covalent.

## 2. Why nitrogen is different

- Small size, high electronegativity, high ionisation enthalpy, and **no d orbitals**.
- It forms strong $p\pi$–$p\pi$ multiple bonds with itself and with C and O. So nitrogen is a diatomic gas, $\ce{N\bond{#}N}$ (bond enthalpy 941.4 kJ/mol), while P, As, Sb are single-bonded solids such as $\ce{P4}$.
- The N–N **single** bond is weaker than P–P: the short bond brings the lone pairs close and they repel. So nitrogen catenates poorly.
- Maximum covalency of N is **4** (as in $\ce{NH4+}$). P can expand its octet, as in $\ce{PCl5}$ and $\ce{PF6-}$.
- N cannot form $d\pi$–$p\pi$ bonds; P can, as in $\ce{R3P=O}$.

## 3. Dinitrogen and ammonia

- **$\ce{N2}$ in the lab:** warm aqueous $\ce{NH4Cl}$ with $\ce{NaNO2}$:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{NH4Cl + NaNO2} \\ & \ce{-> N2 + 2H2O + NaCl} \end{aligned}$$
- Very pure $\ce{N2}$: heat barium azide. Industry: distil liquid air.
- $\ce{N2}$ is inert at room temperature (strong triple bond). It reacts on heating: with Mg to give $\ce{Mg3N2}$, with $\ce{O2}$ at about 2000 K to give NO.
- **Ammonia (Haber process):** $\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}$ is exothermic with fewer gas moles on the right, so high pressure helps. Conditions are on the formula sheet (about 200 atm, 700 K, iron oxide catalyst).
- $\ce{NH3}$ is **trigonal pyramidal** (3 bond pairs, 1 lone pair on N). It is hydrogen bonded, so it boils higher than $\ce{PH3}$.
- Its water solution is weakly basic: $\ce{NH3 + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH-}$. The lone pair makes it a Lewis base: $\ce{Cu^2+}$ gives the deep blue $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4]^2+}$, and AgCl dissolves as $\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]+}$.

## 4. Oxides of nitrogen

| Oxide | N state | Look and nature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ce{N2O}$ | +1 | Colourless gas, neutral |
| NO | +2 | Colourless gas, neutral |
| $\ce{N2O3}$ | +3 | Blue solid, acidic |
| $\ce{NO2}$ | +4 | Brown gas, acidic |
| $\ce{N2O4}$ | +4 | Colourless, acidic |
| $\ce{N2O5}$ | +5 | Colourless solid, acidic |

- $\ce{N2O}$: heat ammonium nitrate, $\ce{NH4NO3 -> N2O + 2H2O}$. Linear molecule.
- $\ce{NO2}$: heat lead nitrate at 673 K. Like NO, it has an **odd** electron (paramagnetic). It is bent, and on cooling pairs up into colourless $\ce{N2O4}$: $\ce{2NO2 <=> N2O4}$.

## 5. Nitric acid

- Made by the **Ostwald process**: $\ce{NH3}$ is burnt to NO on Pt/Rh gauze, NO is oxidised to $\ce{NO2}$, and $\ce{NO2}$ is absorbed in water. Distillation gives about 68% acid; conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ dehydrates it to 98%.
- A strong acid and a strong **oxidising agent**. The product depends on the acid's concentration and the metal:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{3Cu + 8HNO3}\ \text{(dilute)} \\ & \ce{-> 3Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO + 4H2O} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Cu + 4HNO3}\ \text{(conc.)} \\ & \ce{-> Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO2 + 2H2O} \end{aligned}$$
- Zinc with dilute $\ce{HNO3}$ gives $\ce{N2O}$. Al and Cr are **not** dissolved by conc. $\ce{HNO3}$: a thin oxide film makes them passive.
- Conc. $\ce{HNO3}$ oxidises non-metals: $\ce{I2}$ to $\ce{HIO3}$, C to $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{S8}$ to $\ce{H2SO4}$, $\ce{P4}$ to $\ce{H3PO4}$. It is itself reduced to $\ce{NO2}$.
- **Brown ring test** for nitrate: $\ce{Fe^2+}$ reduces nitrate to NO, which gives the brown $\ce{[Fe(H2O)5(NO)]^2+}$ where conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ meets the solution.

## 6. Phosphorus and its compounds

| | White P | Red P |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | Separate $\ce{P4}$ tetrahedra | Polymer: chains of $\ce{P4}$ units |
| Reactivity | Very reactive, catches fire in air | Much less reactive |
| Other | Poisonous, glows in the dark, dissolves in $\ce{CS2}$ | Not poisonous, no glow, insoluble in $\ce{CS2}$ |

- White P is reactive because of **angular strain**: the P–P–P angle in $\ce{P4}$ is only 60°. Heating white P at 573 K in an inert gas gives red P. **Black** P is a third form; it does not oxidise in air.
- **Phosphine:** white P with conc. NaOH (inert gas), or calcium phosphide with water:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{P4 + 3NaOH + 3H2O} \\ & \ce{-> PH3 + 3NaH2PO2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Ca3P2 + 6H2O} \\ & \ce{-> 3Ca(OH)2 + 2PH3} \end{aligned}$$
- $\ce{PH3}$: colourless, highly poisonous, smells of rotten fish, weakly basic ($\ce{PH4Br}$ with HBr). Used in **Holme's signals** and smoke screens.
- **$\ce{PCl3}$:** white P with dry $\ce{Cl2}$. A colourless oily liquid, pyramidal. Water gives $\ce{H3PO3}$:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{PCl3 + 3H2O} \\ & \ce{-> H3PO3 + 3HCl} \end{aligned}$$
- **$\ce{PCl5}$:** white P with excess dry $\ce{Cl2}$. A yellowish-white powder. Water acts in two steps:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{PCl5 + H2O} \\ & \ce{-> POCl3 + 2HCl} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{POCl3 + 3H2O} \\ & \ce{-> H3PO4 + 3HCl} \end{aligned}$$
- On heating, $\ce{PCl5 <=> PCl3 + Cl2}$. Both chlorides turn –OH groups into –Cl ($\ce{C2H5OH}$ to $\ce{C2H5Cl}$).
- $\ce{PCl5}$ gas and liquid: **trigonal bipyramidal**, with the 2 axial bonds longer than the 3 equatorial ones. **Solid** $\ce{PCl5}$ is ionic, $\ce{[PCl4]+[PCl6]-}$: a tetrahedral cation and an octahedral anion.
- **Oxoacids:** in each, P is tetrahedral, with at least one P=O and one P–OH. Basicity = number of P–OH groups. $\ce{H3PO2}$ and $\ce{H3PO3}$ have P–H bonds and are reducing agents. On heating, $\ce{H3PO3}$ disproportionates:
$$\ce{4H3PO3 -> 3H3PO4 + PH3}$$

## 7. Group 16: O, S, Se, Te, Po

- Outer shell $ns^2np^4$. O and S are non-metals, Se and Te metalloids, Po a radioactive metal.
- Oxygen's electron gain enthalpy is **less** negative than sulphur's (O is compact, so an added electron is repelled more). From S downwards it gets less negative again.
- Oxygen is the second most electronegative element, after F.
- **Oxidation states:** O is −2, except in $\ce{OF2}$ (+2) and $\ce{O2F2}$ (+1). S, Se and Te show −2, +2, +4 and +6. Down the group +4 becomes more stable than +6.
- **Why O is different:** small size, high electronegativity, no d orbitals. So $\ce{H2O}$ is hydrogen bonded (a liquid) while $\ce{H2S}$ is a gas. O's covalency is limited to 4 (in practice rarely above 2); S can reach 6, as in $\ce{SF6}$.
- O exists as $\ce{O2}$ (a gas, with a double bond); S exists as $\ce{S8}$ rings (a solid).
- **Halides:** $\ce{SF6}$ is exceptionally stable (steric reasons) and octahedral. $\ce{SF4}$ is see-saw (sp³d, with the lone pair at an equatorial position).

## 8. Dioxygen and ozone

- Lab $\ce{O2}$: heat $\ce{KClO3}$ with a little $\ce{MnO2}$, $\ce{2KClO3 -> 2KCl + 3O2}$. $\ce{O2}$ is **paramagnetic**.
- **Ozone:** pass a silent electric discharge through dry $\ce{O2}$:
$$\ce{3O2 -> 2O3}$$
This is endothermic: $\Delta H^\ominus = +142$ kJ/mol.
- $\ce{O3}$ is thermodynamically unstable and slowly turns back into $\ce{O2}$. It is a strong oxidising agent:
$$\ce{PbS + 4O3 -> PbSO4 + 4O2}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2I- + H2O + O3} \\ & \ce{-> 2OH- + I2 + O2} \end{aligned}$$
- The iodine set free from KI (buffered at pH 9.2 with borate) can be titrated with sodium thiosulphate to estimate ozone.
- $\ce{O3}$ is **bent** (angular). Both O–O bonds are equal (128 pm), angle about 117°: a resonance hybrid.
- NO from supersonic jets and CFCs destroy the ozone layer: $\ce{NO + O3 -> NO2 + O2}$.

## 9. Sulphur, SO₂ and sulphuric acid

- **Allotropes:** rhombic (α) is stable below **369 K**, monoclinic (β) above it. Both are made of puckered $\ce{S8}$ rings (crown shape). At about 1000 K, $\ce{S2}$ is the main form and is paramagnetic, like $\ce{O2}$.
- **$\ce{SO2}$:** burn S in air, or treat a sulphite with dilute acid. Colourless, choking smell, very soluble in water (gives $\ce{H2SO3}$). It is **bent**, with two equal S–O bonds.
- Moist $\ce{SO2}$ is a **reducing agent**: it turns $\ce{Fe^3+}$ into $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and decolourises acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{5SO2 + 2MnO4- + 2H2O} \\ & \ce{-> 5SO4^2- + 4H+ + 2Mn^2+} \end{aligned}$$
- **Contact process** (equations on the formula sheet): burn S or a sulphide ore to $\ce{SO2}$, remove dust and arsenic compounds, oxidise to $\ce{SO3}$ over $\ce{V2O5}$, absorb in $\ce{H2SO4}$ to get oleum, then dilute. The oxidation is exothermic and lowers the gas volume, so low temperature and high pressure favour yield; 720 K (for a good rate) and 2 bar are used.
- **$\ce{H2SO4}$:** a dense, oily, colourless liquid. Mixing with water gives out a lot of heat: always add the **acid to water**, slowly, while stirring.
- It is a strong **dibasic** acid. Its low volatility lets it make more volatile acids from their salts ($\ce{NaCl}$ to HCl, $\ce{KNO3}$ to $\ce{HNO3}$).
- It is a strong **dehydrating** agent: it chars sugar to carbon, $\ce{C12H22O11 -> 12C + 11H2O}$.
- Hot and concentrated, it is an **oxidising** agent:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Cu + 2H2SO4}\ \text{(conc.)} \\ & \ce{-> CuSO4 + SO2 + 2H2O} \end{aligned}$$

## 10. Group 17: the halogens

- Outer shell $ns^2np^5$, one electron short of a noble gas: very reactive non-metals with the smallest atoms in their periods.
- $\ce{F2}$ and $\ce{Cl2}$ are gases, $\ce{Br2}$ a liquid, $\ce{I2}$ a solid. All are coloured (they absorb visible light): $\ce{F2}$ pale yellow, $\ce{Cl2}$ greenish yellow, $\ce{Br2}$ red, $\ce{I2}$ violet.
- **Oxidation states:** F only −1. Cl, Br, I show −1, and +1, +3, +5, +7 with O or F.
- **Reactivity falls** down the group. $\ce{F2}$ is the strongest oxidising agent: it even oxidises water to $\ce{O2}$. $\ce{Cl2}$ and $\ce{Br2}$ react with water to give HX and HOX.
- **Why F is different:** smallest size, highest electronegativity, low F–F bond enthalpy, no d orbitals. So it shows only −1, forms only one oxoacid (HOF), HF is a liquid (hydrogen bonding) while the other HX are gases, and its compounds with metals are the most ionic.
- $\ce{OF2}$ and $\ce{O2F2}$ are **fluorides**, not oxides (F is more electronegative than O). Chlorine oxides tend to explode; $\ce{ClO2}$ bleaches paper pulp and treats water.

## 11. Chlorine and hydrogen chloride

- **Lab chlorine:** heat $\ce{MnO2}$ with conc. HCl (or add HCl to $\ce{KMnO4}$):
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{MnO2 + 4HCl} \\ & \ce{-> MnCl2 + Cl2 + 2H2O} \end{aligned}$$
- Industry: Deacon's process (formula sheet) or electrolysis of brine. A greenish-yellow, choking gas.
- **With ammonia:** excess $\ce{NH3}$ gives $\ce{NH4Cl}$ and $\ce{N2}$; excess $\ce{Cl2}$ gives the explosive $\ce{NCl3}$.
- **With alkali** (disproportionation of Cl from 0):
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2NaOH + Cl2}\ \text{(cold, dilute)} \\ & \ce{-> NaCl + NaOCl + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{6NaOH + 3Cl2}\ \text{(hot, conc.)} \\ & \ce{-> 5NaCl + NaClO3 + 3H2O} \end{aligned}$$
- With dry slaked lime it gives **bleaching powder**.
- **Bleaching:** moist chlorine gives HOCl, which releases nascent oxygen. This **oxidises** the dye, so the bleaching is permanent. Dry chlorine does not bleach.
- **HCl:** heat NaCl with conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$; dry the gas by passing it through conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$. It is very soluble and a strong acid in water. With $\ce{NH3}$ it gives white fumes of $\ce{NH4Cl}$.
- **Aqua regia** = 3 parts conc. HCl + 1 part conc. $\ce{HNO3}$. It dissolves gold and platinum:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Au + 4H+ + NO3- + 4Cl-} \\ & \ce{-> AuCl4- + NO + 2H2O} \end{aligned}$$

## 12. Interhalogen compounds

- Formed between two different halogens: XX′, XX′₃, XX′₅, XX′₇. X is the **larger**, less electronegative halogen.
- All are covalent and diamagnetic. Most are volatile solids or liquids at 298 K; ClF is a gas.
- They are **more reactive than the halogens** (except $\ce{F2}$), because the X–X′ bond is weaker than the X–X bond (F–F excepted).
- Shapes from VSEPR (lone pairs on X): $\ce{ClF3}$ bent T (2), $\ce{BrF5}$ square pyramidal (1), $\ce{IF7}$ pentagonal bipyramidal (0).
- Uses: non-aqueous solvents, fluorinating agents; $\ce{ClF3}$ and $\ce{BrF3}$ make $\ce{UF6}$ for enriching uranium-235.

## 13. Group 18: the noble gases

- Outer shell $ns^2np^6$ (He: $1s^2$), a full octet. Very high ionisation enthalpies and positive electron gain enthalpies. Rn is radioactive.
- Monatomic, colourless, odourless. Only weak dispersion forces act, so boiling points are very low; **He** has the lowest of any substance (4.2 K).
- **Bartlett (1962):** $\ce{O2}$ reacts with $\ce{PtF6}$ to give $\ce{O2+[PtF6]-}$. Xe has almost the same first ionisation enthalpy as $\ce{O2}$, so he tried Xe and got a red compound, $\ce{Xe+PtF6-}$.
- Xenon forms fluorides and oxides because it has a fairly low ionisation enthalpy and F and O are very electronegative. Krypton forms only a few compounds ($\ce{KrF2}$). No true compounds of He, Ne or Ar are known.
- The xenon fluorides are colourless crystalline solids, strong fluorinating agents, and hydrolyse easily. They can give fluoride ions or accept them:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{XeF2 + PF5} \\ & \ce{-> [XeF]+[PF6]-} \end{aligned}$$
- **$\ce{XeO3}$** (from hydrolysis of $\ce{XeF4}$ or $\ce{XeF6}$) is a colourless, **explosive** solid.
- **Uses:** He in weather balloons, as a cryogen (MRI magnets) and with $\ce{O2}$ for divers. Ne in advertising signs. Ar as an inert gas for welding and in bulbs.
