## 1. What a solution is

- A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more components: its composition and properties are the same throughout.
- The component present in the largest amount is usually the **solvent**. It decides the physical state of the solution. The others are **solutes**.
- This chapter deals with **binary** solutions (two components).

| Type | Solute in solvent | Example (NCERT) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gaseous | Gas in gas | Oxygen and nitrogen |
| Gaseous | Liquid in gas | Chloroform mixed with nitrogen |
| Gaseous | Solid in gas | Camphor in nitrogen |
| Liquid | Gas in liquid | Oxygen in water |
| Liquid | Liquid in liquid | Ethanol in water |
| Liquid | Solid in liquid | Glucose in water |
| Solid | Gas in solid | Hydrogen in palladium |
| Solid | Liquid in solid | Amalgam of mercury with sodium |
| Solid | Solid in solid | Copper dissolved in gold |

## 2. Ways to express concentration

- **Mass %** (w/w): common in industry. Commercial bleach is 3.62% sodium hypochlorite by mass.
- **Volume %** (V/V): common for liquids. A 35% (V/V) ethylene glycol antifreeze lowers the freezing point of water to 255.4 K (−17.6 °C).
- **Mass by volume %** (w/V): grams of solute per 100 mL of solution; used in medicine and pharmacy.
- **ppm**: for trace amounts, such as pollutants or dissolved oxygen in sea water (about 5.8 ppm).
- **Mole fraction** ($x$): moles of a component ÷ total moles. All mole fractions add up to 1.
- **Molarity** ($M$): moles of solute per litre of **solution**.
- **Molality** ($m$): moles of solute per kg of **solvent**.

:::remember
Mass %, ppm, mole fraction and molality depend only on masses, so they **do not change with temperature**. Molarity uses volume, which changes with temperature, so **molarity does**.
:::

## 3. Solubility of a solid in a liquid

- **Solubility** = the maximum amount of a substance that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature.
- **Like dissolves like:** polar solutes (NaCl, sugar) dissolve in polar solvents (water); non-polar solutes (naphthalene, anthracene) dissolve in non-polar solvents (benzene).
- Dissolution and crystallisation go on together. When their rates are equal, a **dynamic equilibrium** is reached:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Solute + Solvent} \\ & \ce{<=> Solution} \end{aligned}$$
- A **saturated** solution is in equilibrium with undissolved solute and holds the maximum amount. An **unsaturated** solution can dissolve more.
- **Temperature** (Le Chatelier): if dissolving is endothermic ($\Delta_{sol}H > 0$), solubility rises with temperature; if exothermic ($\Delta_{sol}H < 0$), it falls.
- **Pressure** has no significant effect: solids and liquids are almost incompressible.

## 4. Solubility of a gas in a liquid: Henry's law

- Gas solubility rises with **pressure** and falls with **temperature**.
- **Henry's law:** at constant temperature, the solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above it. Most common form: $p = K_H\,x$.
- $K_H$ depends on the gas and the temperature. A larger $K_H$ means a **less** soluble gas. $K_H$ for $\ce{N2}$ and $\ce{O2}$ rises with temperature.
- Why heating lowers gas solubility: dissolving a gas is like condensing it, so it **releases heat** (exothermic). By Le Chatelier, heating pushes the gas out. That is why aquatic life is more comfortable in cold water.

**Applications of Henry's law (NCERT):**
- Soft drinks and soda water are sealed under **high pressure** of $\ce{CO2}$ to dissolve more of it.
- **Bends (scuba divers):** at depth, more air dissolves in the blood. As the pressure falls on the way up, nitrogen bubbles form and block capillaries. Tanks use air diluted with **helium** (11.7% He, 56.2% $\ce{N2}$, 32.1% $\ce{O2}$).
- **Anoxia (high altitude):** the partial pressure of oxygen is low, so blood and tissues get less oxygen; climbers become weak and can't think clearly.

## 5. Vapour pressure of liquid–liquid solutions: Raoult's law

- For a solution of **volatile** liquids, the partial vapour pressure of each component is proportional to its mole fraction: $p_1 = x_1 p_1^0$, $p_2 = x_2 p_2^0$.
- By Dalton's law, $p_{total} = p_1 + p_2$. For an ideal solution, $p_{total}$ varies **linearly** with $x_2$, from $p_1^0$ to $p_2^0$.
- The vapour composition comes from $p_i = y_i\,p_{total}$. For an ideal solution the vapour is **richer in the more volatile component** than the liquid is.
- **Raoult's law is a special case of Henry's law** in which $K_H = p_1^0$.

## 6. Vapour pressure of solutions of solids in liquids

- Adding a **non-volatile** solute lowers the vapour pressure: solute particles take up part of the surface, so fewer solvent molecules escape.
- The lowering depends on the **amount** of solute, not its nature: 1 mol of sucrose or 1 mol of urea in 1 kg of water lowers the vapour pressure by nearly the same amount.
- Only the solvent is volatile, so $p_1 = x_1 p_1^0$ is the whole vapour pressure (Raoult's law).

## 7. Ideal and non-ideal solutions

**Ideal solution:** obeys Raoult's law at **all** compositions; $\Delta_{mix}H = 0$ and $\Delta_{mix}V = 0$. A–B attractions are nearly equal to A–A and B–B. No solution is perfectly ideal, but these come close: n-hexane + n-heptane, bromoethane + chloroethane, benzene + toluene.

**Non-ideal solution:** does not obey Raoult's law over the whole range.

| Feature | Positive deviation | Negative deviation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vapour pressure | Higher than Raoult's law | Lower than Raoult's law |
| A–B attractions | Weaker than A–A, B–B | Stronger than A–A, B–B |
| Escaping tendency | Increases | Decreases |
| Examples (NCERT) | Ethanol + acetone; carbon disulphide + acetone | Phenol + aniline; chloroform + acetone |

- **Ethanol + acetone:** acetone breaks some of ethanol's hydrogen bonds. **$\ce{CS2}$ + acetone:** solute–solvent dipolar attractions are weaker.
- **Phenol + aniline:** the phenolic O–H forms a hydrogen bond to aniline's N lone pair, stronger than those between like molecules. **Chloroform + acetone:** chloroform's C–H forms a hydrogen bond with acetone's oxygen.
- Positive deviation goes with $\Delta_{mix}H > 0$; negative deviation with $\Delta_{mix}H < 0$. NCERT Exercise 1.14 asks this, though the text does not explain it.
- (Exam extra, not in NCERT) Usually $\Delta_{mix}V > 0$ for positive deviation and $\Delta_{mix}V < 0$ for negative deviation.

**Azeotropes:** binary mixtures that have the **same composition in liquid and vapour** and boil at a constant temperature. They can't be separated by fractional distillation.
- **Minimum boiling azeotrope:** from a **large positive** deviation. Ethanol–water gives about **95% ethanol by volume**.
- **Maximum boiling azeotrope:** from a **large negative** deviation. Nitric acid–water: about **68% $\ce{HNO3}$ by mass**, boiling at **393.5 K**.

## 8. Colligative properties

Properties that depend on the **number of solute particles**, not on their identity (Latin: *co* = together, *ligare* = to bind). Four of them, all linked to the lowering of vapour pressure:

1. **Relative lowering of vapour pressure** = mole fraction of the solute (Raoult). Gives $M_2$ from $\dfrac{p_1^0 - p_1}{p_1^0} \approx \dfrac{w_2 M_1}{M_2 w_1}$.
2. **Elevation of boiling point:** a liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the outside pressure. The solution's vapour pressure is lower, so it must be heated more. $\Delta T_b = K_b m$. 1 mol of sucrose in 1000 g of water boils at 373.52 K at 1 atm.
3. **Depression of freezing point:** at the freezing point the liquid and solid have the same vapour pressure. The solution's lower vapour pressure meets the solid's curve at a lower temperature. $\Delta T_f = K_f m$.
4. **Osmotic pressure:** $\Pi = CRT$ (next section).

- $K_b$ and $K_f$ depend only on the **solvent**. Their unit is K kg mol⁻¹.
- For water, $K_b$ = 0.52 and $K_f$ = 1.86 K kg mol⁻¹.

## 9. Osmosis and osmotic pressure

- A **semipermeable membrane** (SPM) lets small solvent molecules through but not larger solute particles. Natural: pig's bladder, parchment. Synthetic: cellophane.
- **Osmosis:** solvent flows through the SPM from pure solvent (or a dilute solution) into the more concentrated solution.
- **Osmotic pressure** ($\Pi$): the extra pressure that must be applied **on the solution side** to just stop osmosis. For dilute solutions $\Pi = CRT$.
- Osmotic pressure is the **preferred method for molar masses of proteins, polymers and other macromolecules**: it is measured at room temperature (biomolecules are not stable when heated), it uses molarity, and its value is large even for very dilute solutions.
- **Isotonic:** same osmotic pressure; no osmosis between them. Blood-cell fluid is isotonic with **0.9% (w/V) NaCl** (normal saline), which is safe to inject into veins.
- **Hypertonic** (more than 0.9% NaCl): water leaves the cells and they **shrink**. **Hypotonic** (less than 0.9%): water enters and they **swell**.
- Examples (NCERT): raw mango shrivels in brine; wilted flowers and a limp carrot revive in water; too much salt causes swelling of tissues (**edema**); salted meat and candied fruit kill bacteria by drawing water out of them.

## 10. Reverse osmosis

- If a pressure **larger than the osmotic pressure** is applied on the solution side, pure solvent flows **out** of the solution through the SPM.
- Used for **desalination** of sea water. The membrane is a film of **cellulose acetate** on a support: it lets water through but not the impurities and ions in sea water.

## 11. Abnormal molar mass and the van't Hoff factor

- Colligative properties count particles. If the solute **dissociates** (KCl gives $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$), there are more particles: the colligative effect is larger and the molar mass found is **lower** than the true value.
- If the solute **associates**, there are fewer particles: the molar mass found is **higher**. Ethanoic acid **dimerises in benzene** through hydrogen bonding (this happens in solvents of low dielectric constant), so its molar mass comes out nearly double.
- **van't Hoff factor** $i$ = normal molar mass ÷ abnormal molar mass = observed ÷ calculated colligative property. $i > 1$ for dissociation, $i < 1$ for association.
- Corrected equations: $\Delta T_b = i K_b m$, $\Delta T_f = i K_f m$, $\Pi = i\,n_2RT/V$, and relative lowering $= i\,n_2/n_1$.

| Salt (NCERT Table 1.4) | $i$ at 0.1 m | $i$ at 0.001 m | Complete dissociation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NaCl | 1.87 | 1.97 | 2 |
| KCl | 1.85 | 1.98 | 2 |
| $\ce{MgSO4}$ | 1.21 | 1.82 | 2 |
| $\ce{K2SO4}$ | 2.32 | 2.84 | 3 |

- For these strong electrolytes, real values of $i$ are **below** the whole number for complete dissociation and approach it as the solution gets more dilute (interionic attractions weaken).
- Typical values: $i \approx 2$ for KCl in water, $i \approx 0.5$ for ethanoic acid in benzene.
