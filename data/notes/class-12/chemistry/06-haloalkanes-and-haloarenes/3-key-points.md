## The C–X bond

- Bond length goes up and bond enthalpy goes down from C–F to C–I. So **R–I reacts fastest** in both SN1 and SN2: I⁻ is the best leaving group.

:::trap
The **dipole moment** does not follow electronegativity simply. NCERT's values: $\ce{CH3Cl}$ 1.860 D > $\ce{CH3F}$ 1.847 D > $\ce{CH3Br}$ 1.830 D > $\ce{CH3I}$ 1.636 D. $\ce{CH3Cl}$ is the **highest**: F is more electronegative, but the C–F bond is much shorter.
:::

- Chlorobenzene has a **lower** dipole moment than cyclohexyl chloride: its sp² carbon is more electronegative, and resonance gives the C–Cl bond partial double-bond character, so the bond is less polar.

## Preparation

:::trap
Don't use $\ce{H2SO4}$ to make an alkyl iodide from an alcohol and KI. It turns KI into HI and then **oxidises HI to $\ce{I2}$**. Use 95% $\ce{H3PO4}$.
:::

- $\ce{SOCl2}$ is the best reagent for alkyl chlorides: the other products ($\ce{SO2}$, HCl) are gases and escape.
- Alcohol methods do **not** work for aryl halides: the C–O bond of phenol has partial double-bond character.
- Fluorides are **not** made by direct fluorination of arenes (too violent). Use Swarts for alkyl fluorides.
- Free radical halogenation gives a **mixture**; count the different kinds of H to find the number of monohalo isomers. (NCERT: $\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2CH3}$ gives **4**.)

:::remember
**Finkelstein** = NaI in **dry acetone**, gives alkyl **iodides**; NaCl/NaBr precipitates and pulls the reaction forward.
**Swarts** = AgF, $\ce{Hg2F2}$, $\ce{CoF2}$ or $\ce{SbF3}$, gives alkyl **fluorides**.
:::

## Physical properties

- Boiling point: RI > RBr > RCl > RF, and it falls with branching.
- For $\ce{CH3Br}$, $\ce{CH2Br2}$, $\ce{CHBr3}$: more Br means higher mass and higher boiling point.
- p-Dichlorobenzene has the **highest melting point** of the three isomers (symmetry, better packing), though their boiling points are almost equal.
- Alkyl halides are polar but **don't mix with water**: the new attractions are weaker than the hydrogen bonds they would have to break.

## Ambident nucleophiles

:::trap
**K gives the "normal" product, Ag gives the "other" one:**
- KCN gives the **nitrile** RCN; AgCN gives the **isocyanide** RNC.
- $\ce{KNO2}$ gives the alkyl **nitrite** R–O–N=O; $\ce{AgNO2}$ gives the **nitroalkane** $\ce{R-NO2}$.

KCN is mainly ionic: the free $\ce{CN-}$ attacks through C, because a C–C bond is more stable than C–N. AgCN is mainly covalent, so the N lone pair is the one free to attack.
:::

## SN1 and SN2

:::trap
**The two orders are opposite.** SN2: $\ce{CH3X}$ > 1° > 2° > 3° (crowding). SN1: 3° > 2° > 1° > $\ce{CH3X}$ (carbocation stability). Ask yourself which mechanism the question is about.
:::

- Between two **1°** bromides in SN1, the one with the more branched (more electron-donating) group reacts faster: $\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2Br}$ > $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2Br}$ (NCERT Example 6.7).
- Benzylic order in SN1 (NCERT Example 6.7): $\ce{C6H5C(CH3)(C6H5)Br}$ > $\ce{C6H5CH(C6H5)Br}$ > $\ce{C6H5CH(CH3)Br}$ > $\ce{C6H5CH2Br}$. The SN2 order is the reverse (a phenyl group is bulkier than methyl).
- $\ce{C6H5CHClC6H5}$ is hydrolysed by aqueous KOH faster than $\ce{C6H5CH2Cl}$: two phenyl rings stabilise the carbocation.
- In SN1, the slow step makes the carbocation. Changing $[\ce{OH-}]$ does **not** change the rate.

:::remember
- SN2 of a chiral halide: **inversion** (the umbrella flip).
- SN1 of a chiral halide: **racemisation** (planar sp² carbocation, attack from both faces).
- No bond to the stereocentre broken: **retention**.
:::

:::trap
The sign of rotation (+ or –) tells you **nothing** about the configuration. In NCERT's retention example, (–)-2-methylbutan-1-ol gives **(+)**-1-chloro-2-methylbutane with the **same** configuration.
:::

- Propan-2-ol is achiral (two $\ce{CH3}$ on the central C). Butan-2-ol and 2-chlorobutane are chiral.

## Substitution or elimination

:::trap
**Aqueous KOH** mainly gives an **alcohol** (substitution: $\ce{OH-}$ acts as a nucleophile). **Alcoholic KOH** mainly gives an **alkene** (elimination: the base pulls off a β-H). NCERT asks why in Exercise 6.20. Exam extra (not in NCERT's text): the usual answer is that alcoholic KOH contains the alkoxide ion $\ce{RO-}$, a stronger base than $\ce{OH-}$.
:::

- Elimination needs a **β-hydrogen**. Zaitsev: the more substituted alkene is major.
- A **bulky** base favours elimination; a 1° halide favours SN2; a 3° halide goes SN1 or elimination.

## Grignard and Wurtz

- Make Grignard reagents in **dry** ether: even water, alcohols and amines destroy them (RMgX + $\ce{H2O}$ gives RH).
- With $\ce{D2O}$, a Grignard reagent gives a deuterated hydrocarbon ($\ce{RMgX + D2O -> RD + Mg(OD)X}$), as in NCERT Intext 6.9.
- (Exam extra, not in NCERT) Wurtz with **two different** alkyl halides RX and R'X gives a **mixture** of three alkanes (R–R, R–R', R'–R'), hard to separate. So Wurtz is used only for symmetrical alkanes (R–R), and it can't make methane (the product has at least 2 C).

## Haloarenes

:::trap
**Halogens are deactivating but o,p-directing.** Don't call Cl "activating" because it directs o,p. Reactivity is set by the stronger –I effect; orientation by resonance.
:::

- Haloarenes resist nucleophilic substitution: resonance (partial double bond), sp² carbon (shorter bond, 169 pm), unstable phenyl cation, and repulsion of the nucleophile by the ring.
- $\ce{-NO2}$ at **o** or **p** speeds up nucleophilic substitution; at **m** it has **no effect**. More o/p nitro groups, milder conditions: 623 K/300 atm, then 443 K, 368 K, warm water.
- In electrophilic substitution of chlorobenzene, the **para** product is major every time.

## Polyhalogen compounds: NCERT lines

:::remember
- Chloroform + $\ce{O2}$ in light gives **phosgene** ($\ce{COCl2}$). Store it in dark, closed bottles filled to the top.
- Chloroform's main use today: making freon **R-22**.
- Iodoform's antiseptic action is due to the **free iodine** it releases.
- **Freon-12** is $\ce{CCl2F2}$, made from $\ce{CCl4}$ by the Swarts reaction.
- **DDT** was the first chlorinated organic insecticide; it builds up in fatty tissue and is toxic to fish.
- $\ce{CCl4}$ released into air rises and depletes the ozone layer.
:::

:::tip
**Silver goes to nitrogen:** with both AgCN and $\ce{AgNO2}$ the new bond is C–N (RNC, $\ce{R-NO2}$). With the potassium salts it is C–C (RCN) and C–O (R–O–N=O).
:::
