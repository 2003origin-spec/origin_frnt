## 1. Classification

- By the **number of halogen atoms**: mono-, di-, tri-, tetra- (poly)halogen compounds.
- **sp³ C–X** (halogen on a saturated carbon):
  - **alkyl halides** (haloalkanes), $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}X}$: 1°, 2° or 3° by the carbon that carries X;
  - **allylic halides**: X on the sp³ carbon next to a C=C ($\ce{CH2=CH-CH2X}$);
  - **benzylic halides**: X on the sp³ carbon next to a benzene ring ($\ce{C6H5CH2X}$).
- **sp² C–X** (halogen on a C=C carbon or an aromatic-ring carbon; a ring alone is not enough, e.g. chlorocyclohexane is sp³):
  - **vinylic halides**: X on a C=C carbon ($\ce{CH2=CHX}$);
  - **aryl halides** (haloarenes): X on a benzene-ring carbon ($\ce{C6H5X}$).

## 2. Naming

- Common names: alkyl group + halide (isopropyl chloride). IUPAC: halo + alkane (2-chloropropane).
- Dihalobenzenes: o-, m-, p- in common names; 1,2-, 1,3-, 1,4- in IUPAC.
- **gem-dihalides**: both X on the same carbon. Common name: alkylidene halide ($\ce{CH3CHCl2}$, ethylidene chloride = 1,1-dichloroethane).
- **vic-dihalides**: X on neighbouring carbons. Common name: alkylene dihalide ($\ce{ClCH2CH2Cl}$, ethylene dichloride = 1,2-dichloroethane).

## 3. The C–X bond

- X is more electronegative than C, so the bond is polar: $\ce{C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-}}$. The δ+ carbon is what nucleophiles attack.
- Down the group, X gets bigger, so the C–X bond gets **longer and weaker**: C–F 139 pm, C–I 214 pm; bond enthalpy C–F 452, C–I 234 kJ/mol.

## 4. Preparing haloalkanes and haloarenes

(All the equations are on the Reactions Sheet.)

- **From alcohols** (the best route): HX, $\ce{PX3}$, $\ce{PCl5}$, red P with $\ce{Br2}$/$\ce{I2}$, or $\ce{SOCl2}$. Thionyl chloride is preferred because $\ce{SO2}$ and HCl escape as gases. With HCl, 1° and 2° alcohols need $\ce{ZnCl2}$. Reactivity: 3° > 2° > 1°.
- These methods do **not** make aryl halides: the C–O bond of phenol has partial double-bond character.
- **From alkanes** (free radical): a mixture of isomers, low yield. **From alkenes:** HX (Markovnikov; HBr with peroxide adds the other way) or $\ce{Br2}$ in $\ce{CCl4}$ (vic-dibromide).
- **Halogen exchange:** Finkelstein (iodides) and Swarts (fluorides).
- **Haloarenes:** $\ce{Cl2}$/$\ce{Br2}$ with Fe or $\ce{FeCl3}$ (iodination needs $\ce{HNO3}$ or $\ce{HIO4}$; never fluorine), or the **Sandmeyer reaction** on a diazonium salt ($\ce{Cu2Cl2}$/$\ce{Cu2Br2}$; KI alone for iodine).

## 5. Physical properties

- Pure alkyl halides are colourless; bromides and iodides turn coloured in light. Many volatile ones smell sweet.
- $\ce{CH3Cl}$, $\ce{CH3Br}$, $\ce{C2H5Cl}$ and some chlorofluoromethanes are **gases** at room temperature.
- **Boiling points** are higher than those of hydrocarbons of similar mass (polarity and bigger mass give stronger dipole–dipole and van der Waals forces).
  - Same alkyl group: RI > RBr > RCl > RF.
  - Isomers: more branching, lower boiling point (1-bromobutane 375 K > 2-bromo-2-methylpropane 346 K).
- **Melting points of dihalobenzenes:** boiling points are nearly the same, but the **para** isomer melts highest. It is symmetrical and packs better in the crystal.
- **Density:** bromo, iodo and polychloro compounds are heavier than water. Density rises with more C atoms, more X atoms and heavier X.
- **Solubility:** only very slightly soluble in water. Dissolving would mean breaking water's hydrogen bonds, and the new haloalkane–water attractions are weaker. They dissolve well in organic solvents.

## 6. Nucleophilic substitution (haloalkanes)

- A nucleophile ($\ce{Nu-}$) attacks the δ+ carbon and the halide ion leaves (the **leaving group**).
- **Ambident nucleophiles** can attack through two different atoms:
  - cyanide: KCN is ionic, so the free ion attacks through **C** and gives the **nitrile** RCN (a C–C bond is more stable than C–N); AgCN is mainly covalent, so it attacks through **N** and gives the **isocyanide** RNC;
  - nitrite: $\ce{KNO2}$ gives the alkyl **nitrite** R–O–N=O; $\ce{AgNO2}$ gives the **nitroalkane** $\ce{R-NO2}$.

## 7. SN2 mechanism

- One step: the nucleophile bonds as the leaving group leaves. In the transition state, carbon is bonded to five groups (partly to Nu and X); it can't be isolated.
- The nucleophile attacks from the side **opposite** to X, so the configuration **inverts**, like an umbrella turning inside out in strong wind.
- Rate $= k[\ce{RX}][\ce{Nu-}]$: second order (Hughes and Ingold, 1937).
- Bulky groups block the attack. Reactivity: $\ce{CH3X}$ > 1° > 2° > 3°.

## 8. SN1 mechanism

- Two steps. Step I (slow, reversible): C–X breaks to give a **carbocation**. The energy comes from solvation of the halide ion by the protic solvent. Step II (fast): the nucleophile attacks the carbocation.
- Rate $= k[\ce{RX}]$: first order. It does not depend on $[\ce{OH-}]$.
- A more stable carbocation forms faster. Reactivity: 3° > 2° > 1° > $\ce{CH3X}$. **Allylic and benzylic** halides are also very reactive: their carbocations are resonance-stabilised.
- Usually done in polar protic solvents (water, alcohol, acetic acid).

## 9. Stereochemistry

- **Optical activity:** rotating the plane of plane-polarised light (measured with a polarimeter). Clockwise: dextrorotatory, d or (+). Anticlockwise: laevorotatory, l or (–).
- **Chiral:** not superimposable on its mirror image, like your two hands. A carbon with **four different groups** is an asymmetric carbon (stereocentre). Butan-2-ol is chiral; propan-2-ol is achiral.
- **Enantiomers:** non-superimposable mirror images. Same m.p., b.p. and refractive index; they rotate light by equal amounts in opposite directions.
- **Racemic mixture** (dl or ±): a 50:50 mix of enantiomers. Rotation is zero. Making one is **racemisation**.
- The sign of rotation is **not** linked to the actual (absolute) configuration.
- **Retention:** no bond to the stereocentre is broken, so the arrangement stays. Example: (–)-2-methylbutan-1-ol + HCl gives (+)-1-chloro-2-methylbutane. Configuration kept, sign changed.
- **SN2** on a chiral halide: **inversion**. (–)-2-Bromooctane + NaOH gives (+)-octan-2-ol.
- **SN1** on a chiral halide: **racemisation**. The carbocation is sp² and **planar**, so $\ce{OH-}$ attacks from both faces. Optically active 2-bromobutane gives (±)-butan-2-ol.

## 10. Elimination

- Heating a haloalkane that has a β-hydrogen with **alcoholic KOH**: H leaves from the β-carbon, X from the α-carbon (the carbon bearing X), giving an alkene. This is **β-elimination** (dehydrohalogenation).
- **Zaitsev (Saytzeff) rule:** the major alkene has more alkyl groups on the C=C carbons. 2-Bromopentane gives pent-2-ene (81%) over pent-1-ene (19%).
- **Substitution or elimination?** A bulky base prefers to pull off a proton (elimination). 1° halides prefer SN2; 2° halides go SN2 or elimination, depending on the strength of the base/nucleophile; 3° halides go SN1 or elimination.

## 11. Reactions with metals

- **Grignard reagent** (Victor Grignard, 1900): RX + Mg in **dry ether** gives RMgX. The C–Mg bond is covalent but very polar (C is δ–); the Mg–X bond is essentially ionic.
- Grignard reagents react with any proton source (water, alcohols, amines) to give the hydrocarbon RH. So they must be made and used dry.
- **Wurtz reaction:** 2RX + 2Na (dry ether) gives R–R, with double the carbon atoms.

## 12. Haloarenes: reactions

**Why they resist nucleophilic substitution:**
1. **Resonance:** the lone pairs on X are in conjugation with the ring, giving the C–X bond partial double-bond character.
2. **sp² carbon:** more s-character, so it holds the bond pair more tightly. C–Cl is 169 pm in chlorobenzene against 177 pm in a haloalkane; a shorter bond is harder to break.
3. **Phenyl cation is unstable** (not resonance-stabilised), so SN1 is ruled out.
4. The electron-rich nucleophile is repelled by the electron-rich ring.

**Nitro groups help:** chlorobenzene needs NaOH at 623 K and 300 atm to give phenol. An $\ce{-NO2}$ at the ortho or para position makes it much easier (4-nitro: 443 K; 2,4-dinitro: 368 K; 2,4,6-trinitro: warm water). The negative charge of the intermediate carbanion can then sit on the carbon bearing $\ce{-NO2}$ and be stabilised by it. A meta $\ce{-NO2}$ can't do this, so it has no effect.

**Electrophilic substitution** (halogenation, nitration, sulphonation, Friedel–Crafts): X is **o,p-directing** but **deactivating**.
- Resonance raises the electron density at ortho and para, so attack goes there (para is major).
- The –I effect withdraws electrons and is stronger, so the ring is less reactive than benzene and needs harsher conditions.
- In short: the inductive effect controls **reactivity**, resonance controls **orientation**.

**With sodium:** Wurtz–Fittig (ArX + RX + Na gives Ar–R) and Fittig (2ArX + 2Na gives Ar–Ar, e.g. diphenyl).

## 13. Polyhalogen compounds

| Compound | Uses | Hazards |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ce{CH2Cl2}$ | Paint remover, aerosol propellant, solvent for drugs, metal cleaning | Harms the central nervous system; burns skin and eyes |
| $\ce{CHCl3}$ | Solvent (fats, alkaloids, iodine); mainly to make freon R-22; once an anaesthetic | Depresses the CNS; liver damage; turns into phosgene in air and light |
| $\ce{CHI3}$ | Was an antiseptic | Effect is due to the iodine it releases; replaced because of its smell |
| $\ce{CCl4}$ | Making refrigerants, propellants and CFCs; solvent | Liver cancer (some evidence), nerve damage; depletes ozone |
| Freons | Aerosols, refrigeration, air conditioning | Start radical chain reactions that upset the ozone balance in the stratosphere |
| DDT | Insecticide (malaria mosquito, typhus lice) | Not easily broken down; stored in fat; toxic to fish |

- **Freons** are chlorofluorocarbons of methane and ethane: very stable, unreactive, non-toxic, non-corrosive, easily liquefied. Freon-12 is $\ce{CCl2F2}$, made from $\ce{CCl4}$ by the Swarts reaction.
- **DDT** is p,p'-dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane. Made in 1873; Paul Müller found it to be an insecticide in 1939 (Nobel Prize, 1948). Banned in the USA in 1973.
