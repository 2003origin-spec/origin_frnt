No NCERT chapter: Unit 20 of JEE Main 2026 and NEET UG 2026 (the same list), following the NCERT lab manuals. Parts are in the NCERT textbooks: Lassaigne's test (Class 11, Unit 8) and the alcohol, aldehyde and amine tests (Class 12, Units 7–9).

For each topic: **do** (what you do), **see** (what you observe), **why** (the chemistry).

## 1. Detecting N, S and halogens (Lassaigne's test)

- **Do:** heat a small piece of sodium with the organic compound in a fusion tube until red hot, plunge it into distilled water, boil and filter. The filtrate is the **sodium fusion extract**.
- **Why:** covalent N, S and X give no ionic tests. Sodium turns them into $\ce{NaCN}$, $\ce{Na2S}$ and $\ce{NaX}$, which dissolve as ions.

| Element | Do | See |
| --- | --- | --- |
| N | Boil with $\ce{FeSO4}$, then acidify with conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ | Prussian blue |
| N and S | Add $\ce{FeCl3}$ | Blood red |
| S | Acetic acid + lead acetate | Black ppt (PbS) |
| S | Sodium nitroprusside | Violet colour |
| Cl | Conc. $\ce{HNO3}$, boil, then $\ce{AgNO3}$ | White ppt, soluble in $\ce{NH4OH}$ |
| Br | Same | Pale yellow ppt, sparingly soluble |
| I | Same | Yellow ppt, insoluble |

- **Why boil with $\ce{HNO3}$ first:** $\ce{CN-}$ and $\ce{S^2-}$ would also precipitate with $\ce{Ag+}$ (as AgCN and $\ce{Ag2S}$) and give a false result.

## 2. Functional-group tests

### Alcohols (–OH)

- **Sodium metal:** a dry alcohol gives bubbles of $\ce{H2}$ ($\ce{2ROH + 2Na -> 2RONa + H2}$).
- **Ceric ammonium nitrate:** the yellow reagent turns **red**, as a cerium(IV)–alcohol complex forms.
- **Ester test:** warm with acetic acid and a few drops of conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$: a **fruity smell** (ester).
- **Lucas test** (conc. HCl + anhydrous $\ce{ZnCl2}$): the alkyl chloride is insoluble and makes the liquid **cloudy**. Tertiary: at once. Secondary: in about 5 minutes. Primary: no cloudiness at room temperature.

### Phenols (Ar–OH)

- **Litmus:** turns blue litmus red (weak acid), but gives **no** $\ce{CO2}$ with $\ce{NaHCO3}$.
- **Neutral $\ce{FeCl3}$:** violet colour (an iron(III)–phenoxide complex). Make "neutral" $\ce{FeCl3}$ by adding dilute $\ce{NH4OH}$ drop by drop until a slight brown precipitate stays, then filter.
- **Bromine water:** decolourised, white ppt of 2,4,6-tribromophenol.
- **Liebermann's test:** phenol + $\ce{NaNO2}$ + conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ gives a deep green or blue colour; it turns red when poured into water, and blue or green again when made alkaline with NaOH.
- **Phthalein dye test:** heat with phthalic anhydride and conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$, then pour into dilute NaOH: **pink** (phenolphthalein forms).

### Aldehydes and ketones (C=O)

- **2,4-DNP:** both give a **yellow, orange or red** precipitate (a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone). This shows a C=O group is present.
- **Schiff's reagent** (decolourised magenta): aldehydes bring back the **pink** colour; ketones do not.
- **Tollens' reagent:** aldehydes give a **silver mirror**. Ketones do not.
- **Fehling's solution** (A: $\ce{CuSO4}$; B: alkaline Rochelle salt; mix equal volumes): aliphatic aldehydes give a **red-brown** ppt of $\ce{Cu2O}$. Aromatic aldehydes (benzaldehyde) and ketones do not.
- **Sodium nitroprusside** (ketones such as acetone): a **red** colour in NaOH.
- **Iodoform test:** a yellow ppt from methyl ketones ($\ce{CH3CO-}$ bonded to C), ethanal, and alcohols with $\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}$. Acetic acid and its esters do not respond.

### Carboxylic acids (–COOH)

- **Litmus:** blue to red.
- **$\ce{NaHCO3}$:** brisk effervescence of $\ce{CO2}$ (turns lime water milky), because carboxylic acids are stronger than carbonic acid.
- **Ester test:** warm with ethanol and conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$: fruity smell.

### Amines (–NH₂)

- **Solubility:** amines are bases, so they dissolve in dilute HCl.
- **Carbylamine test:** heat with chloroform and ethanolic KOH: a **foul smell** (isocyanide). **Primary** amines only.
- **Azo dye test:** dissolve in dil. HCl, cool to 0–5 °C, add $\ce{NaNO2}$, then alkaline 2-naphthol: an **orange-red dye**. Aromatic **primary** amines only (their diazonium salts are stable when cold).
- **Hinsberg's test** (benzenesulphonyl chloride): primary amine gives a sulphonamide that **dissolves in KOH**; secondary gives one that **does not**; tertiary does not react.

## 3. Preparations

### Mohr's salt, $\ce{FeSO4*(NH4)2SO4*6H2O}$

- **Do:** dissolve equimolar ferrous sulphate and ammonium sulphate in the least hot water with a little dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$. Concentrate, then cool slowly.
- **See:** pale green crystals.
- **Why the acid:** it stops $\ce{Fe^2+}$ from **hydrolysing**. Do not heat for long: $\ce{Fe^2+}$ is oxidised to yellow $\ce{Fe^3+}$.

### Potash alum, $\ce{K2SO4*Al2(SO4)3*24H2O}$

- **Do:** the same method, with equimolar potassium sulphate and aluminium sulphate.
- **See:** colourless, octahedral crystals.
- **Why the acid:** it stops aluminium sulphate from hydrolysing (a milky $\ce{Al(OH)3}$ turbidity).
- Both are **double salts**: in water they give only simple ions ($\ce{Fe^2+}$, $\ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Al^3+}$, $\ce{SO4^2-}$), unlike a complex such as $\ce{K4[Fe(CN)6]}$.

### Acetanilide

- **Do:** add acetic anhydride to aniline in glacial acetic acid, warm, then pour into ice-cold water. Recrystallise from hot water.
- **See:** white, shining crystals (m.p. about 114 °C).
- **Why:** acetylation. The $\ce{-NH2}$ group attacks the C=O of the anhydride; the H on N is replaced by $\ce{CH3CO-}$.

### p-Nitroacetanilide

- **Do:** dissolve acetanilide in glacial acetic acid, add conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$, cool in ice. Add the nitrating mixture (conc. $\ce{HNO3}$ + conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$) drop by drop, keeping it ice-cold. Pour onto crushed ice; recrystallise from ethanol.
- **See:** pale yellow crystals. The ortho isomer is more soluble and stays in the ethanol.
- **Why acetanilide and not aniline:** nitric acid **oxidises** aniline; and in strong acid aniline becomes the anilinium ion, which is **meta**-directing (NCERT: nitrating aniline gives about 47% meta product). The acetyl group makes the ring less reactive (one nitro group only) and its bulk favours **para**.

### Aniline yellow (p-aminoazobenzene)

- **Do:** dissolve aniline in dilute HCl, cool to 0–5 °C, add cold $\ce{NaNO2}$ solution (diazotisation). Add this to aniline dissolved in dilute HCl.
- **See:** a yellow solid (the dye).
- **Why:** the diazonium ion is a weak electrophile. It attacks the para position of aniline (coupling; electrophilic substitution). Keep it cold, because the diazonium salt decomposes on warming.

### Iodoform, $\ce{CHI3}$

- **Do:** warm ethanol or acetone with iodine and NaOH (or sodium carbonate) solution until the iodine colour fades; cool.
- **See:** pale yellow crystals with a typical antiseptic smell.
- **Why:** the haloform reaction (see the iodoform test above).

## 4. Titrations

- **Rinsing:** rinse the burette with the solution it will hold, and the pipette with the solution it will measure. Rinse the titration flask with distilled water **only**.
- **Primary standard:** a pure, stable solid that can be weighed exactly (oxalic acid, Mohr's salt). NaOH (absorbs water and $\ce{CO2}$) and $\ce{KMnO4}$ (not pure, decomposes slowly) are not; they are standardised by titration.
- **Making a standard solution:** mass $= M \times$ molar mass $\times V$ (in L). For 250 mL of 0.1 M oxalic acid: $0.1 \times 126 \times 0.25 = 3.15$ g.
- Repeat until two readings agree (**concordant** readings).

### Acid–base titrations and indicators

- An indicator changes colour over a **pH range**. It must change inside the steep jump in pH at the equivalence point.
- **Strong acid–strong base:** the jump is large (about pH 4 to 10 for 0.1 M solutions), so methyl orange or phenolphthalein both work.
- **Weak acid–strong base** (oxalic acid or acetic acid vs NaOH): the equivalence point is **above 7** (the salt hydrolyses). Use **phenolphthalein**.
- **Strong acid–weak base** ($\ce{Na2CO3}$ or $\ce{NH4OH}$ vs HCl): the equivalence point is **below 7**. Use **methyl orange**.
- **Weak acid–weak base:** no sharp jump, so no simple indicator works.

### Redox titrations with $\ce{KMnO4}$

- $\ce{KMnO4}$ is its own indicator. At the end point one extra drop leaves a **permanent light pink** colour.
- **Dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$** is the acid. HCl is oxidised by $\ce{KMnO4}$ to $\ce{Cl2}$ (too much $\ce{KMnO4}$ used); $\ce{HNO3}$ is an oxidising agent itself. Too little acid gives a **brown ppt of $\ce{MnO2}$**.
- **Oxalic acid:** warm to 50–60 °C first; the reaction is slow at room temperature. Once some $\ce{Mn^2+}$ forms, it speeds up the reaction (**autocatalysis**).
- **Mohr's salt:** titrate **cold**; warming lets air oxidise $\ce{Fe^2+}$. Add dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$ when making the solution.
- $\ce{KMnO4}$ is dark, so read the **upper** meniscus in the burette.

## 5. Salt analysis

The lists: cations $\ce{Pb^2+}$, $\ce{Cu^2+}$, $\ce{Al^3+}$, $\ce{Fe^3+}$, $\ce{Zn^2+}$, $\ce{Ni^2+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{NH4+}$; anions $\ce{CO3^2-}$, $\ce{S^2-}$, $\ce{SO4^2-}$, $\ce{NO3-}$, $\ce{NO2-}$, $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{Br-}$, $\ce{I-}$. Insoluble salts are excluded.

### Preliminary tests

- **Colour of the salt:** blue or green ($\ce{Cu^2+}$), green ($\ce{Ni^2+}$), yellow-brown ($\ce{Fe^3+}$). The other listed cations give white or colourless salts.
- **Flame test** (paste with conc. HCl): $\ce{Ca^2+}$ brick red, $\ce{Ba^2+}$ apple green, $\ce{Cu^2+}$ bluish green.
- **Cobalt nitrate test** (charcoal cavity): blue residue ($\ce{Al^3+}$), green ($\ce{Zn^2+}$), pink ($\ce{Mg^2+}$).

### Anions

- **Dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$ group:** $\ce{CO3^2-}$, $\ce{S^2-}$, $\ce{NO2-}$ give a gas even in the cold.
- **Concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ group:** $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{Br-}$, $\ce{I-}$, $\ce{NO3-}$ react only with the concentrated acid, on warming.
- **Sulphate** reacts with neither; test the water solution with $\ce{BaCl2}$.

### Cations: the group scheme

| Group | Reagent | Listed ions (ppt) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | None (NaOH, heat) | $\ce{NH4+}$ |
| I | Dilute HCl | $\ce{Pb^2+}$ (white $\ce{PbCl2}$) |
| II | $\ce{H2S}$ in dilute HCl | $\ce{Cu^2+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$ (black sulphides) |
| III | $\ce{NH4OH}$ + $\ce{NH4Cl}$ | $\ce{Fe^3+}$, $\ce{Al^3+}$ (hydroxides) |
| IV | $\ce{H2S}$ in $\ce{NH4OH}$ + $\ce{NH4Cl}$ | $\ce{Zn^2+}$ (white), $\ce{Ni^2+}$ (black) |
| V | $\ce{(NH4)2CO3}$ + $\ce{NH4OH}$ + $\ce{NH4Cl}$ | $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$ (white carbonates) |
| VI | None ($\ce{Na2HPO4}$ test) | $\ce{Mg^2+}$ |

**Why it works (solubility product):**
- **Group II:** HCl gives a lot of $\ce{H+}$, which holds back the ionisation of $\ce{H2S}$ (common-ion effect). $[\ce{S^2-}]$ stays very low, so only the least soluble sulphides (CuS, PbS) reach their $K_{sp}$.
- **Group IV:** $\ce{NH4OH}$ removes $\ce{H+}$, so $\ce{H2S}$ ionises more and $[\ce{S^2-}]$ is high. Now ZnS and NiS, with larger $K_{sp}$, precipitate too.
- **Group III:** $\ce{NH4Cl}$ holds back the ionisation of $\ce{NH4OH}$. $[\ce{OH-}]$ is just enough for $\ce{Fe(OH)3}$ and $\ce{Al(OH)3}$ (very small $K_{sp}$), not for the hydroxides of later groups. Before group III, boil off $\ce{H2S}$ and boil with conc. $\ce{HNO3}$ to oxidise any $\ce{Fe^2+}$ to $\ce{Fe^3+}$.
- **Group V:** $\ce{NH4Cl}$ keeps $\ce{Mg^2+}$ in solution, so it is left for group VI.

## 6. Four physical-chemistry experiments

### Enthalpy of solution of $\ce{CuSO4}$

- **Do:** put a known mass of water in a calorimeter (its water equivalent known), note the temperature, add a weighed amount of copper sulphate, stir, note the final temperature.
- **Why:** $\Delta_{sol}H = -q/n$ per mole of salt (formula sheet).
- **See:** **anhydrous** $\ce{CuSO4}$ makes the water **warmer** (about $-66.5$ kJ/mol: hydration releases heat). **Hydrated** $\ce{CuSO4*5H2O}$ makes it slightly **cooler** (about $+11.7$ kJ/mol).

### Enthalpy of neutralisation (strong acid + strong base)

- **Do:** mix equal volumes of, say, 1 M HCl and 1 M NaOH at the same starting temperature in a calorimeter; note the highest temperature.
- **Why:** strong acids and bases are fully ionised, so the only reaction is $\ce{H+ + OH- -> H2O}$. The value is nearly constant, about **−57 kJ per mole of water** formed (books quote −57.1 or −57.3). With a weak acid or base it is smaller in size, because some heat is used to ionise the weak electrolyte.

### Lyophilic and lyophobic sols

(Surface chemistry is not in the current NCERT Class 12 book, but this experiment is still in both lists.)
- **Lyophilic** (starch, gum, gelatin, egg albumin): just warm or stir with water. Starch: make a paste and pour it into boiling water. They are **reversible** and stable.
- **Lyophobic** (need a chemical method):
  - $\ce{Fe(OH)3}$ sol: add $\ce{FeCl3}$ solution drop by drop to **boiling** water (hydrolysis): a red-brown sol, **positively** charged.
  - $\ce{As2S3}$ sol: pass $\ce{H2S}$ into a solution of arsenious oxide (double decomposition): a yellow sol, **negatively** charged.
  - They are **irreversible** and are easily coagulated by electrolytes. Excess electrolyte is removed by **dialysis**.

### Kinetics: iodide ions + hydrogen peroxide

- **Do:** mix KI, dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$, a fixed small amount of sodium thiosulphate and starch; add $\ce{H2O2}$ and start the clock. Note the time $t$ for the blue colour. Repeat with different $[\ce{I-}]$, keeping everything else the same.
- **Why:** thiosulphate turns the iodine back to iodide. Only when it is used up does free $\ce{I2}$ stay and turn starch blue. The same amount of iodine forms each time, so rate $\propto 1/t$.
- **See:** doubling $[\ce{I-}]$ roughly halves $t$. A graph of $1/t$ against $[\ce{I-}]$ is a straight line: the reaction is **first order** in iodide under these conditions.
