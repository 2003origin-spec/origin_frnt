## 1. What amines are

- Amines are **derivatives of ammonia**: one, two or all three H atoms of $\ce{NH3}$ are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups.
- They occur in proteins, vitamins, alkaloids and hormones. Adrenaline and ephedrine (both have a **secondary** amino group) raise blood pressure. Novocain is an anaesthetic used in dentistry. Benadryl, an antihistamine, has a **tertiary** amino group. Quaternary ammonium salts are used as surfactants.

## 2. Structure

- N is **sp³** hybridised. Three sp³ orbitals form bonds; the fourth holds the **lone pair**.
- Shape: **pyramidal**, like $\ce{NH3}$.
- The lone pair squeezes the angles below 109.5°. In trimethylamine the C–N–C angle is **108°**.

## 3. Classification

| Type | Formula | H on N |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary (1°) | $\ce{RNH2}$ | 2 |
| Secondary (2°) | $\ce{R2NH}$ | 1 |
| Tertiary (3°) | $\ce{R3N}$ | 0 |

- 1°, 2°, 3° depends on how many H atoms of $\ce{NH3}$ are replaced, **not** on the carbon the N sits on. $\ce{(CH3)3C-NH2}$ is a **primary** amine.
- **Simple** amines: all groups the same. **Mixed** amines: different groups.
- **Arylamines:** the N is bonded directly to the ring ($\ce{C6H5NH2}$). Benzylamine, $\ce{C6H5CH2NH2}$, is **not** an arylamine; its N is on a $\ce{CH2}$.

## 4. Nomenclature

- **Common names:** alkyl group + amine, as one word (methylamine, ethylmethylamine, trimethylamine).
- **IUPAC, primary:** replace the "e" of the alkane by "amine": $\ce{CH3NH2}$ is **methanamine**; $\ce{CH3CH2CH2NH2}$ is propan-1-amine.
- **Two or more $\ce{-NH2}$ groups:** keep the "e" and add di, tri: $\ce{H2N-CH2-CH2-NH2}$ is **ethane-1,2-diamine**.
- **2° and 3°:** the largest group gives the parent name; the others get the locant **N**: $\ce{CH3NHCH2CH3}$ is N-methylethanamine; $\ce{(C2H5)3N}$ is N,N-diethylethanamine.
- **Arylamines:** aniline (also accepted by IUPAC) = benzenamine. o-Toluidine = 2-methylaniline. p-Bromoaniline = 4-bromobenzenamine.

| Structure | Common name | IUPAC name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ce{(CH3)2CHNH2}$ | Isopropylamine | Propan-2-amine |
| $\ce{(CH3)3N}$ | Trimethylamine | N,N-Dimethylmethanamine |
| $\ce{CH2=CHCH2NH2}$ | Allylamine | Prop-2-en-1-amine |
| $\ce{H2N(CH2)6NH2}$ | Hexamethylenediamine | Hexane-1,6-diamine |

## 5. Preparation (summary)

| Method | Gives | Carbon count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reduction of nitro compounds | 1° (alkyl or aryl) | Same |
| Ammonolysis of RX | Mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° + salt | Same |
| Reduction of nitriles | 1° | One more than RX |
| Reduction of amides ($\ce{LiAlH4}$) | 1° from $\ce{RCONH2}$ | Same as amide |
| Gabriel phthalimide | Pure 1° (alkyl only) | Same as RX |
| Hoffmann bromamide | 1° | One less than amide |

- **Ascent** (one C more): $\ce{RX}$ → $\ce{RCN}$ → $\ce{RCH2NH2}$.
- **Descent** (one C less): $\ce{RCONH2}$ → $\ce{RNH2}$ (Hoffmann).
- Gabriel is preferred for pure 1° amines because ammonolysis gives a mixture.

## 6. Physical properties

- Lower aliphatic amines are **gases with a fishy smell**. 1° amines with 3 or more carbons are liquids; higher ones are solids.
- Aniline and other arylamines are colourless when pure but **turn coloured on storage** (oxidation by air).
- **Solubility:** lower aliphatic amines dissolve in water (hydrogen bonds with water). Solubility falls as the hydrophobic alkyl part grows. NCERT's expected answer: butan-1-ol is more soluble in water than butan-1-amine, because O (3.5) is more electronegative than N (3.0) and makes stronger hydrogen bonds. (Beyond the syllabus: measured data actually show butan-1-amine mixing fully with water; in exams give NCERT's answer.)
- **Boiling points:** 1° and 2° amines are H-bonded to each other; 1° more (two H on N). 3° amines have no H on N, so no intermolecular H-bonding.

Boiling points of isomeric amines:

$$1^\circ > 2^\circ > 3^\circ$$

| Compound (molar mass ≈ 73) | b.p./K |
| --- | --- |
| n-$\ce{C4H9OH}$ (alcohol) | 390.3 |
| n-$\ce{C4H9NH2}$ (1°) | 350.8 |
| $\ce{(C2H5)2NH}$ (2°) | 329.3 |
| $\ce{C2H5N(CH3)2}$ (3°) | 310.5 |
| $\ce{C2H5CH(CH3)2}$ (alkane) | 300.8 |

Alcohol > 1° amine > 2° amine > 3° amine > alkane of similar mass.

## 7. Basic character

- The **lone pair** on N accepts a proton, so amines are bases (Lewis bases). They form salts with acids; NaOH gives the free amine back.
- The more stable the cation ($\ce{RNH3+}$) compared with the amine, the stronger the base.

**Alkylamines vs $\ce{NH3}$:** the alkyl group pushes electrons to N (**+I effect**). The lone pair is more available and the cation is stabilised. So alkylamines are stronger bases than $\ce{NH3}$ (p$K_b$ 3 to 4.22, against 4.75).

**Gas phase:** only +I counts, so 3° > 2° > 1° > $\ce{NH3}$.

**In water**, three effects compete:
- **+I effect:** favours 3° > 2° > 1°.
- **Solvation (H-bonding with water):** the cation with more N–H bonds is solvated better and so is more stable. $\ce{RNH3+}$ > $\ce{R2NH2+}$ > $\ce{R3NH+}$. This favours 1° > 2° > 3°.
- **Steric hindrance:** groups bigger than $\ce{CH3}$ get in the way of H-bonding.

So methyl and ethyl series give different orders in water:
- Methyl: $\ce{(CH3)2NH}$ > $\ce{CH3NH2}$ > $\ce{(CH3)3N}$ > $\ce{NH3}$
- Ethyl: $\ce{(C2H5)2NH}$ > $\ce{(C2H5)3N}$ > $\ce{C2H5NH2}$ > $\ce{NH3}$

**Arylamines vs $\ce{NH3}$:** in aniline the lone pair is in **conjugation** with the ring. Aniline has **5** resonance structures, the anilinium ion only **2** (Kekulé). Aniline is stabilised more than its cation, so it takes a proton less readily. Aniline (p$K_b$ 9.38) is a much weaker base than $\ce{NH3}$.

**Substituted anilines:** electron-releasing groups ($\ce{-OCH3}$, $\ce{-CH3}$) **increase** basic strength; electron-withdrawing groups ($\ce{-NO2}$, $\ce{-SO3H}$, $\ce{-COOH}$, $\ce{-X}$) **decrease** it.

## 8. Reactions of the N–H and the lone pair

- **Alkylation:** with RX, up to the quaternary salt (as in ammonolysis).
- **Acylation:** 1° and 2° amines + acid chloride, anhydride or ester give **amides**. A base stronger than the amine (pyridine) removes the HCl. With benzoyl chloride it is called **benzoylation**. With carboxylic acids, amines only form **salts** at room temperature.
- **Carbylamine reaction:** 1° amine + $\ce{CHCl3}$ + ethanolic KOH, heated, gives a foul-smelling **isocyanide**. Test for 1° amines.
- **Nitrous acid** ($\ce{NaNO2}$ + mineral acid, made in situ):
  - 1° aliphatic: unstable diazonium salt, then **alcohol + $\ce{N2}$** (quantitative, used to estimate amino acids and proteins);
  - 1° aromatic, at 273–278 K: **diazonium salt** (diazotisation);
  - 2° and 3°: NCERT only says they react "in a different manner". (Exam extra, not in NCERT but under "identification of amines" in the JEE Main and NEET syllabi: 2° amines give a yellow, oily **N-nitrosamine**.)
- **Hinsberg's reagent** (benzenesulphonyl chloride): see the table below.

## 9. Telling 1°, 2° and 3° amines apart

| Test | 1° | 2° | 3° |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbylamine | Foul smell | No | No |
| Hinsberg product | Soluble in alkali | Insoluble in alkali | No reaction |
| $\ce{HNO2}$ (aliphatic) | $\ce{N2}$ gas | No $\ce{N2}$ | No $\ce{N2}$ |

The Hinsberg test also **separates** a mixture of amines. Today **p-toluenesulphonyl chloride** is used in place of benzenesulphonyl chloride.

## 10. Ring reactions of aniline

- $\ce{-NH2}$ is a **strong activator** and **ortho/para directing**: resonance puts high electron density at o and p.
- **Bromine water** at room temperature: white precipitate of **2,4,6-tribromoaniline** at once. Hard to stop at one Br.
- **Control by acetylation:** $\ce{(CH3CO)2O}$ turns $\ce{-NH2}$ into $\ce{-NHCOCH3}$. The N lone pair is now shared with the C=O by resonance, so it activates the ring less. Substitute, then hydrolyse back. Gives **4-bromoaniline** or **p-nitroaniline** as the major product.
- **Direct nitration** (288 K): tarry oxidation products, plus p 51%, m 47%, o 2%. In strong acid aniline becomes the **anilinium ion**, which is **meta directing**.
- **Sulphonation:** conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ gives anilinium hydrogensulphate; heating at 453–473 K gives **sulphanilic acid** (p-aminobenzenesulphonic acid), a zwitter ion.
- **No Friedel–Crafts:** aniline forms a salt with $\ce{AlCl3}$ (a Lewis acid). N gets a positive charge and deactivates the ring.

## 11. Diazonium salts

- Formula $\ce{ArN2+ X-}$, with $\ce{X-}$ = $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{Br-}$, $\ce{HSO4-}$, $\ce{BF4-}$. $\ce{-N2+}$ is the **diazonium group**. $\ce{C6H5N2+ Cl-}$ = benzenediazonium chloride.
- **Diazotisation:** aniline + $\ce{NaNO2}$ + HCl at **273–278 K**.
- **Stability:** aryl diazonium salts are stable for a short time in cold solution, because of **resonance** with the ring. Alkyl diazonium salts are highly unstable. Diazonium salts are used at once, not stored.
- Benzenediazonium chloride: **colourless crystalline solid**, very soluble in water, stable in the cold, reacts with water when warmed, decomposes easily when dry. Benzenediazonium **fluoroborate** is insoluble in water and stable at room temperature.

**Two kinds of reaction:**
- **$\ce{N2}$ leaves** (it is a very good leaving group): replaced by Cl, Br, CN (Sandmeyer with Cu(I), or Gattermann with Cu powder), I (KI), F (via $\ce{HBF4}$, then heat), H ($\ce{H3PO2}$ or ethanol), OH (warm water, 283 K), $\ce{NO2}$ (fluoroborate + $\ce{NaNO2}$, Cu, heat).
- **$\ce{N2}$ stays (coupling):** with phenol (alkaline) or aniline (acidic), at the **para** position. The products, joined by $\ce{-N=N-}$, have an extended conjugated system, so they are **coloured** and used as **azo dyes**.

## 12. Why diazonium salts matter in synthesis

- They bring in $\ce{-F}$, $\ce{-Cl}$, $\ce{-Br}$, $\ce{-I}$, $\ce{-CN}$, $\ce{-OH}$, $\ce{-NO2}$ in places direct substitution can't reach.
- **Aryl fluorides and iodides** cannot be made by direct halogenation.
- $\ce{-CN}$ cannot be put on by substituting the Cl of chlorobenzene, but **cyanobenzene** forms easily from the diazonium salt.
- Removing $\ce{-NH2}$ ($\ce{H3PO2}$) after it has done its directing job gives patterns such as **1,3,5-tribromobenzene** from aniline.
