## 1. Position and definition

- **d-block:** groups 3–12; the $(n-1)d$ orbitals fill. Series: **3d** (Sc–Zn), **4d** (Y–Cd), **5d** (La, Hf–Hg), **6d** (Ac, Rf–Cn).
- **f-block:** **lanthanoids** (4f: Ce–Lu) and **actinoids** (5f: Th–Lr), the inner transition elements.
- **IUPAC:** a transition metal has an **incomplete d subshell in the atom or in its ions**.
- Zn, Cd, Hg are $d^{10}$ in the atom and in their common ions, so they are **not** transition elements.
- Ag ($4d^{10}5s^1$) **is** one: $\ce{Ag^2+}$ is $4d^9$.

## 2. Electronic configuration

- $(n-1)d^{1-10}\, ns^{1-2}$; Pd is $4d^{10} 5s^0$.
- The $(n-1)d$–$ns$ gap is tiny and half-filled or full d sets are extra stable: **Cr $3d^5 4s^1$**, **Cu $3d^{10} 4s^1$**.
- Ions lose **ns electrons first**.

## 3. Physical properties

- Hard metals with high melting and boiling points (not Zn, Cd, Hg): $(n-1)d$ electrons join the metallic bonding. In each row the melting point peaks near $d^5$ (Mn and Tc are anomalous).
- **High enthalpies of atomisation**, highest mid-series (one unpaired electron per d orbital bonds strongly). Zn is lowest (126 kJ/mol): no d electrons in its metallic bond.
- 4d and 5d metals have higher atomisation enthalpies than 3d, so metal–metal bonds are common in heavy-metal compounds. Metals with very high atomisation enthalpy **tend to be noble**.

## 4. Atomic and ionic sizes

- Along a series, radii fall slowly: a new d electron shields poorly.
- 3d → 4d: bigger. 4d → 5d: **almost equal** (Zr 160 pm, Hf 159 pm), because of the **lanthanoid contraction** (4f fills first and shields very poorly).
- Density rises from Ti to Cu (smaller radius, bigger mass).

## 5. Ionisation enthalpies

- Rise along a series, much **less steeply** than for main-group elements, so variable numbers of d electrons can be lost.
- 2nd IE is unusually **high for Cr and Cu** ($\ce{Cr+}$ is $d^5$, $\ce{Cu+}$ is $d^{10}$) and low for Zn.
- The 2nd IE of Mn (below Cr's) and the 3rd IE of Fe (below Mn's) break the rising trend: these steps **make** a $d^5$ ion.
- 3rd IE is high for Mn ($\ce{Mn^2+}$ is $d^5$) and Zn ($d^{10}$). High 3rd IEs of Ni, Cu, Zn: they rarely go above +2.
- **Exchange energy** (roughly, pairs of parallel spins) stabilises a configuration. Removing an electron from $d^5$ costs exchange energy; removing the paired electron from $d^6$ costs none. So $\ce{Fe^2+}$ ionises more easily than $\ce{Mn^2+}$.

## 6. Oxidation states

| Element | States (NCERT Table 4.3) |
| --- | --- |
| Sc | +3 only |
| Ti | +2, +3, +4 |
| V | +2 to +5 |
| Cr | +2 to +6 |
| Mn | +2 to +7 |
| Fe | +2, +3, +4, +6 |
| Co, Ni | +2, +3, +4 |
| Cu | +1, +2 |
| Zn | +2 only |

- Most states mid-series (**Mn**). Sc, Ti have too few electrons; Cu, Zn too many d electrons.
- Up to Mn, the highest stable state = s + d electrons: $\ce{TiO2}$, $\ce{VO2+}$, $\ce{CrO4^2-}$, $\ce{MnO4-}$. After Mn it drops sharply.
- States differ by **one** (V +2, +3, +4, +5); in the p-block by two.
- **Down a d-group, higher states are more stable** (opposite to the p-block): Mo(VI), W(VI) are more stable than Cr(VI), so dichromate is a strong oxidant but $\ce{MoO3}$, $\ce{WO3}$ are not.
- **Zero** oxidation state with π-acceptor ligands: $\ce{Ni(CO)4}$, $\ce{Fe(CO)5}$.

## 7. Electrode potentials and stability

- **$\ce{M^2+}/\ce{M}$** gets less negative along the series ($\Delta_i H_1 + \Delta_i H_2$ grows). **Cu is positive** (+0.34 V): its high atomisation and ionisation enthalpies are not repaid by hydration. It gives no $\ce{H2}$ with acids; only nitric and hot concentrated sulphuric acid attack it.
- Mn, Ni, Zn are more negative than the trend: $d^5$, most negative hydration enthalpy, $d^{10}$.
- **$\ce{M^3+}/\ce{M^2+}$:** high for Mn (+1.57 V, $\ce{Mn^2+}$ is $d^5$) and Co (+1.97 V), so $\ce{Mn^3+}$ and $\ce{Co^3+}$ are strong oxidants. Low for Fe ($\ce{Fe^3+}$ is $d^5$), V ($\ce{V^2+}$ has a half-filled $t_{2g}$ level) and Sc (noble-gas $\ce{Sc^3+}$). Highest for Zn (the $d^{10}$ of $\ce{Zn^2+}$ would break).
- $\ce{Ti^2+}$, $\ce{V^2+}$, $\ce{Cr^2+}$ are strong reductants: $\ce{2Cr^2+ + 2H+ -> 2Cr^3+ + H2}$.

**Highest states in halides and oxides**
- Halides: $\ce{TiX4}$, $\ce{VF5}$, $\ce{CrF6}$; Mn(VII) only as $\ce{MnO3F}$. Beyond Mn only $\ce{FeX3}$ and $\ce{CoF3}$ as trihalides.
- **Oxygen beats fluorine** because it forms multiple bonds to metals: $\ce{MnF4}$ but $\ce{Mn2O7}$.
- Oxides reach the group number from $\ce{Sc2O3}$ to $\ce{Mn2O7}$; nothing above $\ce{Fe2O3}$ for iron.
- $\ce{CuI2}$ is unknown: $\ce{2Cu^2+ + 4I- -> Cu2I2(s) + I2}$.
- $\ce{Cu+}$ disproportionates in water ($\ce{2Cu+ -> Cu^2+ + Cu}$): the much more negative hydration enthalpy of $\ce{Cu^2+}$ more than pays for the second IE.

## 8. Magnetic properties

- Diamagnetic: repelled. Paramagnetic: attracted (unpaired electrons). Ferromagnetic: very strongly attracted (extreme paramagnetism).
- 3d ions: $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}$ BM (orbital part quenched). The measured moment gives the number of unpaired electrons.

## 9. Coloured ions

- A **d–d transition** absorbs visible light; we see the **complementary** colour. The ligand sets the frequency.
- $d^0$ and $d^{10}$ ions are colourless: $\ce{Sc^3+}$, $\ce{Ti^4+}$, $\ce{Zn^2+}$, $\ce{Cu+}$.

## 10. Complexes, catalysts, interstitial compounds, alloys

- **Complexes:** small ions, high charge, free d orbitals.
- **Catalysts:** variable oxidation states and complex formation; on a surface, bonding to the metal raises reactant concentration and weakens their bonds. $\ce{V2O5}$ (Contact process), Fe (Haber), Ni (hydrogenation). $\ce{Fe^3+}$ catalyses iodide + persulphate by cycling:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2Fe^3+ + 2I-} \\ & \ce{-> 2Fe^2+ + I2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2Fe^2+ + S2O8^2-} \\ & \ce{-> 2Fe^3+ + 2SO4^2-} \end{aligned}$$
- **Interstitial compounds:** H, C or N trapped in the metal lattice (TiC, $\ce{Mn4N}$, $\ce{Fe3H}$, $\ce{TiH_{1.7}}$). Non-stoichiometric; higher melting than the metal, very hard, metallic conductors, chemically inert.
- **Alloys** form when radii differ by less than about 15%: steels (Cr, V, W, Mo, Mn), brass (Cu–Zn), bronze (Cu–Sn).

## 11. Oxides, dichromate and permanganate

- Higher oxidation state → **less ionic, more acidic** oxide. $\ce{Mn2O7}$ is a covalent green oil; $\ce{Mn2O7}$ gives $\ce{HMnO4}$, $\ce{CrO3}$ gives $\ce{H2CrO4}$ and $\ce{H2Cr2O7}$.
- $\ce{V2O3}$ basic, $\ce{V2O4}$ less basic, $\ce{V2O5}$ amphoteric (mainly acidic). CrO basic, $\ce{Cr2O3}$ amphoteric.
- Oxocations: $\ce{VO2+}$ (V(V)), $\ce{VO^2+}$ (V(IV)), $\ce{TiO^2+}$ (Ti(IV)).
- **$\ce{K2Cr2O7}$:** chromite ore ($\ce{FeCr2O4}$) → yellow chromate → orange dichromate → KCl swap (full equations on the Trends and Reactions Sheet). $\ce{CrO4^2-}$ is tetrahedral; $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ is two tetrahedra sharing a corner (Cr–O–Cr 126°). Used in leather industry, for azo compounds, and as a **primary standard**.
- **$\ce{KMnO4}$:** pyrolusite ($\ce{MnO2}$) → green $\ce{K2MnO4}$ → disproportionation or electrolytic oxidation. Dark purple crystals, isostructural with $\ce{KClO4}$, 6.4 g per 100 g water at 293 K, decomposes at 513 K.
- Manganate (Mn(VI), $d^1$) is **paramagnetic**; permanganate (Mn(VII), $d^0$) is **diamagnetic**. Both tetrahedral, with O p–Mn d π bonding.
- $\ce{KMnO4}$: acid → $\ce{Mn^2+}$; neutral or faintly alkaline → $\ce{MnO2}$. Titrations in HCl are unsatisfactory: the HCl is oxidised to $\ce{Cl2}$. Uses: analysis, organic oxidant, bleaching wool, cotton, silk; decolourising oils.

## 12. Lanthanoids

- All $6s^2$ with variable 4f; every $\ce{Ln^3+}$ is $4f^n$.
- **Lanthanoid contraction:** radii fall from La to Lu (smooth for $\ce{Ln^3+}$, less regular for atoms) because 4f electrons shield each other poorly. Result: Zr and Hf have nearly equal size, occur together and are hard to separate.
- **+3** is the main state. +4 and +2 appear occasionally, mainly where they give an empty, half-filled or full f subshell ($f^0$, $f^7$, $f^{14}$). Not every case fits this (Pr, Nd, Dy +4; Sm +2):
  - $\ce{Ce^4+}$ ($f^0$): strong oxidant ($E^\circ$ = +1.74 V), but slow with water, so a good analytical reagent; $\ce{Tb^4+}$ ($f^7$) is an oxidant; Pr, Nd, Tb, Dy show +4 only in oxides $\ce{MO2}$;
  - $\ce{Eu^2+}$ ($f^7$) and $\ce{Yb^2+}$ ($f^{14}$) are reductants; Sm shows +2 like Eu.
- Silvery white, soft, tarnish in air. Hardness rises with Z (Sm is steel-hard). Melting points 1000–1200 K (Sm 1623 K).
- $\ce{Ln^3+}$ ions are mostly coloured (f electrons, narrow bands); $\ce{La^3+}$ and $\ce{Lu^3+}$ are colourless. All are paramagnetic except $f^0$ ($\ce{La^3+}$, $\ce{Ce^4+}$) and $f^{14}$ ($\ce{Yb^2+}$, $\ce{Lu^3+}$).
- IE1 about 600, IE2 about 1200 kJ/mol (like Ca). IE3 abnormally low for La, Gd, Lu.
- Early members react like Ca, later ones like Al. $E^\circ(\ce{Ln^3+}/\ce{Ln})$ = −2.2 to −2.4 V (Eu −2.0 V).
- They burn in $\ce{O2}$ and halogens, give $\ce{H2}$ with dilute acids and water, and form sulphides, nitrides and carbides on heating. $\ce{Ln(OH)3}$ are basic, like alkaline earth hydroxides.
- **Mischmetall:** ~95% lanthanoid metal, ~5% Fe, traces of S, C, Ca, Al. Used in Mg-based alloys for bullets, shells and lighter flints. Mixed oxides: cracking catalysts; oxides: phosphors in TV screens.

## 13. Actinoids

- Th to Lr, all **radioactive** (Lr half-life about 3 minutes; later members only in nanogram amounts).
- $7s^2$ with variable 5f and 6d. Th is $6d^2 7s^2$ (no 5f). 5f orbitals are **less buried** than 4f, so 5f electrons bond much more.
- **Actinoid contraction** is larger per step than the lanthanoid contraction (5f shields even more poorly).
- Wider range of states (5f, 6d, 7s close in energy). +3 general; maximum Th +4, Pa +5, U +6, Np +7 (Pu also +7 in NCERT's Table 4.11), then lower. More +3 compounds than +4; both ions hydrolyse.
- Very reactive, especially finely divided. HCl attacks all; nitric acid barely (oxide layer); alkalis do nothing.
- Early actinoid IEs are **lower** than early lanthanoid IEs (5f electrons better shielded).

| Feature | Lanthanoids | Actinoids |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Orbitals filling | 4f | 5f |
| Oxidation states | Mainly +3 | +3 up to +7 |
| Contraction per step | Smaller | Larger |
| Magnetism | Simpler | More complex |

## 14. Uses

$\ce{MnO2}$ in dry cells; Zn, Ni/Cd in batteries; Cu, Ag, Au coinage metals; AgBr in photography. Catalysts: $\ce{TiCl4}$ + $\ce{Al(CH3)3}$ (Ziegler, polythene), $\ce{PdCl2}$ (Wacker process: ethene → ethanal; NCERT's text says "ethyne", see Traps), Ni complexes (polymerising alkynes). NCERT also names "TiO" for the pigment industry; the well-known white pigment is titanium dioxide, $\ce{TiO2}$, so this looks like a misprint.
