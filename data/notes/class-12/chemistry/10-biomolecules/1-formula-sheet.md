## Carbohydrates: definition and classes

:::formula{title="General formula (old idea)"}
$$\ce{C_x(H2O)_y}$$
Most carbohydrates fit it, but it is **not** a test. Acetic acid, $\ce{C2(H2O)2}$, fits and is not a carbohydrate. Rhamnose, $\ce{C6H12O5}$, is a carbohydrate and does not fit.

Correct definition (NCERT): optically active **polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones**, or compounds that give such units on hydrolysis.
:::

| Class | Units on hydrolysis | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monosaccharide | Cannot be hydrolysed | Glucose, fructose, ribose |
| Oligosaccharide | 2 to 10 | Sucrose, maltose, lactose |
| Polysaccharide | A large number | Starch, cellulose, glycogen |

| C atoms | Aldose | Ketose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | Aldotriose | Ketotriose |
| 5 | Aldopentose (ribose) | Ketopentose |
| 6 | Aldohexose (glucose) | Ketohexose (fructose) |

## Making glucose

:::formula{title="From sucrose (cane sugar)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C12H22O11 + H2O} \\ & \ce{->[H+] C6H12O6 + C6H12O6} \end{aligned}$$
Boil sucrose with dilute HCl or $\ce{H2SO4}$ in alcoholic solution. The products are glucose and fructose in **equal amounts**.
:::

:::formula{title="From starch (commercial method)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{(C6H10O5)_n + nH2O} \\ & \ce{->[H+] nC6H12O6} \end{aligned}$$
Boil starch with dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$ at **393 K** under **2–3 atm** pressure.
:::

## Reactions of glucose and what they prove

Open chain: $\ce{OHC-(CHOH)4-CH2OH}$ (molecular formula $\ce{C6H12O6}$).

| Reagent | Product | Shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HI, long heating | n-Hexane | 6 C in a straight chain |
| $\ce{NH2OH}$ | Oxime | C=O group |
| HCN | Cyanohydrin | C=O group |
| Bromine water | Gluconic acid | C=O is –CHO |
| Acetic anhydride | Pentaacetate | 5 –OH groups |
| $\ce{HNO3}$ | Saccharic acid | A 1° alcohol (–$\ce{CH2OH}$) |

Gluconic acid is $\ce{HOOC-(CHOH)4-CH2OH}$. Saccharic acid is $\ce{HOOC-(CHOH)4-COOH}$.

:::trap
Facts the open chain **cannot** explain: no Schiff's test, no $\ce{NaHSO3}$ addition product, the pentaacetate does not react with $\ce{NH2OH}$, and glucose has two crystalline forms (α and β). The cyclic hemiacetal (C-5 –OH added to C-1) explains all four.
:::

## Sugars at a glance

| Sugar | Made of | Reducing? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sucrose | α-D-glucose + β-D-fructose (C1–C2) | No |
| Maltose | Two α-D-glucose (C1–C4) | Yes |
| Lactose | β-D-galactose + β-D-glucose (C1–C4) | Yes |
| Amylose | α-D-glucose, C1–C4, no branches | — |
| Amylopectin | α-D-glucose, C1–C4 chain, C1–C6 branches | — |
| Cellulose | β-D-glucose, C1–C4, straight | — |
| Glycogen | Like amylopectin, more branched | — |

## Counting rules

:::formula{title="Invert sugar rotation (from NCERT values)"}
$$[\alpha]_{mix} = \tfrac12\,(+52.5^\circ - 92.4^\circ)$$
$$[\alpha]_{mix} \approx -20^\circ$$
Glucose $+52.5^\circ$, fructose $-92.4^\circ$. The halving works because hydrolysis gives **equal moles** of two isomers, so equal masses. The sign changes from + (sucrose) to −, hence "invert" sugar.
:::

:::formula{title="Peptide bonds in a chain"}
$$\text{peptide bonds} = n - 1$$
$n$ = number of amino acid residues. Making the chain releases $n - 1$ water molecules. Holds for one **open** (linear) chain.
:::

:::formula{title="Molar mass of a peptide"}
$$M = \textstyle\sum M_{\text{amino acids}} - 18(n - 1)$$
$M$ in g/mol; 18 g/mol is water. Same condition: one linear chain.
:::

:::formula{title="Glucose units in a polysaccharide"}
$$n \approx \frac{M}{162}$$
Each $\ce{C6H10O5}$ unit is 162 g/mol (glucose 180 minus water 18). Good for large chains, where the end groups don't matter.
:::

:::formula{title="Stereoisomers (exam extra)"}
$$N = 2^n$$
Exam extra: stereoisomerism is in the JEE Main and NEET syllabi, but this rule is not in NCERT. $N$ = number of stereoisomers, $n$ = number of chiral carbons. Valid only when the molecule has no internal symmetry (it fails for tartaric acid, for example). Open-chain aldohexose: 4 chiral C (C-2 to C-5), so 16 (8 D and 8 L). The ring form adds C-1 as a new chiral centre, which gives the α and β anomers.
:::

:::formula{title="Base pairing in DNA (Chargaff's rule)"}
$$\%\text{A} = \%\text{T}$$
$$\%\text{G} = \%\text{C}$$
Named in NCERT Class 12 Biology, not in this chapter; it follows from the pairing here (A with T, C with G). So $\%\text{A} + \%\text{G} = 50\%$. Only for **double-stranded** DNA, not for single-stranded RNA.
:::

## Vitamins

Fat soluble: **A, D, E, K** (stored in liver and fat). Water soluble: **B group and C** (not stored, except B₁₂).

| Vitamin | Sources | Deficiency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Fish liver oil, carrots, butter, milk | Xerophthalmia, night blindness |
| B₁ (thiamine) | Yeast, milk, green vegetables, cereals | Beri beri |
| B₂ (riboflavin) | Milk, egg white, liver, kidney | Cheilosis |
| B₆ (pyridoxine) | Yeast, milk, egg yolk, cereals, grams | Convulsions |
| B₁₂ | Meat, fish, egg, curd | Pernicious anaemia |
| C (ascorbic acid) | Citrus fruits, amla, leafy greens | Scurvy |
| D | Sunlight, fish, egg yolk | Rickets, osteomalacia |
| E | Wheat germ oil, sunflower oil | Fragile RBCs, muscle weakness |
| K | Green leafy vegetables | Longer blood clotting time |
