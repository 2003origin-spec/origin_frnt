## 1. Carbohydrates: what they are

- Carbohydrates are optically active **polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones**, or compounds that give these on hydrolysis.
- The old formula $\ce{C_x(H2O)_y}$ ("hydrates of carbon") gave the name, but it does not define them: acetic acid fits it, rhamnose ($\ce{C6H12O5}$) does not.
- Sweet carbohydrates are called **sugars**. Cane sugar is sucrose; milk sugar is lactose. Another name is **saccharides**.

**Classes (by hydrolysis):**
- **Monosaccharides:** can't be hydrolysed further. About 20 occur in nature (glucose, fructose, ribose).
- **Oligosaccharides:** give 2 to 10 monosaccharide units. Disaccharides are the most common.
- **Polysaccharides:** give a large number of units (starch, cellulose, glycogen, gums). Not sweet, so also called **non-sugars**.

**Reducing sugars** reduce Fehling's solution and Tollens' reagent. **All monosaccharides**, aldoses and ketoses alike, are reducing sugars.

A monosaccharide with a –CHO group is an **aldose**; with a keto group, a **ketose**. The carbon count goes into the name: glucose is an aldohexose, fructose a ketohexose.

## 2. Glucose

- Also called **dextrose**. Found in sweet fruits, honey and ripe grapes. Monomer of starch and cellulose; probably the most abundant organic compound on earth.
- **Preparation:** boil sucrose with dilute acid in alcoholic solution (gives glucose + fructose), or, commercially, boil starch with dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$ at 393 K and 2–3 atm.

**Evidence for the open chain** $\ce{OHC-(CHOH)4-CH2OH}$:
- HI gives **n-hexane**: 6 C in a straight chain.
- **Oxime** ($\ce{NH2OH}$) and **cyanohydrin** (HCN): a C=O group.
- **Bromine water** gives **gluconic acid**: the C=O is an aldehyde.
- **Pentaacetate**: five –OH groups, on five different carbons (it is stable).
- $\ce{HNO3}$ gives **saccharic acid** (from glucose and from gluconic acid): a primary alcohol group.

**Fischer** worked out the exact arrangement of the –OH groups.

## 3. D and L, (+) and (−)

- **D/L** is the **relative configuration**, compared with glyceraldehyde. **(+)/(−)** is the measured direction of optical rotation. The two are **not related**.
- (+)-Glyceraldehyde is D: its –OH is on the **right** in the standard drawing.
- For a sugar, write the most oxidised carbon (–CHO) at the top and look only at the **lowest asymmetric carbon** (C-5 in glucose). –OH on the right means D.
- So natural glucose is **D-(+)-glucose**; fructose is D-(−) (section 5).

## 4. Cyclic structure of glucose

The open chain fails to explain these facts:
1. No **Schiff's test**, and no addition product with $\ce{NaHSO3}$, even though there is a –CHO group.
2. The **pentaacetate** does not react with $\ce{NH2OH}$: no free –CHO.
3. Glucose has **two crystalline forms**: α (m.p. 419 K) and β (m.p. 423 K).

**Explanation:** the –OH at **C-5** adds to the –CHO group to form a six-membered **cyclic hemiacetal**.
- The ring has 5 C and 1 O, like **pyran**, so it is a **pyranose** ring.
- C-1 becomes a new chiral centre, called the **anomeric carbon**. The α and β forms differ only in the –OH at C-1. They are **anomers**.
- Both cyclic forms are in **equilibrium** with a small amount of the open chain.
- The ring is drawn more correctly as a **Haworth structure**.

## 5. Fructose

- A **ketohexose**, $\ce{C6H12O6}$, with the C=O at **C-2** and six carbons in a straight chain.
- Found in fruits, honey and vegetables; used as a sweetener.
- **D-series** and **laevorotatory**: D-(−)-fructose.
- Rings: the –OH at **C-5** adds to the C=O at C-2, giving a **five-membered** ring (4 C, 1 O) called **furanose**, after furan. It also has two anomers.

## 6. Disaccharides

Two monosaccharides join through an oxygen atom, losing one water molecule. This C–O–C link is the **glycosidic linkage**.
- If the reducing groups (–CHO or C=O) of **both** units are used in the link, the sugar is **non-reducing** (sucrose).
- If one stays free, it is **reducing** (maltose, lactose).

| Disaccharide | Units and link | Reducing? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sucrose | α-D-glucose C1 – C2 β-D-fructose | No |
| Maltose | α-D-glucose C1 – C4 α-D-glucose | Yes |
| Lactose | β-D-galactose C1 – C4 β-D-glucose | Yes |

- **Sucrose** is dextrorotatory. Hydrolysis gives equal moles of glucose (+52.5°) and fructose (−92.4°). Fructose wins, so the mixture is laevorotatory. The sign flips from + to −, and the product is called **invert sugar**.
- **Maltose** and **lactose** (milk sugar) are reducing because a free –CHO can form in solution at C-1 of the second glucose (maltose) or of the glucose unit (lactose).

## 7. Polysaccharides

These mainly store food or build structures.

| Polysaccharide | Units and links | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amylose | α-D-glucose, C1–C4, unbranched | Plants |
| Amylopectin | α-D-glucose, C1–C4 chain, C1–C6 branches | Plants |
| Cellulose | β-D-glucose, C1–C4, straight chain | Plant cell walls |
| Glycogen | Like amylopectin, more branched | Liver, muscles, brain |

- **Starch:** the main plant store and our chief dietary source. Amylose (15–20%, 200–1000 units) is **water soluble**; amylopectin (80–85%) is **insoluble**.
- **Cellulose:** only in plants; the most abundant organic substance in the plant kingdom.
- **Glycogen** ("animal starch"): broken down to glucose when the body needs it. Also in yeast and fungi.
- **Uses:** food (honey for instant energy), wood and cotton (cellulose), textiles, paper, lacquers, breweries. **D-ribose** and **2-deoxy-D-ribose** are in nucleic acids.

## 8. Amino acids

- Contain –$\ce{NH2}$ and –COOH groups. Proteins give only **α-amino acids** on hydrolysis: $\ce{R-CH(NH2)-COOH}$.
- Trivial names reflect source or property: **glycine** is sweet (glykos), **tyrosine** came from cheese (tyros). Each has a 3-letter and a 1-letter code (Gly, G).
- **Acidic:** more –COOH than –$\ce{NH2}$ (aspartic acid, glutamic acid). **Basic:** more –$\ce{NH2}$ (lysine, arginine). **Neutral:** equal numbers (glycine, alanine).
- **Essential** amino acids can't be made in the body and must come from the diet. NCERT lists **ten**: valine, leucine, isoleucine, arginine, lysine, threonine, methionine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, histidine. The rest are **non-essential**. (Many nutrition books list nine for adults and call arginine semi-essential, as the body makes some of it. Use NCERT's ten in exams.)

**Properties:**
- Colourless, crystalline, water soluble, high-melting. They behave like **salts**, not like simple amines or acids.
- In water, –COOH loses $\ce{H+}$ and –$\ce{NH2}$ gains it, giving a **zwitter ion** (dipolar ion):

$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{H2N-CHR-COOH} \\ & \ce{<=> H3N^+-CHR-COO^-} \end{aligned}$$

- The zwitter ion is neutral overall and **amphoteric**: it reacts with both acids and bases.
- All natural α-amino acids except **glycine** are optically active. Most are **L**: drawn with –$\ce{NH2}$ on the **left**.

## 9. Peptides and proteins

- The –COOH of one amino acid joins the –$\ce{NH2}$ of another, losing water. The amide link –CO–NH– is the **peptide bond**.
- Glycine (–COOH end) + alanine (–$\ce{NH2}$ end) gives the dipeptide **glycylalanine** (Gly-Ala):

$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{H2N-CH2-COOH} \\ & \ce{+ H2N-CH(CH3)-COOH} \\ & \ce{-> H2N-CH2-CO-NH-} \\ & \quad \ce{CH(CH3)-COOH + H2O} \end{aligned}$$

- 2 amino acids: dipeptide; 3: tripeptide (2 peptide bonds); more than 10: **polypeptide**.
- **Protein:** a polypeptide with more than 100 residues and molecular mass above 10,000 u. The line is not sharp: **insulin** (51 amino acids) is called a protein because it has a well-defined shape.

| | Fibrous | Globular |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Chains parallel, fibre-like | Chains coiled into a sphere |
| In water | Insoluble | Usually soluble |
| Examples | Keratin, myosin | Insulin, albumins |

Fibrous chains are held together by hydrogen and disulphide bonds. NCERT says keratin is in hair, wool and silk; myosin is in muscles. (Strictly, silk's main protein is **fibroin**, also fibrous; keep NCERT's line for exams.)

## 10. Four levels of protein structure

1. **Primary:** the **sequence** of amino acids. Change one amino acid and you get a different protein.
2. **Secondary:** the shape of the chain, from H-bonds between C=O and –NH– groups of peptide bonds.
   - **α-Helix:** a **right-handed** coil; each –NH– bonds to a C=O on the next turn.
   - **β-Pleated sheet:** chains stretched out side by side, joined by **intermolecular** H-bonds, like the folds of drapery.
3. **Tertiary:** overall folding of the chain, giving fibrous or globular shapes.
4. **Quaternary:** how two or more chains (**sub-units**) are arranged. Haemoglobin is NCERT's example.

2° and 3° structures are held by **hydrogen bonds, disulphide links, van der Waals forces and electrostatic attraction**.

## 11. Denaturation

- A protein with its natural 3D shape and activity is a **native protein**.
- Heat or a change of pH disturbs the H-bonds: globules unfold, helices uncoil and activity is lost. This is **denaturation**.
- **2° and 3° structures are destroyed; the 1° structure stays.**
- Examples: egg white **coagulating** on boiling; **curdling of milk** (lactic acid made by bacteria).

## 12. Enzymes

- **Biocatalysts.** Almost all are **globular proteins**. Very **specific** for one reaction and one substrate. Needed in small amounts.
- Named after the substrate (maltase hydrolyses maltose to glucose) or the reaction (oxidoreductases). Names end in **-ase**.
- Like other catalysts they **lower the activation energy**. NCERT: sucrose hydrolysis needs 6.22 kJ/mol with acid but only 2.15 kJ/mol with **sucrase**.

## 13. Vitamins

- Organic compounds needed in **small amounts** in the diet; lack of one causes a specific disease. Most can't be made by the body (plants make almost all; gut bacteria make some).
- **Fat soluble: A, D, E, K.** Stored in the liver and adipose tissue.
- **Water soluble: B group and C.** Excreted in urine, so they must be eaten regularly; they can't be stored, **except B₁₂**.
- Too much of a vitamin is also harmful.

Sources and deficiency diseases: see the vitamin table on the Key Facts sheet.

## 14. Nucleic acids

- Found in chromosomes (with proteins); carry heredity. Two types: **DNA** and **RNA**. They are polymers of nucleotides (**polynucleotides**).
- **Complete hydrolysis** gives a pentose sugar, phosphoric acid and nitrogen bases.

| | DNA | RNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sugar | β-D-2-deoxyribose | β-D-ribose |
| Bases | A, G, C, T | A, G, C, U |
| Strands | Double helix | Single strand |

- **Nucleoside** = base + sugar (base at C-1′). **Nucleotide** = nucleoside + phosphate (at C-5′). Sugar carbons get primes (1′, 2′…) to tell them from base atoms.
- Nucleotides join by **phosphodiester links** between C-5′ and C-3′ of the sugars. The base **sequence** is the primary structure.
- **DNA (Watson and Crick):** two strands wound round each other, held by H-bonds between bases. **A pairs with T; C pairs with G.** So the strands are **complementary**, not identical.
- **RNA:** a single-stranded helix that sometimes folds back on itself. Three types: **mRNA, rRNA, tRNA**.
- **Functions:** DNA is the chemical basis of heredity; it copies itself in cell division. DNA holds the message for proteins; the RNAs actually make them.
- **DNA fingerprinting:** a person's base sequence is unique and the same in every cell. Used in forensics, paternity tests and identifying dead bodies.

## 15. Hormones

Intercellular messengers made by **endocrine glands** and carried by the blood.

| Chemical type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Steroids | Estrogens, androgens |
| Polypeptides | Insulin, endorphins |
| Amino acid derivatives | Epinephrine, norepinephrine, thyroxine |

- **Insulin** lowers blood glucose (released when it rises fast); **glucagon** raises it. Together they control it.
- Epinephrine and norepinephrine handle responses to outside stimuli.
- **Thyroxine** is an iodinated derivative of **tyrosine**. Too little: hypothyroidism (lethargy, obesity); low iodine also enlarges the thyroid, prevented by **iodised salt** (sodium iodide added). Too much: hyperthyroidism.
- **Adrenal cortex:** glucocorticoids (carbohydrate metabolism, inflammation, stress) and mineralocorticoids (water and salt excretion). Failure: **Addison's disease** (low blood sugar, weakness, stress).
- **Gonads:** testosterone (male traits), estradiol (female traits, menstrual cycle), progesterone (readies the uterus for implantation).
