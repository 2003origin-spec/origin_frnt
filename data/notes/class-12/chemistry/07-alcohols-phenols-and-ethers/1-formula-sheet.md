Reactions are written as NCERT writes them: the main organic product, not always balanced. R = alkyl, Ar = aryl.

## Preparation of alcohols

:::formula{title="Acid-catalysed hydration of alkenes"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3CH=CH2 + H2O} \\ & \ce{<=>[H+] CH3-CH(OH)-CH3} \end{aligned}$$
Follows **Markovnikov's rule**: OH goes to the carbon with fewer H atoms. Goes through a carbocation (protonation, attack of water, loss of $\ce{H+}$).
:::

:::formula{title="Hydroboration–oxidation"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3-CH=CH2} \\ & \ce{->[(BH3)2] (CH3CH2CH2)3B} \\ & \ce{->[H2O2, OH-] CH3CH2CH2OH} \end{aligned}$$
Boron adds to the C=C carbon with **more H atoms**. The product looks like **anti-Markovnikov** addition of water (propene gives propan-1-ol). Excellent yield. By-product $\ce{B(OH)3}$.
:::

:::formula{title="Reduction of aldehydes and ketones"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{RCHO + H2} \\ & \ce{->[Pd] RCH2OH} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{RCOR'} \\ & \ce{->[NaBH4] RCH(OH)R'} \end{aligned}$$
Catalyst: finely divided Pt, Pd or Ni; or $\ce{NaBH4}$ / $\ce{LiAlH4}$. Aldehyde gives a **1°** alcohol, ketone a **2°** alcohol.
:::

:::formula{title="Reduction of acids and esters"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{RCOOH} \\ & \ce{->[(i) LiAlH4][(ii) H2O] RCH2OH} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{RCOOR'} \\ & \ce{->[H2][{catalyst}] RCH2OH + R'OH} \end{aligned}$$
$\ce{LiAlH4}$ is expensive, so it is used only for special chemicals. Industry first makes the ester (acid + R′OH, $\ce{H+}$) and then hydrogenates it. "(i), (ii)" means the second reagent is added after the first reaction is over.
:::

:::formula{title="Grignard reagent + carbonyl compound"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{HCHO + RMgX -> RCH2OMgX} \\ & \ce{->[H2O] RCH2OH + Mg(OH)X} \end{aligned}$$
Methanal gives a **1°** alcohol, any other aldehyde a **2°** alcohol, a ketone a **3°** alcohol. The R of the Grignard reagent joins the carbonyl carbon.
:::

## Preparation of phenols

:::formula{title="From chlorobenzene"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5Cl + NaOH} \\ & \ce{->[{623 K}][{300 atm}] C6H5ONa} \\ & \ce{->[HCl] C6H5OH} \end{aligned}$$
Fused NaOH, 623 K, high pressure (NCERT's text says 320 atm, its scheme 300 atm; either is fine). Acidify the sodium phenoxide to get phenol.
:::

:::formula{title="From benzenesulphonic acid"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H6} \\ & \ce{->[{oleum}] C6H5SO3H} \\ & \ce{->[(i) NaOH][(ii) H+] C6H5OH} \end{aligned}$$
Step 2 uses **molten** NaOH (gives sodium phenoxide), then acid.
:::

:::formula{title="From diazonium salts"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5NH2} \\ & \ce{->[NaNO2 + HCl][{273-278 K}] C6H5N2+Cl-} \\ & \ce{->[H2O][{warm}] C6H5OH + N2 + HCl} \end{aligned}$$
Diazotisation needs **273–278 K**. Warming with water (or dilute acid) hydrolyses the salt.
:::

:::formula{title="From cumene (industrial)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5CH(CH3)2} \\ & \ce{->[O2] C6H5C(CH3)2-O-O-H} \\ & \ce{->[H+][H2O] C6H5OH + CH3COCH3} \end{aligned}$$
Cumene = isopropylbenzene. Air oxidises it to cumene hydroperoxide; dilute acid splits that into phenol and **acetone** (a useful by-product). Most of the world's phenol is made this way.
:::

## O–H bond breaks (alcohols and phenols)

:::formula{title="With active metals"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2R-OH + 2Na} \\ & \ce{-> 2R-ONa + H2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{6(CH3)3C-OH + 2Al} \\ & \ce{-> 2[(CH3)3CO]3Al + 3H2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2C6H5OH + 2Na} \\ & \ce{-> 2C6H5ONa + H2} \end{aligned}$$
Na, K, Al. Alcohols give alkoxides, phenols give phenoxides. Product of the Al reaction: aluminium tert-butoxide.
:::

:::formula{title="Phenol with NaOH (alcohols do not react)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OH + NaOH} \\ & \ce{-> C6H5ONa + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Shows phenol is a stronger acid than water and alcohols.
:::

:::formula{title="Alkoxide with water"}
$$\ce{R-O- + H2O -> R-OH + OH-}$$
Water is a **stronger acid** than an alcohol, so an alkoxide is a **stronger base** than hydroxide (sodium ethoxide is a stronger base than NaOH).
:::

:::formula{title="Esterification"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{ROH + R'COOH} \\ & \ce{<=>[H+] ROCOR' + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{ROH + (R'CO)2O} \\ & \ce{<=>[H+] ROCOR' + R'COOH} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{ROH + R'COCl} \\ & \ce{->[{pyridine}] ROCOR' + HCl} \end{aligned}$$
Acid and anhydride: a little conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$; reversible, so water is removed as it forms. Acid chloride: **pyridine** (a base) neutralises HCl and pushes the reaction forward. Works for phenols (ArOH) too.
:::

:::formula{title="Aspirin (acetylation of salicylic acid)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H4(OH)COOH + (CH3CO)2O} \\ & \ce{->[H+] C6H4(OCOCH3)COOH} \\ & \ce{+ CH3COOH} \end{aligned}$$
Acetylation = putting a $\ce{CH3CO}$ group on the O. Aspirin is acetylsalicylic acid.
:::

## C–O bond breaks (alcohols only)

:::formula{title="With hydrogen halides"}
$$\ce{ROH + HX -> R-X + H2O}$$
Reactivity 3° > 2° > 1°. **Lucas test** (conc. HCl + $\ce{ZnCl2}$): 3° turbid at once, 1° no turbidity at room temperature. Phenols do not do this.
:::

:::formula{title="With phosphorus tribromide and thionyl chloride (Unit 6)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{3ROH + PBr3} \\ & \ce{-> 3RBr + H3PO3} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{ROH + SOCl2} \\ & \ce{-> RCl + SO2 + HCl} \end{aligned}$$
:::

:::formula{title="Dehydration to alkenes"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C2H5OH} \\ & \ce{->[{conc.} H2SO4][{443 K}] CH2=CH2 + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3} \\ & \ce{->[85\% H3PO4][{440 K}] CH3CH=CH2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{(CH3)3C-OH} \\ & \ce{->[20\% H3PO4][{358 K}] (CH3)2C=CH2} \end{aligned}$$
Ease: **3° > 2° > 1°** (milder conditions). Also with anhydrous $\ce{ZnCl2}$ or alumina. Slow step: forming the carbocation.
:::

:::formula{title="Oxidation of 1° alcohols"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{RCH2OH} \\ & \ce{->[CrO3] RCHO} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3CH=CHCH2OH} \\ & \ce{->[PCC] CH3CH=CHCHO} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{RCH2OH} \\ & \ce{->[KMnO4][H+] RCOOH} \end{aligned}$$
$\ce{CrO3}$ in an **anhydrous** medium or **PCC** (pyridinium chlorochromate: $\ce{CrO3}$ + pyridine + HCl) stops at the aldehyde; PCC gives a better yield and leaves C=C alone. Acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ goes straight to the acid.
:::

:::formula{title="Oxidation of 2° alcohols"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{RCH(OH)R'} \\ & \ce{->[CrO3] RCOR'} \end{aligned}$$
2° gives a ketone. 3° alcohols are not oxidised; with strong oxidisers ($\ce{KMnO4}$) and heat, C–C bonds break into a mixture of smaller acids.
:::

:::formula{title="Heated copper at 573 K"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{RCH2OH} \\ & \ce{->[Cu][{573 K}] RCHO} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{RCH(OH)R'} \\ & \ce{->[Cu][{573 K}] RCOR'} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{(CH3)3C-OH} \\ & \ce{->[Cu][{573 K}] (CH3)2C=CH2} \end{aligned}$$
1° and 2° are **dehydrogenated** (lose $\ce{H2}$); 3° is **dehydrated** (loses $\ce{H2O}$). Distinguishes the three classes.
:::

## Reactions of phenol

:::formula{title="Nitration"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OH} \\ & \ce{->[{dil.} HNO3][{298 K}] O2N-C6H4-OH} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OH} \\ & \ce{->[{conc.} HNO3] C6H2(NO2)3OH} \end{aligned}$$
Dilute: **o- + p-nitrophenol**, separated by steam distillation. Concentrated: **2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid)**, poor yield. Better route: conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ first (phenol-2,4-disulphonic acid), then conc. $\ce{HNO3}$.
:::

:::formula{title="Bromination in a non-polar solvent"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OH} \\ & \ce{->[Br2/CS2][{273 K}] Br-C6H4-OH} \end{aligned}$$
Solvent of low polarity ($\ce{CS2}$ or $\ce{CHCl3}$), low temperature: **p-bromophenol** (major) + o-bromophenol (minor). No Lewis acid needed.
:::

:::formula{title="Bromine water"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OH + 3Br2} \\ & \ce{-> C6H2Br3OH v + 3HBr} \end{aligned}$$
White precipitate of **2,4,6-tribromophenol**.
:::

:::formula{title="Sulphonation"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OH} \\ & \ce{->[{conc.} H2SO4] HO-C6H4-SO3H} \end{aligned}$$
This one-group reaction is exam extra (JEE Main and NEET syllabi, not in NCERT): near room temperature the **ortho** acid is the main product; at about 373 K the **para** acid is. NCERT only shows conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ giving phenol-2,4-disulphonic acid (picric acid route).
:::

:::formula{title="Kolbe's reaction"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OH} \\ & \ce{->[NaOH] C6H5ONa} \\ & \ce{->[(i) CO2][(ii) H+] C6H4(OH)COOH} \end{aligned}$$
Phenoxide is reactive enough to attack the weak electrophile $\ce{CO2}$. Main product: **2-hydroxybenzoic acid (salicylic acid)**, the ortho acid.
:::

:::formula{title="Reimer–Tiemann reaction"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OH} \\ & \ce{->[CHCl3][{aq} NaOH] C6H4(ONa)CHCl2} \\ & \ce{->[NaOH] C6H4(ONa)CHO} \\ & \ce{->[H+] C6H4(OH)CHO} \end{aligned}$$
Puts –CHO at the **ortho** position. The intermediate is a substituted benzal chloride. Product: **salicylaldehyde** (2-hydroxybenzaldehyde).
:::

:::formula{title="Zinc dust"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OH + Zn} \\ & \ce{->[\Delta] C6H6 + ZnO} \end{aligned}$$
The only C–O cleavage that phenol shows.
:::

:::formula{title="Oxidation of phenol"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OH} \\ & \ce{->[Na2Cr2O7][H2SO4] C6H4O2} \end{aligned}$$
Chromic acid gives **benzoquinone**, a conjugated diketone. In air, phenols slowly turn into dark mixtures containing quinones.
:::

## Commercial alcohols

:::formula{title="Methanol from carbon monoxide"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CO + 2H2} \\ & \ce{->[ZnO-Cr2O3][{573-673 K}] CH3OH} \end{aligned}$$
Pressure **200–300 atm**. Methanol ("wood spirit") boils at 337 K and is highly poisonous.
:::

:::formula{title="Ethanol by fermentation"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C12H22O11 + H2O} \\ & \ce{->[{invertase}] C6H12O6 + C6H12O6} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H12O6} \\ & \ce{->[{zymase}] 2C2H5OH + 2CO2} \end{aligned}$$
Sucrose gives glucose + fructose. Zymase (from yeast) works without air and stops above about **14%** alcohol. Ethanol boils at 351 K.
:::

## Preparation of ethers

:::formula{title="Dehydration of alcohols"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2C2H5OH} \\ & \ce{->[H2SO4][{413 K}] C2H5OC2H5 + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
At **413 K** ether (an $\mathrm{S_N2}$ reaction); at **443 K** ethene. Good only for **1°, unhindered** alkyl groups; 2° and 3° alcohols give alkenes.
:::

:::formula{title="Williamson synthesis"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{R-X + R'-ONa} \\ & \ce{-> R-O-R' + NaX} \end{aligned}$$
$\mathrm{S_N2}$ attack of the alkoxide on the alkyl halide. Use a **methyl or 1° alkyl halide**. The right and wrong pair:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{(CH3)3C-ONa + CH3CH2Cl} \\ & \ce{-> (CH3)3C-OC2H5 + NaCl} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{(CH3)3C-Br + CH3ONa} \\ & \ce{-> (CH3)2C=CH2 + NaBr} \\ & \ce{+ CH3OH} \end{aligned}$$
Put the bulky group on the **alkoxide**, never on the halide. The alkoxide is also a strong base: with a 2° halide elimination competes, and a 3° halide gives **only** the alkene (here 2-methylpropene).
:::

:::formula{title="Aryl alkyl ethers"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5ONa + R-X} \\ & \ce{-> C6H5-O-R + NaX} \end{aligned}$$
Phenol (as phenoxide) supplies the O; the alkyl halide supplies R. An aryl halide cannot be the halide here: it does not undergo $\mathrm{S_N2}$.
:::

## Reactions of ethers

:::formula{title="Cleavage by HX"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{R-O-R + HX} \\ & \ce{-> R-X + R-OH} \end{aligned}$$
Needs **conc. HI or HBr** and high temperature. Reactivity HI > HBr > HCl. With excess HX the alcohol also turns into a halide (ROH + HX, above), so a dialkyl ether gives two alkyl halide molecules.
:::

:::formula{title="Mixed ethers with HI"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3-O-CH2CH3 + HI} \\ & \ce{-> CH3I + CH3CH2OH} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{(CH3)3C-O-CH3 + HI} \\ & \ce{-> (CH3)3C-I + CH3OH} \end{aligned}$$
1° and 2° groups: $\ce{I-}$ attacks the **smaller** group ($\mathrm{S_N2}$). One 3° group: the **3° halide** forms ($\mathrm{S_N1}$, via the stable 3° carbocation).
:::

:::formula{title="Anisole with HI"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5-O-CH3 + HI} \\ & \ce{-> C6H5OH + CH3I} \end{aligned}$$
The O–C₆H₅ bond is stronger (sp² carbon, partial double bond), so the **O–CH₃** bond breaks. Phenol does **not** go on to give iodobenzene.
:::

:::formula{title="Electrophilic substitution of anisole"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OCH3} \\ & \ce{->[Br2][CH3COOH] Br-C6H4-OCH3} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OCH3 + CH3Cl} \\ & \ce{->[{anhyd.} AlCl3][CS2] CH3-C6H4-OCH3} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OCH3 + CH3COCl} \\ & \ce{->[{anhyd.} AlCl3]} \\ & \ce{CH3CO-C6H4-OCH3} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{C6H5OCH3} \\ & \ce{->[{conc.} HNO3][{conc.} H2SO4] O2N-C6H4-OCH3} \end{aligned}$$
–OCH₃ activates the ring and directs **ortho/para**; **para is major** each time. Bromination needs no $\ce{FeBr3}$ (para about 90%). Products: 4-methoxytoluene, 4-methoxyacetophenone, 4-nitroanisole.
:::
