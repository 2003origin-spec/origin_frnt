## 1. What they are

- **Alcohol:** –OH on a carbon of an **aliphatic** system ($\ce{CH3OH}$).
- **Phenol:** –OH directly on a carbon of an **aromatic ring** ($\ce{C6H5OH}$).
- **Ether:** R–O–R′, R–O–Ar or Ar–O–Ar. You can see it as the H of an –OH replaced by an alkyl or aryl group ($\ce{CH3OCH3}$).

## 2. Classification

- **By number of –OH groups:** mono-, di-, tri- or polyhydric (ethane-1,2-diol is dihydric, glycerol trihydric).
- **–OH on an sp³ carbon:**
  - **1°, 2°, 3°** alcohols: the –OH carbon carries 1, 2 or 3 other carbons;
  - **allylic:** the –OH carbon is next to a C=C;
  - **benzylic:** the –OH carbon is next to an aromatic ring. Allylic and benzylic alcohols can be 1°, 2° or 3°.
- **–OH on an sp² carbon:** vinylic alcohols ($\ce{CH2=CH-OH}$) and phenols (aryl carbon).
- **Ethers:** **simple (symmetrical)** if both groups are the same ($\ce{C2H5OC2H5}$); **mixed (unsymmetrical)** if they differ ($\ce{C2H5OCH3}$, $\ce{C2H5OC6H5}$).

## 3. Naming

- **Alcohols:** drop the "e" of the alkane, add "ol". Number the longest chain from the end **nearer the –OH**. Polyhydric alcohols keep the "e": ethane-1,2-diol, propane-1,2,3-triol. In a ring the –OH carbon is C-1 (2-methylcyclopentanol).
- **Phenols:** "phenol" is also the IUPAC name. Cresols are methylphenols.
- **Ethers:** common name = the two groups in alphabetical order + "ether" (ethyl methyl ether). IUPAC: alkoxyalkane; the **larger** group is the parent.

## 4. Structure of the functional groups

| Compound | C–O bond | Angle at O |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Methanol | 142 pm | C–O–H 108.9° |
| Phenol | 136 pm | C–O–H 109° |
| Methoxymethane | 141 pm | C–O–C 111.7° |

- **Alcohols:** sp³ O. The angle is a little **less** than tetrahedral (109°28′) because the two lone pairs on O repel the bonds.
- **Phenol:** the C–O bond is **shorter** because (i) the lone pair of O is conjugated with the ring, giving partial double bond character, and (ii) the carbon is sp².
- **Ethers:** the angle is a little **more** than tetrahedral because the two bulky R groups repel each other.

## 5. Making alcohols

- **Hydration of alkenes** (acid catalyst): Markovnikov. Mechanism: (1) $\ce{H3O+}$ protonates the alkene to a carbocation; (2) water attacks; (3) loss of $\ce{H+}$.
- **Hydroboration–oxidation:** $\ce{(BH3)2}$, then $\ce{H2O2}$/$\ce{OH-}$. Looks anti-Markovnikov. Excellent yield.
- **Reduction:** aldehyde → 1°, ketone → 2° ($\ce{H2}$/Pt, Pd, Ni; $\ce{NaBH4}$; $\ce{LiAlH4}$). Acids → 1° with $\ce{LiAlH4}$, or (industry) via the ester and $\ce{H2}$/catalyst.
- **Grignard reagent:** methanal → 1°, other aldehydes → 2°, ketones → 3°.

## 6. Making phenols

1. Chlorobenzene + NaOH at 623 K and high pressure, then acid.
2. Benzene + oleum → benzenesulphonic acid; fuse with NaOH, then acid.
3. Aniline → diazonium salt ($\ce{NaNO2}$ + HCl, 273–278 K) → warm with water.
4. **Cumene** + air → cumene hydroperoxide → dilute acid → phenol + acetone. Most phenol is made this way.

## 7. Physical properties

- **Boiling point** of alcohols and phenols rises with the number of carbons (more van der Waals force) and **falls with branching** (smaller surface area).
- They boil **much higher** than hydrocarbons, ethers and haloalkanes of similar mass, because of **intermolecular hydrogen bonding**. Ethanol ≫ methoxymethane > propane.
- **Solubility** in water comes from hydrogen bonds with water. It falls as the alkyl or aryl (water-hating) part gets bigger. Lower alcohols mix with water in all proportions.
- **Ethers:** polar C–O bonds and a net dipole moment, but **no O–H**, so no hydrogen bonds between ether molecules. Their boiling points are close to alkanes of similar mass. Ether O **can** accept a hydrogen bond from water, so their solubility is like that of alcohols of the same mass.

## 8. Acidity

- Alcohols and phenols are **Brønsted acids**: they react with Na, K and Al to give $\ce{H2}$.
- **Alcohols:** acidic because O–H is polar. An electron-releasing alkyl group makes it less polar, so acid strength is **1° > 2° > 3°**.
- Alcohols are **weaker acids than water**, so an alkoxide is a **stronger base** than $\ce{OH-}$. Alcohols are also Brønsted **bases** (lone pairs on O).
- **Phenol is more acidic than alcohols and water:** it dissolves in aqueous NaOH, alcohols do not.
  - the –OH carbon is sp² and more electronegative, which makes O–H more polar;
  - the **phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilised**: its negative charge is spread over the ring. An alkoxide's charge stays on O.
- **Substituents:** electron-withdrawing groups ($\ce{-NO2}$) **raise** acidity, most at **ortho and para**. Electron-releasing groups (alkyl) **lower** it: cresols are weaker than phenol.

| Compound | pKa |
| --- | --- |

Larger pKa means a weaker acid. NCERT: phenol is about a **million times** more acidic than ethanol.

## 9. Reactions of alcohols

- **Esterification** with acids, anhydrides (conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$, remove water) or acid chlorides (pyridine). Acetylation of salicylic acid gives **aspirin** (analgesic, anti-inflammatory, antipyretic).
- **With HX** → alkyl halide. **Lucas test** (conc. HCl + anhydrous $\ce{ZnCl2}$): 3° turbid at once; 1° no turbidity at room temperature.
- **Dehydration** → alkene: conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ or $\ce{H3PO4}$, anhydrous $\ce{ZnCl2}$ or alumina. Ease 3° > 2° > 1°.
- **Mechanism (ethanol, 443 K):** (1) fast protonation to the ethyl oxonium ion; (2) loss of water to a **carbocation**: slow, **rate-determining**; (3) loss of $\ce{H+}$ gives ethene. The acid is given back, and ethene is removed to drive the equilibrium.
- **Oxidation** (O–H and C–H break, C=O forms; also called dehydrogenation):
  - 1° → aldehyde ($\ce{CrO3}$ anhydrous, PCC) → acid (acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$);
  - 2° → ketone ($\ce{CrO3}$);
  - 3° resists; harsh conditions break C–C bonds;
  - over Cu at 573 K: 1° → aldehyde, 2° → ketone, 3° → alkene.

:::remember
**Methanol poisoning:** the body oxidises methanol to methanal and then methanoic acid, which can cause blindness and death. Treatment: intravenous **dilute ethanol**. It keeps the enzyme busy, so the kidneys have time to remove the methanol.
:::

## 10. Reactions of phenol

- –OH **activates** the ring and directs to **ortho and para** (resonance puts extra electron density there).
- **Nitration:** dilute $\ce{HNO3}$ at 298 K gives o- + p-nitrophenol. Concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ gives picric acid (poor yield; better via the 2,4-disulphonic acid).
- **Bromination:** $\ce{Br2}$ in $\ce{CS2}$ or $\ce{CHCl3}$ at 273 K gives mainly p-bromophenol. **Bromine water** gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol. No Lewis acid is needed: –OH is so activating that it polarises $\ce{Br2}$ by itself.
- **Kolbe's reaction:** sodium phenoxide + $\ce{CO2}$, then acid → **salicylic acid**.
- **Reimer–Tiemann reaction:** $\ce{CHCl3}$ + aqueous NaOH, then acid → **salicylaldehyde**.
- **Zinc dust** → benzene. **Chromic acid** → benzoquinone.

## 11. Methanol and ethanol

| | Methanol | Ethanol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Made from | CO + $\ce{H2}$ ($\ce{ZnO-Cr2O3}$) | Fermentation of sugars; hydration of ethene |
| Boiling point | 337 K | 351 K |
| Notes | "Wood spirit"; highly poisonous | Solvent; made unfit to drink |

- **Fermentation:** invertase turns sucrose into glucose + fructose; zymase (in yeast) turns them into ethanol + $\ce{CO2}$. It needs **no air** (anaerobic). Zymase stops working above about 14% alcohol. If air gets in, ethanol is oxidised to ethanoic acid and the drink turns sour.
- **Denatured alcohol:** commercial alcohol made unfit for drinking with **copper sulphate** (colour) and **pyridine** (foul smell).

## 12. Ethers: preparation

- **Acidic dehydration** of 1° alcohols at **413 K** (ethanol gives ethoxyethane; at 443 K ethene). The alcohol attacks a protonated alcohol ($\mathrm{S_N2}$). Fails for 2° and 3° alcohols: elimination wins and alkenes form. Not suitable for mixed ethers such as ethyl methyl ether (a mixture of three ethers forms).
- **Williamson synthesis:** alkyl halide + sodium alkoxide, $\mathrm{S_N2}$. Gives simple and mixed ethers. The halide should be **methyl or 1°**; with a 2° halide elimination competes, and a 3° halide gives only alkene, because alkoxides are strong bases as well as nucleophiles. Aryl alkyl ethers: phenoxide + alkyl halide.

## 13. Ethers: reactions

- Ethers are the **least reactive** of these functional groups.
- **C–O cleavage** needs **concentrated HI or HBr** at high temperature (HI > HBr > HCl). Mechanism: (1) protonation of the ether; (2) $\ce{I-}$ attacks the **less substituted** carbon ($\mathrm{S_N2}$), releasing an alcohol; (3) with excess HI the alcohol becomes an alkyl iodide.
- If one group is **3°**, the 3° halide forms instead ($\mathrm{S_N1}$ via the 3° carbocation).
- **Anisole + HI** → phenol + $\ce{CH3I}$. The O–aryl bond is stronger (sp² carbon, partial double bond), and phenol cannot be turned into an aryl halide this way.
- **Ring substitution:** –OR activates the ring and directs **ortho/para**, like –OH. Anisole: bromination in ethanoic acid (no $\ce{FeBr3}$, para about 90%), Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation (anhydrous $\ce{AlCl3}$), nitration (conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ + $\ce{HNO3}$). Para is the main product each time.
