No NCERT chapter — these notes follow the JEE Main and NEET experimental-skills lists. JEE Main 2026 and NEET UG 2026 (Unit 20 in both) list the **same 18 experiments in the same order**, so every experiment below is in both exams. "E1" to "E18" are their numbers in that list. The method follows the NCERT Class 11 and 12 lab manuals.

The exams ask about the **approach and observations**: what is measured, with which instrument, what the graph looks like, and what the result means.

## Measuring instruments

### E1. Vernier calipers

- **Aim:** internal and external diameter, and depth, of a vessel (a beaker or a hollow cylinder).
- **Parts:** the big lower jaws grip the **outside** (external diameter); the small upper jaws go **inside** (internal diameter); the thin strip at the back measures **depth**.
- **Readings:** find the least count and the zero error first. Then reading = MSR + (coinciding vernier mark × LC). Take readings at several places and in two perpendicular directions, then average.
- **Result:** corrected reading = observed − zero error.

### E2. Screw gauge

- **Aim:** diameter of a thin wire or thickness of a thin sheet.
- **Setup:** the object sits between the stud and the spindle; turn the **ratchet** until it clicks, so the grip is never too tight.
- **Readings:** pitch, then LC = pitch ÷ circular divisions, then zero error. Reading = linear scale reading + (circular division on the reference line × LC). For a wire, measure at several points along it and in two perpendicular directions.
- **Result:** mean corrected diameter. Its LC (0.01 mm) is ten times finer than a common vernier (0.1 mm), so thin wires need a screw gauge.

## Mechanics and properties of matter

### E3. Simple pendulum: dissipation of energy

- **Aim:** see how a pendulum loses energy, by plotting the **square of the amplitude** against **time**.
- **Idea:** energy $E \propto A^2$, so $A^2$ tracks the energy left.
- **Readings:** set it swinging with a small amplitude. Read the amplitude on a scale under the bob at regular times (for example every minute).
- **Graph:** $A^2$ against $t$ is a **curve that falls**, steeply at first and then more slowly. It is not a straight line.
- **Result:** energy is lost to air drag and friction at the support. A lighter bob of the same size (or a bob with a card attached) loses energy faster.

### E4. Metre scale: mass by principle of moments

- **Setup:** balance the metre scale on a knife edge and note the balance point (its centre of gravity, near 50 cm). Hang the unknown mass $m$ on one side and a known mass $M$ on the other. Slide them until the scale is horizontal again.
- **Readings:** distances $d_1$ and $d_2$ from the knife edge. Repeat for different positions.
- **Result:** $m = M d_2/d_1$. A graph of $d_1$ against $d_2$ is a straight line through the origin with slope $M/m$.

### E5. Young's modulus (Searle's apparatus)

- **Setup:** two identical long wires hang from the same rigid support. One (the reference wire) carries a fixed weight; the other (the experimental wire) gets the load. A spirit level and a micrometer screw link the two.
- **Why two wires:** a change in temperature or a sag of the support affects both wires equally, so it cancels out.
- **Readings:** $L$ with a metre scale, diameter with a screw gauge (several places), extension $l$ with the micrometer for each load. Take readings while **loading and unloading**, and average them.
- **Graph:** extension against load is a **straight line through the origin** (within the elastic limit).
- **Result:** $Y = \dfrac{MgL}{\pi r^2 l}$.

### E6. Surface tension by capillary rise

- **Setup:** a clean, narrow glass capillary stands vertically in water. Water rises because it wets glass (contact angle about 0).
- **Readings (travelling microscope):** the inner diameter of the bore (two perpendicular directions); the level of the bottom of the meniscus; the level of the water surface outside (a needle tip just touching it). $h$ = difference.
- **Result:** $S = \tfrac12 r h\rho g$. For water at room temperature, about 0.07 N/m.
- **Effect of detergent:** add detergent and the rise **falls**, because detergent **lowers** the surface tension.
- $h \propto 1/r$: a narrower tube gives a higher rise.

### E7. Viscosity by terminal velocity

- **Setup:** a tall, wide glass jar of a viscous liquid (glycerine or castor oil) with two marks, the upper one well below the surface. Small steel balls of known radius.
- **Readings:** time $t$ for each ball to fall the distance $d$ between the marks; $v_t = d/t$. Use balls of different radii.
- **Graph:** $v_t$ against $r^2$ is a straight line through the origin.
- **Result:** $\eta = \dfrac{2r^2(\rho - \sigma)g}{9v_t}$.
- The ball must already be at terminal velocity at the upper mark, so that mark is placed lower down.

## Heat and sound

### E8. Speed of sound: resonance tube

- **Setup:** a vertical tube whose water level can be changed; a tuning fork of known $\nu$ held just above the open end. Air column + water = pipe closed at one end.
- **Readings:** start with a short air column and lengthen it slowly (lower the water level); the sound is loudest at the first resonance $l_1$, then again at $l_2$. Approach each point from both sides and average.
- **Result:** $v = 2\nu(l_2 - l_1)$; the end correction cancels. About 340 m/s at room temperature. The speed rises with temperature, so note the room temperature.

### E9. Specific heat by method of mixtures

- **Solid:** heat the solid (in boiling water or steam) to $T_1$. Drop it quickly into water at $T_2$ in a calorimeter. Stir and note the **highest steady** temperature $T$.
- **Liquid:** heat a solid of known specific heat and drop it into the liquid in the calorimeter.
- **Masses:** weigh the empty calorimeter with stirrer, then with water (or liquid).
- **Result:** heat lost by the hot body = heat gained by the water (or liquid) and the calorimeter.

## Electricity

### E10. Resistivity with a metre bridge

- **Setup:** a 1 m uniform wire with two gaps. Unknown wire $X$ in one gap, resistance box $R$ in the other, galvanometer and jockey between the middle terminal and the wire.
- **Readings:** touch the jockey along the wire to find the null point (no deflection) at length $l$. Choose $R$ so that $l$ is in the middle part of the wire. **Interchange** $X$ and $R$ and repeat; average.
- **Result:** $X = \dfrac{R\,l}{100 - l}$. Then $\rho = \dfrac{X\pi r^2}{L}$ with the wire's length $L$ and radius $r$ (screw gauge).

### E11. Resistance by Ohm's law

- **Setup:** cell, key, rheostat, ammeter and wire in **series**; voltmeter **in parallel** with the wire.
- **Readings:** change the current with the rheostat; note $V$ and $I$ for 5 or 6 settings.
- **Graph:** $V$ against $I$ is a **straight line through the origin**.
- **Result:** $R$ = slope; resistance per cm = $R/L$.

### E12. Galvanometer: resistance and figure of merit

- **Setup:** cell $E$, a high resistance box $R$ and the galvanometer in series (key $K_1$). A shunt resistance box $S$ is connected across the galvanometer (key $K_2$).
- **Readings:** with $K_1$ closed, adjust $R$ for a deflection $\theta$ (an even number of divisions). Close $K_2$ and adjust $S$ until the deflection is $\theta/2$.
- **Result:** $G = \dfrac{RS}{R - S}$; figure of merit $k = \dfrac{E}{(R + G)\theta}$ (current for one division, in A/div). Repeat for several $R$ and average.

## Optics

### E13. Focal length by the parallax method

- **No parallax:** two objects are at the same place when they don't shift against each other as you move your eye sideways. A needle (image needle) is moved until there is **no parallax** between its tip and the image's tip.
- **Concave mirror:** object needle in front of the mirror beyond $F$; locate the real, inverted image with the image needle. Measure $u$ and $v$ and use the mirror formula. Graph: $|u|$ against $|v|$ is a hyperbola-like curve; it meets the line $|u| = |v|$ at $(2f, 2f)$.
- **Convex lens:** the same idea with a real image on the other side of the lens.
- **Convex mirror:** its image is virtual, so a convex lens is used. First the lens forms a real image $I$ of the object needle. The convex mirror is put between the lens and $I$ and moved until the image formed after reflection coincides with the object needle (no parallax). Then the rays hit the mirror normally, so $I$ is at its centre of curvature. Remove the mirror and locate $I$: $R$ = distance from the mirror's position to $I$, and $f = R/2$.
- **Index correction:** compare a rod's true length with the length read on the optical bench, and correct each distance.

### E14. Prism: angle of deviation against angle of incidence

- **Setup:** prism on a sheet of paper; pins mark the incident ray and the emergent ray for several angles of incidence (about 30° to 60°).
- **Readings:** for each $i$, measure $\delta$ between the incident ray (produced forward) and the emergent ray.
- **Graph:** a **U-shaped curve** with one lowest point, the angle of minimum deviation $\delta_m$. It is not symmetric.
- **Result:** read $\delta_m$; then $n = \dfrac{\sin\frac{A + \delta_m}{2}}{\sin\frac{A}{2}}$.

### E15. Refractive index of a glass slab (travelling microscope)

- **Readings:** focus on a mark on paper ($R_1$). Put the slab on the mark and focus on the mark's image ($R_2$). Sprinkle a little powder (lycopodium or chalk) on the top and focus on it ($R_3$).
- **Result:** real thickness $= R_3 - R_1$, apparent thickness $= R_3 - R_2$, $n = \dfrac{R_3 - R_1}{R_3 - R_2}$.

## Semiconductor devices

### E16. p-n junction diode characteristics

- **Forward bias** (p to +): milliammeter. Current is tiny until the threshold (knee) voltage, about **0.7 V for Si** and **0.2 V for Ge**, then rises sharply.
- **Reverse bias:** microammeter. A very small, almost constant current (μA), until breakdown.
- **Graph:** forward part in mA, reverse part in μA, so the two halves use **different scales**.
- **Result:** threshold voltage from the graph; dynamic resistance $\Delta V/\Delta I$.

### E17. Zener diode characteristics

- **Setup:** Zener in **reverse** bias, with a series resistance to limit the current; voltmeter across it, milliammeter in series.
- **Readings:** raise the reverse voltage slowly. The current stays almost zero, then at the breakdown voltage $V_Z$ it rises sharply while the voltage stays nearly constant.
- **Result:** $V_Z$ is the voltage at which the reverse current suddenly increases. This constant voltage is why a Zener is used as a voltage regulator.

### E18. Identifying diode, LED, resistor and capacitor

Use a multimeter (resistance range) across the two legs, then swap the leads.

| Item | Two-way test | Clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resistor | Same resistance both ways | Steady reading |
| Diode | Low one way, high the other | No light |
| LED | Low one way, high the other | Glows when forward biased |
| Capacitor | No steady current | Needle kicks, then falls back |

A component with three or more legs (a transistor or an IC) is none of these four.
