## Composition and size

- $Z$ = protons, $N$ = neutrons, $A = Z + N$. In ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$ the **top** number is $A$, the bottom is $Z$.
- **Isotopes** share $Z$, **isobars** share $A$, **isotones** share $N$.
- There are **no electrons inside the nucleus**. The electron given out in $\beta^-$ decay is created at the moment of decay.
- A free neutron decays (mean life about 1000 s); a free proton is stable.

:::tip
**Isot-o-pe** has a **p**: same **p**rotons. **Isot-o-ne** has an **n**: same **n**eutrons. **Isob-a-r**: same **A**.
:::

:::trap
$R = R_0 A^{1/3}$ uses the **mass number** $A$, not $Z$. For ratios, $R_1/R_2 = (A_1/A_2)^{1/3}$: doubling $A$ makes $R$ only $2^{1/3} \approx 1.26$ times larger.
:::

:::trap
Nuclear density does **not** depend on $A$. Uranium and helium nuclei have the same density. The **atom's** density does not follow this rule.
:::

- Nuclear volume $\propto A$ and surface area $\propto A^{2/3}$.
- Electron scattering senses the nucleus's **charge**; $\alpha$-scattering senses the nuclear **matter**. So the two give slightly different radii.

## Mass defect and binding energy

:::trap
Do not mix nuclear and atomic masses. With **atomic** masses use $m_H = 1.007825$ u (hydrogen atom) for each proton; the electrons then cancel. With **nuclear** masses use $m_p = 1.00727$ u.
:::

:::trap
Higher **total** binding energy does not mean more stable. Stability is judged by binding energy **per nucleon**. Uranium has a much larger $E_b$ than iron but a lower $E_{bn}$.
:::

- Binding energy is energy that must be **supplied** to break a nucleus apart. It is not energy stored inside and waiting to come out.
- A bound nucleus is always **lighter** than its free nucleons: $\Delta M > 0$.
- NCERT writes the O-16 mass defect as 127.5 MeV/$c^2$; the binding energy is 127.5 MeV.

:::remember
- $1\ \text{u} = 931.5$ MeV/$c^2$.
- $E_{bn} \approx 8$ MeV for $30 < A < 170$.
- Peak about **8.75 MeV at $A = 56$** (iron); **7.6 MeV at $A = 238$**.
- Peaks at ${}^{4}$He and ${}^{16}$O hint at a shell structure.
:::

:::tip
On the $E_{bn}$ curve, **any move towards iron releases energy**: heavy nuclei split (fission), light nuclei join (fusion). Moving away from iron needs energy.
:::

## Nuclear force

- Strongest of the forces here, but **short range** (a few fm). Beyond that it is zero.
- **Attractive** beyond about 0.8 fm, **strongly repulsive** closer than that. The repulsive core stops the nucleus collapsing.
- **Charge independent:** n–n, n–p and p–p forces are about equal.
- **Saturation:** each nucleon interacts only with its nearest neighbours. That is why $E_{bn}$ is nearly constant.

:::trap
The nuclear force does **not** follow an inverse-square law and has no simple formula. Do not write $F \propto 1/r^2$ for it.
:::

## Radioactivity

| Decay | Change in $A$, $Z$ |
| --- | --- |
| $\alpha$ | $A - 4$, $Z - 2$ |
| $\beta^-$ | $A$ same, $Z + 1$ |
| $\beta^+$ | $A$ same, $Z - 1$ |
| $\gamma$ | no change |

- One $\alpha$ followed by two $\beta^-$ gives back the **same element** (an isotope of the parent, 4 lighter).
- $\gamma$-decay only lets an excited nucleus drop to a lower energy level; it is not a new element.
- $\alpha$-rays are helium nuclei, $\beta$-rays are electrons (or positrons), $\gamma$-rays are electromagnetic waves shorter than X-rays.
- Stable light nuclei have $N/Z \approx 1$; heavy ones about **3 : 2**.

:::trap
Half-life, mean life and $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ are **no longer in the NCERT chapter**, and the JEE Main 2026 and NEET 2026 syllabi do not list radioactivity at all. Older question banks still have them; do not spend revision time there unless your exam asks.
:::

## Fission and fusion

- Fission of ${}^{235}$U: about **200 MeV** per nucleus, mostly as kinetic energy of the fragments.
- Fission fragments are **neutron-rich** and radioactive; they $\beta$-decay.
- The fusion barrier for two protons is about **400 keV**. Average-energy estimate: $T \sim 3 \times 10^9$ K.
- The sun's core is only $1.5 \times 10^7$ K; fusion happens through the fastest protons.
- p-p cycle net: **4 hydrogen atoms → 1 helium atom + 26.7 MeV**.

:::trap
Fusion needs high temperature to beat the **Coulomb repulsion**, not because the nuclear force is weak. Once the nuclei are close enough, the nuclear force wins.
:::

:::trap
Per **reaction**, fission gives more energy (about 200 MeV) than any fusion process here (0.42 MeV per step up to 26.7 MeV for the whole p-p cycle). Per **nucleon** it can be the other way round: the p-p cycle gives $26.7/4 \approx 6.7$ MeV per nucleon, fission only $200/236 \approx 0.85$ MeV. Read which one the question asks.
:::

:::trap
Q value: $Q = (\text{initial mass} - \text{final mass})c^2$. Initial minus final. $Q > 0$ means energy is **released**. A negative $Q$ means the reaction needs energy to happen.
:::

## NCERT lines to know

:::remember
- More than 99.9% of an atom's mass is in its nucleus.
- The number of protons and the number of neutrons are separately conserved in a nuclear reaction.
- Nuclear sources give about a **million times** more energy than chemical sources for the same mass.
- Chemical reactions also convert mass into energy, but their mass defects are about a million times smaller.
- Thermonuclear fusion is the energy source of the sun and all stars.
- Electron and positron are a particle–antiparticle pair: same mass, opposite charge.
:::
