## 1. The nucleus in numbers

- The nucleus is about $10^4$ times smaller in radius than the atom, so its volume is about $10^{-12}$ of the atom's. An atom is almost empty.
- Yet the nucleus holds **more than 99.9%** of the atom's mass.
- Atomic masses are measured in **u** (1/12 of the mass of a ${}^{12}$C atom) with a **mass spectrometer**.

## 2. Isotopes and average atomic mass

Most elements are a mix of **isotopes**: same element, same chemistry, different mass. The atomic mass of an element is the weighted average over its isotopes.

:::example{title="Chlorine (NCERT)"}
Isotopes of 34.98 u (75.4%) and 36.98 u (24.6%):
$$\frac{75.4 \times 34.98 + 24.6 \times 36.98}{100} = 35.47\ \text{u}$$
This matches the atomic mass of chlorine, which is far from a whole number.
:::

- Hydrogen has three isotopes: hydrogen (1.0078 u), deuterium (2.0141 u) and tritium (3.0160 u). Tritium is unstable and does not occur naturally.
- The nucleus of ordinary hydrogen (99.985% abundance) is the **proton**: $m_p = 1.00727$ u.

## 3. Composition: protons and neutrons

- Protons give the nucleus its charge $+Ze$. The atom is neutral, so it has $Z$ electrons, **all outside** the nucleus.
- **Neutron** discovered by **Chadwick (1932)**: beryllium hit by $\alpha$-particles gave out a neutral radiation that knocked protons out of light nuclei. Photons could not explain the energies, so it had to be a new neutral particle with mass close to the proton's. Nobel Prize 1935.
- $m_n = 1.00866$ u, slightly more than $m_p$.
- A **free** neutron is unstable: it decays into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino, with a mean life of about 1000 s. Inside a nucleus it is stable. A free proton is stable.
- **Nucleon** = a proton or a neutron. The number of nucleons is $A$.

| Name | Same | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Isotopes | $Z$ | ${}^{2}_{1}\text{H}$, ${}^{3}_{1}\text{H}$ |
| Isobars | $A$ | ${}^{3}_{1}\text{H}$, ${}^{3}_{2}\text{He}$ |
| Isotones | $N$ | ${}^{198}_{80}\text{Hg}$, ${}^{197}_{79}\text{Au}$ |

## 4. Size of the nucleus

- **Geiger–Marsden** ($\alpha$-scattering from gold foil): a 5.5 MeV $\alpha$-particle comes no closer than about $4.0 \times 10^{-14}$ m to a gold nucleus. So the gold nucleus is smaller than this.
- With $\alpha$-particles of higher energy the scattering stops following Rutherford's pure Coulomb result. The distance where this starts gives the nuclear size.
- Sizes are now measured accurately by scattering **fast electrons**.
- Result: $R = R_0 A^{1/3}$, $R_0 = 1.2$ fm.
- Volume $\propto A$, so the **density is the same for all nuclei**, about $2.3 \times 10^{17}$ kg m⁻³. Nuclei are like drops of a liquid of constant density.
- Neutron stars have a density close to this: they are like one huge nucleus.

## 5. Mass-energy and binding energy

- **Einstein:** mass is a form of energy, $E = mc^2$. We now have one law: **conservation of mass-energy**.
- A nucleus always has **less** mass than its separate protons and neutrons. The difference is the **mass defect** $\Delta M$.
- To pull the nucleus apart, energy $E_b = \Delta M c^2$ must be supplied. The same energy is **released** when the nucleus forms. $E_b$ is the **binding energy**.

:::example{title="Oxygen-16 (NCERT)"}
8 protons + 8 neutrons add up to $8 \times 2.01593 = 16.12744$ u. The measured atomic mass is 15.99493 u. Subtracting 8 electrons ($8 \times 0.00055$ u), the nucleus is 15.99053 u. So $\Delta M = 0.13691$ u, and $E_b = 0.13691 \times 931.5 \approx 127.5$ MeV.
:::

## 6. Binding energy per nucleon curve

$E_{bn} = E_b/A$ is the better measure of how tightly a nucleus is held. Plotted against $A$:

- **Nearly flat** for $30 < A < 170$, at about 8 MeV.
- **Maximum** about 8.75 MeV at $A = 56$ (iron).
- **Lower** for light nuclei ($A < 30$) and heavy nuclei ($A > 170$); 7.6 MeV at $A = 238$.
- Small **peaks** at ${}^{4}$He, ${}^{16}$O and others: a sign of a shell structure in nuclei.

What it tells us:
1. The nuclear force is attractive and strong: a few MeV per nucleon.
2. The flat part comes from the **short range** of the force. A nucleon feels only its near neighbours, so adding more nucleons does not change its binding. This is the **saturation** property.
3. A heavy nucleus ($A = 240$) splitting into two middle nuclei ($A = 120$) ends up more tightly bound, so energy is released: **fission**.
4. Two very light nuclei ($A \le 10$) joining also end up more tightly bound, so energy is released: **fusion**.

## 7. Nuclear force

The force that holds nucleons together. Its main features:

- **Much stronger** than the Coulomb force (and gravity is far weaker still). It must beat the repulsion between protons.
- **Short range:** it falls quickly to zero beyond a few fm. This gives saturation.
- The potential energy of a nucleon pair is **lowest at $r_0 \approx 0.8$ fm**. Beyond $r_0$ the force is **attractive**; closer than $r_0$ it is **strongly repulsive**.
- **Charge independent:** n–n, p–p and n–p forces are about the same.
- It has **no simple mathematical form**, unlike Coulomb's law or gravity.

## 8. Radioactivity

Discovered by **Becquerel (1896)** by accident: a uranium compound wrapped in black paper blackened a photographic plate. Radioactivity is a **nuclear** process: an unstable nucleus decays.

| Decay | What comes out |
| --- | --- |
| $\alpha$ | A helium nucleus ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ |
| $\beta$ | An electron or a positron |
| $\gamma$ | A high-energy photon (hundreds of keV or more) |

- A **positron** has the electron's mass and the opposite charge. An electron and a positron annihilate into $\gamma$-ray photons.
- **Stability:** light nuclei need $N/Z \approx 1$; heavy nuclei need about **3 : 2** (extra neutrons offset the proton repulsion). Nuclei with too many neutrons or protons are unstable. Only about 10% of known isotopes are stable.

:::remember
The rationalised NCERT book keeps only this short description of radioactivity. The decay law, half-life and mean life were removed. The JEE Main 2026 and NEET 2026 syllabi for this unit do not name radioactivity at all (they list composition and size, atomic masses, mass defect, binding energy per nucleon, fission and fusion), so learn just these short NCERT facts.
:::

## 9. Nuclear energy

- Moving from less tightly bound nuclei to more tightly bound ones releases energy.
- Chemical reactions: energies of a few **eV**. Nuclear reactions: **MeV**, about a million times more per unit mass.
- Fission of 1 kg of uranium gives about $10^{14}$ J; burning 1 kg of coal gives about $10^{7}$ J.

## 10. Fission

A heavy nucleus splits into two middle-mass fragments. The key example is neutron-induced fission of uranium-235:
$$\begin{aligned} & {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\text{U} \to {}^{236}_{92}\text{U} \\ & \to {}^{144}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{89}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \end{aligned}$$

- The same reaction can give other fragment pairs (for example Sb + Nb, or Xe + Sr) and 2 to 4 neutrons.
- Fragments are radioactive; they emit $\beta$ particles one after another until they are stable.
- Energy released (the **Q value**) is about **200 MeV** per fission. It first appears as kinetic energy of the fragments and neutrons, then as heat.
- Nuclear reactors (electricity) run on fission; an atom bomb is **uncontrolled** fission.
- (Beyond the syllabus: not in the current NCERT text or the syllabus lists, but useful background) The 2 to 4 neutrons from each fission can cause more fissions: a **chain reaction**. A reactor keeps it steady; a bomb lets it grow.

## 11. Fusion

Two light nuclei join into a heavier, more tightly bound nucleus, releasing energy. NCERT examples:

Two protons give a deuteron and a positron (0.42 MeV released):
$$\begin{aligned} & {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \\ & \to {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + e^+ + \nu \end{aligned}$$
Two deuterons give light helium (3.27 MeV):
$$\begin{aligned} & {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} \\ & \to {}^{3}_{2}\text{He} + \text{n} \end{aligned}$$
Two deuterons give a triton and a proton (4.03 MeV):
$$\begin{aligned} & {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} \\ & \to {}^{3}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{1}_{1}\text{H} \end{aligned}$$

- Both nuclei are positive, so they must overcome the **Coulomb barrier** to get within range of the nuclear force. For two protons it is about 400 keV, and higher for larger charges.
- Average energy $\tfrac32 kT \approx 400$ keV needs $T \sim 3 \times 10^9$ K. Fusion by heating is **thermonuclear fusion**.
- The sun's core is only $1.5 \times 10^7$ K, so fusion there uses the few protons with energies far above the average.

**The sun (p-p cycle):** hydrogen burns into helium in several steps. Net result, releasing **26.7 MeV**:
$$4\,{}^{1}_{1}\text{H} + 2e^- \to {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + 2\nu + 6\gamma$$
In atoms: four hydrogen atoms become one helium atom.

- The sun is about $5 \times 10^9$ years old and has hydrogen for about 5 billion more years. Then its core will collapse and heat up, and it will swell into a **red giant**.
- When a star's core hydrogen turns to helium, the core collapses under gravity and heats up. If it reaches about $10^8$ K, helium fuses into carbon. Elements beyond the peak of the binding energy curve (near iron) **cannot** be made this way.
- **Controlled fusion:** the fuel is heated to about $10^8$ K and becomes a **plasma**. The challenge is to confine it, since no container can stand that heat. India is among the countries working on it.

## 12. Balancing nuclear reactions

- A chemical equation keeps the number of atoms of each element. A nuclear reaction can change one element into another, so it does **not**.
- What a nuclear reaction keeps: the number of protons and the number of neutrons (strictly, total charge and baryon number).
- So the rest mass of the nucleons is the same on both sides. The energy released comes from the **difference in binding energies**, which shows up as a difference in mass.
- Chemical reactions also convert mass into energy, but their mass defects are about a **million times smaller**, too small to notice.
