## 1. Variation builds up over generations

- Each new generation gets a **common basic body design** from its parents, plus **small changes** in it.
- When that generation reproduces, its offspring carry the old differences **and** some new ones. So variation keeps building up.
- **Asexual reproduction:** one bacterium divides into two, and those two divide again. The four bacteria are very similar. Their only small differences come from **small inaccuracies in DNA copying**.
- **Sexual reproduction** makes **much more** variation, because DNA from two parents is combined.

## 2. Do all variations survive equally?

- No. Different variations give different kinds of advantage.
- Example from NCERT: bacteria that can withstand heat will survive better in a **heat wave**.
- When the environment picks out some variants over others, this is called **selection**. It is the basis of evolution.

:::remember
In an asexual population, a trait found in **more** individuals has probably been there **longer**. It had more generations to spread. (A trait in 60% of the population likely arose before a trait in 10%.)
:::

## 3. Heredity and inherited traits

- **Heredity:** the rules by which traits and characteristics are reliably passed from parents to offspring.
- A child has all the basic features of a human being, but does not look exactly like its parents.
- Human populations show a lot of variation. Example: **free** and **attached earlobes** (Activity 8.1).
- Father and mother give **practically equal** amounts of genetic material to the child. So each trait is influenced by both, and the child has **two versions** of each trait.

## 4. Mendel

- **Gregor Johann Mendel (1822–1884)** was educated in a monastery and studied science and mathematics at the University of Vienna.
- He failed the exam for a teaching certificate, went back to the monastery and grew **garden peas**.
- Others had studied inheritance before him. Mendel was the **first to count** how many individuals showed a trait in each generation. This counting helped him find the laws of inheritance.
- He used **contrasting visible characters** of peas:

| Character | One form | Other form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seed shape | Round | Wrinkled |
| Plant height | Tall | Short |
| Flower colour | Violet | White |

## 5. One trait: tall × short

- Mendel crossed a tall plant with a short plant. All the **F1** (first generation) plants were **tall**. There were no "medium-height" plants. Only one parent's trait was seen, not a mix.
- He then let the F1 tall plants **self-pollinate**. In the **F2** (second generation), **one quarter were short**.
- So the F1 plants carried both tallness and shortness, but only tallness was **expressed**.
- Mendel said: a sexually reproducing organism has **two copies** of a "factor" for each trait. Today we call these factors **genes**. The two copies may be the same or different.
- TT and Tt plants are tall. Only tt is short. One T is enough for tallness.

:::remember
- **Dominant trait:** shows even with one copy (like T).
- **Recessive trait:** shows only when both copies are the same recessive kind (like tt).
:::

- In Fig. 8.4, violet × white flowers gives all violet in the F1 and 3 violet : 1 white in the F2. Violet is dominant and white is recessive.

## 6. Two traits: independent inheritance

- A tall plant with round seeds × a short plant with wrinkled seeds gives F1 plants that are **all tall with round seeds**. Tallness and round seeds are dominant.
- In the F2 (by self-pollination), some plants are like the parents (tall round, short wrinkled). Some show **new combinations**: tall with wrinkled seeds, short with round seeds.
- Fig. 8.5 shows the same idea for seed shape and seed colour: RRyy (round, green) × rrYY (wrinkled, yellow) gives RrYy (round, yellow), and then a **9 : 3 : 3 : 1** F2.
- New combinations can appear only if the two traits are **inherited independently** of each other.

:::remember
The tall/short trait and the round/wrinkled seed trait are **independently inherited**.
:::

## 7. How do traits get expressed?

- **DNA** in the cell is the information source for making **proteins**.
- **Gene:** a section of DNA that gives the information for **one protein**.
- NCERT's example, plant height:
  - Plants have **hormones** that trigger growth. Height depends on how much of one hormone is made.
  - The amount depends on how well an **enzyme** (a protein) works in the process that makes this hormone.
  - Enzyme works well → a lot of hormone → **tall** plant.
  - The gene for the enzyme is **altered**, so the enzyme is less efficient → less hormone → **short** plant.
- So **genes control characteristics (traits)** through the proteins they make.

## 8. Why each germ cell has one gene set

- Both parents help decide a trait, so both must give a copy of the same gene. So each pea plant has **two sets** of all genes, one from each parent.
- For this to work, each **germ cell** must have only **one** gene set.
- A gene set is not one long thread of DNA. It is split into separate, independent pieces called **chromosomes**.
- Each body cell has **two copies of each chromosome**: one from the male parent and one from the female parent.
- Each germ cell takes **one chromosome from each pair**. That one may be of maternal or paternal origin.
- When two germ cells join, the normal number of chromosomes comes back. This keeps the **DNA of the species stable**.
- Because chromosomes go into germ cells separately, genes on different chromosomes (like R and y) are **not tied together**. This explains independent inheritance.
- All sexually reproducing organisms use this mechanism.

:::remember
How is the equal contribution of both parents ensured? Each parent gives **one chromosome of every pair** through its germ cell. The zygote gets half its chromosomes from each parent.
:::

## 9. Sex determination

Different species decide sex in very different ways:
- **Environment:** in a few reptiles, the **temperature** at which the fertilised eggs are kept decides whether they become male or female.
- **Change of sex:** in snails, individuals can change sex. So sex is not genetically decided there.
- **Genes:** in human beings, sex is **largely genetically determined**.

In humans:
- **22 pairs** of chromosomes have a maternal and a paternal copy.
- One more pair, the **sex chromosomes**, is not always a perfect pair.
- Women have a perfect pair: **XX**. Men have a mismatched pair: a normal-sized **X** and a short **Y**. So women are XX and men are XY.
- Every child gets an **X from the mother**, boy or girl.
- A child who gets an **X from the father** is a **girl** (XX). A child who gets a **Y from the father** is a **boy** (XY).
- So half the children are boys and half are girls, and the **father's chromosome** decides the sex of the child.

:::tip
Mother gives: always X. Father gives: X or Y. **X + X = girl, X + Y = boy.**
:::
