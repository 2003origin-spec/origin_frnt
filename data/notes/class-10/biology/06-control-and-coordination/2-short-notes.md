## 1. Why we need control and coordination

- Living things respond to changes around them (**stimuli**). Each change needs the **right** response: in class we whisper to a friend; we do not shout.
- So the body needs systems that **detect** a change and make only the correct movement.
- In animals, control and coordination are done by **nervous tissue** and **muscle tissue**, and also by **hormones**.

## 2. Neurons and the nerve impulse

- Nervous tissue is a network of nerve cells (**neurons**). It carries information as **electrical impulses**.
- **Receptors** are special tips of some nerve cells, mostly in the sense organs (inner ear, nose, tongue). **Gustatory** receptors detect taste; **olfactory** receptors detect smell.

**How a message travels**
1. At the tip of a **dendrite**, information sets off a chemical reaction that creates an electrical impulse.
2. The impulse travels to the **cell body**, then along the **axon** to its end.
3. There it makes the neuron **release chemicals**.
4. These cross the gap (**synapse**) and start a similar impulse in the next neuron.
5. A similar synapse passes the message to a **muscle cell** (neuromuscular junction) or a **gland**.

:::remember
Inside a neuron the message is **electrical**. Across a synapse it is **chemical**.
:::

**Activity 6.1** (the book only asks why; this answer is beyond the Class 10 book): with the nose blocked, sugar still tastes sweet, because the **tongue** detects sweetness. But food tastes flat, because much of its flavour comes from **smell** (olfactory receptors in the nose). The same happens when we have a cold.

## 3. Reflex action and the reflex arc

- A **reflex action** is a sudden response done **without thinking**, like pulling the hand back from a flame.
- Thinking is slow. If we had to think first, we could get burnt. So the nerves that **detect** heat are joined to the nerves that **move muscles** by a short path: the **reflex arc**.

**The reflex arc (Fig. 6.2)**
1. Receptor: heat or pain receptors in the skin
2. **Sensory neuron** carries the message in
3. **Relay neuron** in the spinal cord passes it on
4. **Motor neuron** carries the order out
5. Effector: muscle in the arm, which pulls the hand away

Reflex arcs are formed in the **spinal cord**, where nerves from all over the body first meet. The message **also** goes on to the brain. Reflex arcs evolved because thinking is not fast enough.

Not every reflex passes through the spinal cord. In the reflex to bright light (the pupil gets smaller), the message goes from the eye to the brain (mid-brain) and straight back to the eye muscles (beyond the Class 10 book).

## 4. Central and peripheral nervous system

- **Central nervous system (CNS):** **brain** + **spinal cord**. They receive and put together information from the whole body. The brain is the **main coordinating centre**.
- **Peripheral nervous system (PNS):** **cranial nerves** (from the brain) and **spinal nerves** (from the spinal cord). It links the CNS with the rest of the body.
- **Voluntary actions** are done after deciding: writing, talking, moving a chair. The brain sends messages to the muscles.

## 5. The human brain

The brain has three main parts: **fore-brain**, **mid-brain** and **hind-brain**.

**Fore-brain** (the main thinking part)
- **Sensory areas** receive impulses; separate areas deal with hearing, smell, sight and so on.
- **Association areas** make sense of it, using other inputs and what is stored in the brain. Then a decision is made.
- **Motor areas** control voluntary muscles, such as leg muscles.
- A separate part has the **hunger centre** (the feeling of being full).
- In Fig. 6.3, the large upper part marked as fore-brain is the **cerebrum**. The **hypothalamus**, labelled just below it, is also a part of the fore-brain (beyond the Class 10 book).

**Mid-brain and hind-brain**
- Control many **involuntary actions**, which we cannot easily control by thinking: heartbeat, breathing, mouth watering at food we like.
- **Medulla** (hind-brain) controls involuntary actions such as **blood pressure, salivation and vomiting**.
- **Cerebellum** (hind-brain) makes voluntary actions **precise** and keeps the body's **posture and balance**. Examples: walking in a straight line, riding a bicycle, picking up a pencil.

:::remember
Three kinds of nervous response: **reflex** (sudden, no thinking, e.g. change in the size of the pupil in bright light), **voluntary** (thought out, e.g. moving a chair) and **involuntary** (no thinking control, e.g. heartbeat).
:::

## 6. Protection, and how muscles move

- The brain sits in a **bony box** (skull, or cranium), inside a **fluid-filled balloon** that absorbs shocks. The **vertebral column** (backbone) protects the spinal cord.
- Muscle cells have **special proteins**. When a nerve impulse arrives, these change their **shape** and **arrangement**, so the cell becomes **shorter**.
- Muscles may be **voluntary** or **involuntary** (studied in Class IX).

## 7. Coordination in plants

Plants have **no nervous system and no muscles**, yet they respond to stimuli. They show two types of movement: one **independent of growth** (sensitive plant) and one **dependent on growth** (shoot bending to light).

**Sensitive plant (chhui-mui, touch-me-not, Mimosa family)**
- Its leaves fold up and droop **quickly** when touched. No growth is involved.
- The leaves move at a point **different** from where they were touched, so the message must travel. It goes from cell to cell by **electrical-chemical** means. Plants have **no special tissue** for conducting information.
- Cells change shape by changing the **amount of water** in them. They swell or shrink, so the leaf moves.

**Tendrils (pea plant)**
- Tendrils are sensitive to touch. The part touching a support grows **more slowly** than the part away from it, so the tendril curls around the support.

## 8. Tropisms (directional growth)

- Plants often respond **slowly**, by growing in one direction, so the plant seems to move. These **tropic** movements can be **towards** the stimulus or **away** from it.
- **Phototropism** (light): shoots bend **towards** light; roots bend **away** from it.
- **Geotropism** (gravity): roots grow **downwards**; shoots usually grow **upwards**, away from the earth.
- **Hydrotropism** (water) and **chemotropism** (chemicals). Example of chemotropism: a **pollen tube** grows towards the **ovule**.
- Activity 6.2: shoots of bean seedlings bend towards light and roots away from it. When the flask is turned, the **old** parts do not change direction. Only the **new** growth bends.
- Speeds differ: the sensitive plant is very quick; sunflowers turning with day and night are slow; growth is slower still.

## 9. Electrical vs chemical messages

Electrical impulses are fast, but:
1. They reach only cells joined by **nervous tissue**.
2. A cell needs time to **reset** after each impulse, so it cannot fire non-stop.

So most multicellular organisms also use **chemicals (hormones)**. A stimulated cell releases a chemical; other cells detect it with **special molecules on their surface**. This is **slower**, but it reaches **all cells** and works **steadily and persistently**.

## 10. Plant hormones

- They coordinate growth, development and responses to the environment. They are **made away from where they act** and **diffuse** to the place of action.

**Auxin and phototropism**
1. Auxin is made at the **shoot tip**. It helps cells grow **longer**.
2. When light comes from one side, auxin diffuses towards the **shady side** of the shoot.
3. Cells on the shady side grow longer than those on the lit side.
4. So the shoot **bends towards the light**.

**Other plant hormones**
- **Gibberellins:** like auxins, help the stem grow.
- **Cytokinins:** promote **cell division**. So they are found in larger amounts where cells divide fast, such as fruits and seeds.
- **Abscisic acid:** **inhibits** growth. Its effects include **wilting** of leaves.

Auxin, gibberellins and cytokinins **promote** growth. Abscisic acid **stops** it.

## 11. Hormones in animals

- Animal hormones are made by **endocrine glands**. The endocrine system is the **second** way of control and coordination in our body (the first is the nervous system).
- Fig. 6.7 shows: hypothalamus, pineal, pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, thymus, pancreas, adrenals, testes or ovaries.

**Adrenaline: getting ready to fight or run**
- A scared animal (say a squirrel) must get ready to fight or run. Nerves reach only some tissues; a hormone reaches all.
- **Adrenaline** is made by the **adrenal glands** and secreted **directly into the blood**.
- Its target organs include the **heart**. The heart beats faster, so muscles get more oxygen.
- Muscles around small arteries of the **digestive system and skin** contract. Less blood goes there and more goes to the **skeletal muscles**.
- The **breathing rate** goes up, because the diaphragm and rib muscles contract more.

**Other hormones**
- **Thyroxin** (thyroid) regulates **carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism** for balanced growth. The thyroid needs **iodine** to make it. Too little iodine can cause **goitre** (a **swollen neck**). So we use **iodised salt**.
- **Growth hormone** (pituitary) regulates growth and development. Too little in **childhood** causes **dwarfism**.
- The **hypothalamus** controls the release of many hormones. When growth hormone is low, it releases **growth hormone releasing factor**, which makes the pituitary release growth hormone.
- **Puberty** changes (around 10–12 years) are caused by **testosterone** in males (testes) and **oestrogen** in females (ovaries).
- **Insulin** (pancreas) regulates **blood sugar**. Too little, and blood sugar rises: **diabetes**. Some patients get **insulin injections**.

## 12. Feedback mechanism

- The timing and amount of each hormone are controlled by **feedback**. Blood sugar rises → pancreas cells detect it → **more insulin**. Blood sugar falls → **less insulin**.
