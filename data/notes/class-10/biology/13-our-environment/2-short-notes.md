## 1. What is an ecosystem?

- Plants, animals, microorganisms, human beings and their physical surroundings all interact. Together they keep a balance in nature.
- **Ecosystem:** all the interacting organisms in an area **together with** the non-living parts of their environment.
- **Biotic components:** the living organisms.
- **Abiotic components:** physical factors such as temperature, rainfall, wind, soil and minerals.
- The growth, reproduction and other activities of living things are affected by the abiotic components.

| Natural ecosystems | Human-made (artificial) ecosystems |
| --- | --- |
| Forests, ponds, lakes | Gardens, crop-fields, aquariums |

**Example, a garden:** grasses, trees, rose, jasmine and sunflower plants, with frogs, insects and birds. They interact with each other and with the soil, water and air. So a garden is an ecosystem.

## 2. The aquarium (NCERT activity)

- Fish need space to swim, water, oxygen (from an aerator) and food.
- If we add a few water plants and animals, the aquarium can become a **self-sustaining** system.
- It is a **human-made ecosystem**.
- We still clean an aquarium now and then: it is small and not fully balanced, so wastes and dead remains build up. In a pond or lake, natural processes such as decomposers clean up, so we do not clean them the same way.
- Don't put in an animal that eats the others. It would eat them up and the system would break down.

## 3. Producers, consumers and decomposers

**Producers**
- Make organic compounds such as sugar and starch from inorganic substances.
- They use the radiant energy of the Sun, in the presence of chlorophyll (photosynthesis).
- All green plants and certain bacteria are producers.

**Consumers**
- Eat the food made by producers, either directly or by eating other consumers.
- Types: **herbivores** (cow, deer), **carnivores** (tiger, lion), **omnivores** (humans, crow) and **parasites** (lice, tapeworm). These examples are ours; the book asks you to find your own.

**Decomposers**
- Microorganisms: **bacteria and fungi**.
- They break down dead remains and waste products of organisms.
- They turn complex organic substances into **simple inorganic substances**. These go into the soil and are used again by plants.
- Without decomposers, garbage and dead plants and animals would pile up, and the soil would not be refilled with nutrients naturally.

## 4. Food chains

- **Food chain:** a series of organisms, each eating the one before it.
- Examples from NCERT's pictures:
  - Forest: plants → deer → tiger
  - Grassland: grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → a bird of prey (not labelled; it looks like an eagle)
- The arrow means "is eaten by". It points in the direction the food and energy move.

## 5. Trophic levels

Each step or level of a food chain is a **trophic level**.

| Level | Who | Also called |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | Autotrophs | Producers |
| 2nd | Herbivores | Primary consumers |
| 3rd | Small carnivores | Secondary consumers |
| 4th | Larger carnivores | Tertiary consumers |

- Producers fix solar energy and make it available to the consumers (heterotrophs).
- There is generally a **greater number of individuals at the lower trophic levels**. The greatest number is of the **producers**.

## 6. Flow of energy

The food we eat is fuel for doing work. So the links in an ecosystem are really a **flow of energy** from one part to another.

- Autotrophs capture the energy in sunlight and turn it into **chemical energy**. This energy supports all activities of the living world.
- From autotrophs the energy goes to heterotrophs and decomposers.
- When energy changes from one form to another, some is lost to the environment in forms that cannot be used again.

What scientists found:
- Green plants on land capture about **1%** of the sunlight falling on their leaves and turn it into food energy.
- When primary consumers eat plants, a great deal of energy is lost as **heat**. Some goes into digestion and doing work. The rest goes into growth and reproduction.
- On average only **10%** of the food eaten becomes the eater's own body and is available to the next level. So 10% is the average amount of organic matter that reaches the next level. This is often called the **10% law** (the book does not use this name).

:::example
Producers hold 10,000 J. Then herbivores get 1,000 J, small carnivores 100 J and larger carnivores only 10 J.
:::

**Why food chains are short:** so little energy reaches the next level that chains generally have only **three or four steps**. After four trophic levels very little usable energy is left.

### Two facts from the energy flow diagram

- **Energy flow is one-way (unidirectional).** Energy captured by plants does not go back to the Sun. Energy passed to herbivores does not come back to plants. Once it moves up, the level below cannot use it again.
- **Energy keeps decreasing** at each higher level, because some is lost at every level.

## 7. Food webs

- Each organism is generally eaten by **two or more** kinds of organisms. These are eaten by several others in turn.
- So the real picture is not one straight line but a set of **branching lines**: a **food web**.
- A food web is made of many food chains linked together.

## 8. Biological magnification

- Farmers use pesticides and other chemicals to protect crops from pests and diseases.
- These chemicals wash into the soil or into water bodies.
- Plants absorb them from the soil along with water and minerals. Water plants and animals take them up from water. This is how they **enter the food chain**.
- These chemicals are **not degradable**, so they **build up at each trophic level**.
- Human beings are at the **top** of any food chain, so the **highest concentration** builds up in our bodies.
- **Biological magnification:** the build-up of non-degradable chemicals, increasing at each higher trophic level.
- This is why wheat, rice, vegetables, fruits and even meat contain some pesticide residue. Washing cannot always remove it.

:::remember
Up a food chain, **energy goes down** but the **concentration of harmful chemicals goes up**.
:::

## 9. The ozone layer

- **Ozone ($\ce{O3}$):** a molecule of **three** oxygen atoms. Ordinary oxygen ($\ce{O2}$) is needed by all aerobic life.
- Ozone itself is a **deadly poison**. But at the **higher levels of the atmosphere** it does an essential job: it shields the Earth's surface from the Sun's **ultraviolet (UV) radiation**.
- UV radiation is highly damaging to living things. For example, it causes **skin cancer** in humans.

How ozone forms high up:
$$\ce{O2 ->[UV] O + O}$$
$$\ce{O + O2 -> O3}$$
High-energy UV splits some $\ce{O2}$ into free oxygen atoms. Each atom then joins an $\ce{O2}$ molecule to form ozone.

**Ozone depletion**
- The amount of ozone began to **drop sharply in the 1980s**.
- This was linked to synthetic chemicals called **chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)**, used as **refrigerants** and in **fire extinguishers**.
- **1987:** the United Nations Environment Programme (**UNEP**) got countries to agree to **freeze CFC production at 1986 levels**.
- Now all makers must build **CFC-free refrigerators** throughout the world.

## 10. Biodegradable and non-biodegradable waste

- **Enzymes are specific.** Each enzyme breaks down only a particular substance. That is why we get no energy from eating coal.
- For the same reason, bacteria and other saprophytes cannot break down many human-made materials, such as **plastics**.
- Heat and pressure can act on them, but in normal conditions in our environment they **persist for a long time**.

| Biodegradable | Non-biodegradable |
| --- | --- |
| Broken down by biological processes | Not broken down by biological processes |
| Kitchen waste, paper, cotton, wood, leaves | Plastics, polythene, glass |
| Breaks down in time (peels in days, wood over years) | Stays a long time; may be inert or may harm the ecosystem |

## 11. Managing our garbage

- Better lifestyles produce **more waste**.
- More and more things we use are **disposable**.
- Changes in **packaging** have made much of our waste non-biodegradable.
- Local bodies (panchayat, municipal corporation) should collect waste and treat biodegradable and non-biodegradable waste **separately**.
- Sewage and factory waste must be treated so they do not pollute soil and water.
- Throwing away electronic items needs care, because they contain hazardous materials.

**Disposable cups in trains (NCERT case study)**

| Stage | Cup | Point to note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earlier | Plastic glasses, returned to the vendor | Replaced for hygiene |

Lesson: a "solution" to one problem can create another. Think about the full impact before choosing.
