## Ecosystem basics

- An ecosystem has **both** parts: living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic). Living things alone are not an ecosystem.
- A garden, a crop-field and an aquarium are **human-made** ecosystems. Forests, ponds and lakes are **natural**.
- Producers include green plants **and certain bacteria**. Not only plants.

:::trap
Decomposers are not just "cleaners". They break complex organic matter into **simple inorganic substances**. These go back into the soil and plants use them again. Without them, the soil would not be refilled with nutrients.
:::

## Food chains and trophic levels

- Producers are always at the **first** trophic level.
- Herbivores = primary consumers = the **second** trophic level, not the first.
- Count the trophic level from the producer. In grass → grasshopper → frog → snake, the frog is at the **third** level, but it is the **secondary** consumer.

:::trap
"Trophic level number" and "consumer order" are off by one. Third trophic level = secondary consumer. Fourth trophic level = tertiary consumer.
:::

- A valid food chain must **start with a producer** and each organism must eat the one before. "Goat, cow, elephant" is not a food chain: all three are herbivores.
- A food web is many food chains joined together. Real organisms usually eat, and are eaten by, more than one kind.

:::trap
If you remove one trophic level, the whole chain is affected. Remove the herbivores: plants may grow unchecked and the carnivores above starve. No level can be removed without some harm.
:::

## Energy and the 10% law

:::remember
- About **1%** of sunlight falling on leaves is captured by green plants (on land).
- About **10%** of the energy at one trophic level reaches the next.
- Energy flow is **one-way** (unidirectional). It never goes back to the level below or to the Sun.
:::

:::trap
Do not mix up the two numbers. **1%** is for sunlight → plants. **10%** is for one trophic level → the next. If a sum starts from sunlight, use 1% only once (at the start), then 10% at every step.
:::

- The 10% is an **average**, not an exact value.
- The other 90% is not "destroyed". It is mostly lost as **heat**, and some is used in digestion and work. It cannot be used again by the next level.
- Energy keeps decreasing up the chain. So food chains generally have only **3 or 4 steps**.

:::tip
Going up one level: **÷ 10**. Going down one level: **× 10**. Three steps up = ÷ 1000.
:::

- Numbers of individuals: **generally** most at the producer level and fewer at each higher level.

## Biological magnification

:::trap
Energy **decreases** up a food chain, but the concentration of non-degradable chemicals **increases**. Don't say "pesticides decrease like energy".
:::

- It happens only with chemicals that are **not degradable**. They are not broken down or passed out, so they build up.
- **Humans** get the highest concentration because we are at the top of the food chain (NCERT's line).
- In a chain without humans, the **top consumer** has the most. Example: the kingfisher in water plants → small fish → big fish → kingfisher.
- Pesticide residue is found in grains, vegetables, fruits and meat. Washing **cannot always** remove it.

## Ozone

:::remember
- Ozone = $\ce{O3}$ (three oxygen atoms). Oxygen we breathe = $\ce{O2}$.
- Ozone at ground level is a **deadly poison**. High up in the atmosphere it **protects** us from UV radiation.
- UV causes **skin cancer** in humans.
:::

:::trap
CFCs were used as **refrigerants and in fire extinguishers**. The 1987 UNEP agreement **froze** CFC production at **1986** levels. It did not say "ban" and the year is not 1986.
:::

- Ozone forms when UV splits $\ce{O2}$ into two O atoms, and each O joins another $\ce{O2}$.
- Ozone levels started falling sharply in the **1980s**.

## Waste

- Enzymes are **specific**. Bacteria and other saprophytes do not have enzymes that break down most plastics. That is why most plastics are non-biodegradable. (The book notes that some newer plastics are said to be biodegradable.)
- Non-biodegradable substances may be **inert** and just stay, or they may **harm** members of the ecosystem.

:::trap
"All our waste is biodegradable" does **not** mean "no harm". Heaps of biodegradable waste still rot, smell, breed flies and germs, and take time to break down. Too much of it at once is still a problem.
:::

- Wood, paper, cotton, grass, fruit peels and cake are all biodegradable. Plastic, polythene and glass are not.
- Kulhads (clay cups) on a large scale would waste **fertile top-soil**. This is NCERT's example of a solution that creates a new problem.

:::tip
Easy ways to cut waste (good for a 2-mark answer): carry cloth bags, separate wet and dry waste, compost kitchen waste, reuse containers, and avoid single-use plastic.
:::
