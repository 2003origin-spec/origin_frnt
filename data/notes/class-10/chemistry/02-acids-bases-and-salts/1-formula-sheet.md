## Reactions of acids

:::formula{title="Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen"}
$$\ce{Zn + H2SO4 -> ZnSO4 + H2}$$
$$\ce{Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2}$$
Use a **dilute** acid (not nitric acid) and a reactive metal such as Mg, Al, Zn or Fe. Copper gives no $\ce{H2}$. Test for $\ce{H2}$: it burns with a **pop** sound near a burning candle.
:::

:::formula{title="Acid + metal carbonate or hydrogencarbonate"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Na2CO3 + 2HCl} \\ & \ce{-> 2NaCl + H2O + CO2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{NaHCO3 + HCl} \\ & \ce{-> NaCl + H2O + CO2} \end{aligned}$$
Always gives salt + carbon dioxide + water. Limestone, chalk, marble and egg-shells are $\ce{CaCO3}$ and react the same way.
:::

:::formula{title="Test for CO2: lime water"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Ca(OH)2 + CO2} \\ & \ce{-> CaCO3 v + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Lime water turns **milky** (white $\ce{CaCO3}$).

With **excess** $\ce{CO2}$ the milkiness goes away:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CaCO3 + H2O + CO2} \\ & \ce{-> Ca(HCO3)2} \end{aligned}$$
$\ce{Ca(HCO3)2}$ is soluble in water.
:::

:::formula{title="Acid + base → salt + water (neutralisation)"}
$$\ce{NaOH + HCl -> NaCl + H2O}$$
The real change in every acid–base neutralisation in water:
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{H+(aq) + OH^-(aq)} \\ & \ce{-> H2O(l)} \end{aligned}$$
:::

:::formula{title="Acid + metal oxide → salt + water"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CuO + 2HCl} \\ & \ce{-> CuCl2 + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Black CuO dissolves and the solution turns **blue-green** ($\ce{CuCl2}$). So metal oxides are generally **basic oxides**. (Exceptions: $\ce{Al2O3}$ and ZnO are **amphoteric**, Chapter 3.)
:::

## Reactions of bases

:::formula{title="Base + metal (only some metals)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2NaOH + Zn} \\ & \ce{-> Na2ZnO2 + H2} \end{aligned}$$
Needs warming. $\ce{Na2ZnO2}$ = sodium zincate.
:::

:::formula{title="Base + non-metal oxide → salt + water"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Ca(OH)2 + CO2} \\ & \ce{-> CaCO3 + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
This is like acid + base, so non-metal oxides (here $\ce{CO2}$) are **acidic oxides**.
:::

## Acids and bases in water

:::formula{title="Acids give H⁺ (as H₃O⁺)"}
$$\ce{HCl + H2O -> H3O+ + Cl-}$$
$$\ce{H+ + H2O -> H3O+}$$
Only **in water**. Dry HCl gas gives no $\ce{H+}$.
:::

:::formula{title="Bases give OH⁻"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{NaOH(s) ->[H2O]} \\ & \ce{Na+(aq) + OH^-(aq)} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Mg(OH)2(s) ->[H2O]} \\ & \ce{Mg^2+(aq) + 2OH^-(aq)} \end{aligned}$$
A base that dissolves in water is an **alkali**.
:::

## pH scale

| pH | Nature |
| --- | --- |
| Below 7 | Acidic (lower pH = more $\ce{H+}$) |
| 7 | Neutral |
| Above 7 | Basic (higher pH = more $\ce{OH-}$) |

Values from NCERT: gastric juice about 1.2, lemon juice about 2.2, pure water 7, blood 7.4, milk of magnesia 10, NaOH solution about 14.

## Chemicals from common salt

:::formula{title="Chlor-alkali process (electrolysis of brine)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2NaCl(aq) + 2H2O(l)} \\ & \ce{-> 2NaOH(aq) + Cl2(g) + H2(g)} \end{aligned}$$
$\ce{Cl2}$ at the **anode**, $\ce{H2}$ at the **cathode**, NaOH near the cathode.
:::

:::formula{title="Bleaching powder"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2Ca(OH)2 + 2Cl2} \\ & \ce{-> Ca(ClO)2 + CaCl2 + 2H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Chlorine on **dry** slaked lime. NCERT writes bleaching powder as $\ce{Ca(ClO)2}$; the real make-up is more complex.
:::

:::formula{title="Baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{NaCl + H2O + CO2 + NH3} \\ & \ce{-> NH4Cl + NaHCO3} \end{aligned}$$
On heating (cooking):
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2NaHCO3 ->[heat]} \\ & \ce{Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2} \end{aligned}$$
:::

:::formula{title="Baking powder in water or on heating"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{NaHCO3 + H+} \\ & \ce{-> CO2 + H2O + {sodium salt}} \end{aligned}$$
Baking powder = baking soda + **tartaric acid**. The $\ce{CO2}$ makes cake rise.
:::

:::formula{title="Washing soda"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{Na2CO3 + 10H2O} \\ & \ce{-> Na2CO3.10H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Made by recrystallising sodium carbonate (got by heating baking soda).
:::

## Water of crystallisation

:::formula{title="Copper sulphate: blue to white and back"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CuSO4.5H2O ->[heat]} \\ & \ce{CuSO4 + 5H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Blue crystals turn **white** on heating. Add water and the blue colour returns.
:::

:::formula{title="Plaster of Paris sets into gypsum"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O + 3/2H2O} \\ & \ce{-> CaSO4.2H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Plaster of Paris is made by heating gypsum at **373 K**. "½ $\ce{H2O}$" means two $\ce{CaSO4}$ units share one water molecule.
:::

## Uses at a glance

| Chemical | Main uses |
| --- | --- |
| $\ce{Cl2}$ | Water treatment, swimming pools, PVC, disinfectants, CFCs, pesticides |
| $\ce{H2}$ | Fuels, margarine, ammonia for fertilisers |
| NaOH | De-greasing metals, soaps and detergents, paper, artificial fibres |
| Bleaching powder | Bleaching cotton, linen, wood pulp, washed clothes; oxidising agent; germ-free drinking water |
| Baking soda | Baking powder, antacid, soda-acid fire extinguisher |
| Washing soda | Glass, soap, paper; making borax; cleaning; removing permanent hardness |
| Plaster of Paris | Plaster for broken bones, toys, decoration, smooth surfaces |
