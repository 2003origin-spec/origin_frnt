## 1. Acids and bases: first look

- **Acids** taste sour and turn **blue litmus red**.
- **Bases** taste bitter and turn **red litmus blue**. Alkalis feel soapy.
- Never taste or touch lab acids and bases. They can harm you.

## 2. Indicators

An **indicator** tells us whether a substance is acidic or basic. It does this by changing colour (or smell).

| Indicator | In acid | In base |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Litmus (purple when neutral) | Red | Blue |
| Phenolphthalein | Colourless | Pink |
| Methyl orange | Red | Yellow |
| Turmeric | Yellow | Reddish-brown |

Phenolphthalein and turmeric do not change colour in acid. They look the same in an acid and in a neutral solution.

- **Natural indicators:** litmus (a purple dye from **lichen**), turmeric, red cabbage leaves, and petals of Hydrangea, Petunia and Geranium.
- **Synthetic indicators:** methyl orange, phenolphthalein.
- **Olfactory indicators** change their **smell** in acid or base. The book tests onion, vanilla essence and clove oil. Their smell goes away in a **base** (NaOH) but stays in an acid (HCl).
- A curry stain (turmeric) turns reddish-brown when soap is rubbed on it. Soap is basic.

## 3. Reactions of acids and bases

**Acid + metal** gives a salt and hydrogen gas (with reactive metals such as Zn, Mg, Al, Fe; not copper). Hydrogen burns with a **pop** sound.

**Base + metal** also gives hydrogen, but only with some metals. Zinc with warm NaOH gives sodium zincate ($\ce{Na2ZnO2}$) and $\ce{H2}$.

**Acid + metal carbonate or hydrogencarbonate** gives a salt, $\ce{CO2}$ and water.
- $\ce{CO2}$ turns **lime water milky** (white $\ce{CaCO3}$ forms).
- Too much $\ce{CO2}$ makes the milkiness go away. Soluble $\ce{Ca(HCO3)2}$ forms.
- Limestone, chalk and marble are all forms of calcium carbonate.

**Acid + base** gives a salt and water. This is **neutralisation**.
- NaOH with phenolphthalein is pink. Add HCl drop by drop and the pink colour goes. Add NaOH again and pink comes back.

**Acid + metal oxide** gives a salt and water. So metal oxides are generally **basic oxides**. (Aluminium oxide and zinc oxide are amphoteric. See Chapter 3.)
- Copper oxide dissolves in dilute HCl. The solution turns **blue-green** because copper(II) chloride forms.

**Base + non-metal oxide** gives a salt and water. So non-metal oxides are **acidic oxides**. Example: $\ce{CO2}$ with lime water.

## 4. What all acids and all bases have in common

- Acid solutions **conduct electricity**. Glucose and alcohol solutions do not, even though they contain hydrogen. So not every compound with hydrogen is an acid.
- Acids give **$\ce{H+}$ ions in water**. These ions cause the acidic properties.
- $\ce{H+}$ cannot exist alone. It joins a water molecule to form the **hydronium ion**, $\ce{H3O+}$. So write it as $\ce{H+(aq)}$ or $\ce{H3O+}$.
- **Dry HCl gas** does not turn dry blue litmus red. Without water, HCl gives no $\ce{H+}$ ions. Wet litmus does turn red.
- Bases give **$\ce{OH-}$ ions in water**.
- An **alkali** is a base that **dissolves in water**. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.
- Neutralisation is really $\ce{H+ + OH- -> H2O}$.

## 5. Mixing acids and bases with water

- Dissolving an acid or a base in water is **highly exothermic** (gives out a lot of heat).
- **Always add acid to water**, slowly, with stirring. Never add water to concentrated acid. The heat can make the mixture splash out and cause burns. The glass may also crack.
- Adding water lowers the amount of $\ce{H3O+}$ or $\ce{OH-}$ in each unit of volume. This is **dilution**.

## 6. The pH scale

- **Universal indicator** is a mixture of several indicators. It shows a different colour at each $\ce{H+}$ concentration.
- **pH** runs from 0 (very acidic) to 14 (very alkaline). The "p" is from German *potenz*, meaning power.
- pH 7 is **neutral**. Below 7 is **acidic**. Above 7 is **basic**.
- **More $\ce{H+}$ means lower pH.** As pH goes from 7 to 14, the $\ce{OH-}$ concentration rises.

| Substance (NCERT Fig. 2.7) | pH (about) |
| --- | --- |
| Gastric juice | 1.2 |
| Lemon juice | 2.2 |
| Pure water | 7 |
| Blood | 7.4 |
| Milk of magnesia | 10 |
| Sodium hydroxide solution | 14 |

## 7. Strong and weak acids and bases

- **Strong acid:** gives **more** $\ce{H+}$ ions, e.g. HCl.
- **Weak acid:** gives **fewer** $\ce{H+}$ ions, e.g. acetic acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$), at the same concentration.
- In the same way, a strong base gives more $\ce{OH-}$ ions and a weak base gives fewer.
- (Beyond the Class 10 book.) Strong is not the same as concentrated. Strength is about how many ions the acid gives. Concentration is about how much acid is in the water.

## 8. pH in everyday life

- **Our body** works between pH **7.0 and 7.8**. Living things survive only in a narrow pH range.
- **Acid rain:** rain with pH **below 5.6**. It lowers the pH of rivers and harms aquatic life.
- **Soil:** each plant needs a certain soil pH. Too-acidic soil is treated with quicklime (CaO), slaked lime ($\ce{Ca(OH)2}$) or chalk ($\ce{CaCO3}$).
- **Digestion:** the stomach makes HCl. Too much acid causes pain and irritation. **Antacids** (mild bases) such as **milk of magnesia**, $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$, neutralise the extra acid.
- **Tooth decay** starts when the pH in the mouth falls **below 5.5**. Bacteria make acids from sugar and food left in the mouth. Enamel (calcium hydroxyapatite, a form of calcium phosphate) is the hardest substance in the body, but it is corroded by this acid. Basic toothpastes neutralise the acid.
- **Bee sting:** leaves an acid. Rub on **baking soda** (a mild base).
- **Nettle sting:** stinging hairs inject **methanoic acid**. The traditional remedy is the leaf of the **dock** plant, which grows nearby.
- **Venus** has thick clouds of sulphuric acid.

| Natural source | Acid |
| --- | --- |
| Vinegar | Acetic acid |
| Orange, lemon | Citric acid |
| Tamarind | Tartaric acid |
| Tomato | Oxalic acid |
| Sour milk (curd) | Lactic acid |
| Ant sting, nettle sting | Methanoic acid |

## 9. Salts and their pH

- Salts with the same positive or negative part form a **family**. NaCl and $\ce{Na2SO4}$ are sodium salts. NaCl and KCl are chloride salts.

| Salt made from | pH of solution | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong acid + strong base | 7 (neutral) | NaCl, $\ce{KNO3}$ |
| Strong acid + weak base | Below 7 (acidic) | $\ce{NH4Cl}$, $\ce{CuSO4}$ |
| Strong base + weak acid | Above 7 (basic) | $\ce{Na2CO3}$, $\ce{CH3COONa}$ |

## 10. Chemicals from common salt

- **Common salt** (NaCl) comes from **seawater**. Brown crystals of **rock salt** are mined. Rock-salt beds formed when ancient seas dried up.
- **Sodium hydroxide:** electricity through **brine** (salt solution) gives NaOH, $\ce{Cl2}$ and $\ce{H2}$. This is the **chlor-alkali process**. $\ce{Cl2}$ forms at the anode, $\ce{H2}$ at the cathode, and NaOH near the cathode.
- **Bleaching powder:** chlorine on **dry slaked lime**. NCERT writes it as $\ce{Ca(ClO)2}$.
- **Baking soda** ($\ce{NaHCO3}$): made from NaCl, water, $\ce{CO2}$ and ammonia. It is a **mild, non-corrosive basic salt**. On heating it gives $\ce{Na2CO3}$, water and $\ce{CO2}$.
- **Baking powder** = baking soda + **tartaric acid** (a weak edible acid). With heat or water it gives $\ce{CO2}$, which makes bread and cake soft and spongy.
- **Washing soda** ($\ce{Na2CO3.10H2O}$): heat baking soda to get sodium carbonate, then recrystallise it. It is also a basic salt.

## 11. Water of crystallisation

- **Water of crystallisation** is the **fixed number of water molecules** in one formula unit of a salt. The crystals still look dry.
- **Copper sulphate**, $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$, is blue. Heating removes the water and leaves a **white** powder. Add a few drops of water and the **blue colour returns**.
- **Gypsum** is $\ce{CaSO4.2H2O}$.
- Heat gypsum at **373 K** to get **Plaster of Paris**, $\ce{CaSO4.1/2H2O}$ (calcium sulphate hemihydrate). It is a white powder.
- Mix Plaster of Paris with water and it sets back into **gypsum**, a hard solid mass. So it must be kept in a **moisture-proof** container.
- "Half a water molecule" means **two** $\ce{CaSO4}$ units share **one** water molecule.
- Uses: supporting fractured bones, toys, decoration, making surfaces smooth. (Beyond the Class 10 book: the name comes from large gypsum beds near Paris.)
