## 1. Has a reaction taken place?

In a chemical change, new substances form. This is a **chemical reaction**. Everyday examples: milk going sour, iron rusting, grapes fermenting, food cooking or being digested, and breathing (respiration).

You can tell a reaction has happened from any of these signs:
- a **change in state** (for example, a solid forms in a solution);
- a **change in colour**;
- a **gas** given off (bubbles);
- a **change in temperature** (it gets warm or cold).

## 2. Chemical equations

- **Word equation:** the reaction in words. Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium oxide.
- **Reactants** are on the left-hand side (LHS). **Products** are on the right-hand side (RHS). A plus sign goes between substances on each side. The arrow points towards the products.
- **Chemical equation:** the same reaction written with formulas: $\ce{Mg + O2 -> MgO}$.
- **Skeletal equation:** a chemical equation that is **not balanced** yet. $\ce{Mg + O2 -> MgO}$ is skeletal: 2 O atoms on the left, 1 on the right.

:::remember
**Law of conservation of mass:** mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. So the number of atoms of each element must be the **same on both sides**. That is why every equation must be balanced.
:::

## 3. Balancing an equation (hit-and-trial method)

NCERT balances $\ce{Fe + H2O -> Fe3O4 + H2}$ like this:
1. Draw a box around each formula. **Never change what is inside a box.**
2. List the atoms of each element on both sides.
3. Start with the compound that has the **most atoms** ($\ce{Fe3O4}$), and in it the element with the most atoms (O: 4). Put 4 in front of $\ce{H2O}$.
4. Balance H: 8 H on the left, so put 4 in front of $\ce{H2}$.
5. Balance Fe: put 3 in front of Fe.
6. Count every element again to check.

$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{3Fe + 4H2O} \\ & \ce{-> Fe3O4 + 4H2} \end{aligned}$$

Use the **smallest whole numbers** that work. To get 4 O atoms write $\ce{4H2O}$, never $\ce{H2O4}$ or $\ce{(H2O)4}$.

## 4. Making equations more informative

- **State symbols:** (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous, meaning dissolved in water.
- In the reaction of iron with water, NCERT writes $\ce{H2O(g)}$, because the water is **steam**.
- **Conditions** such as temperature, pressure, a catalyst or sunlight go above or below the arrow. Example: $\ce{CO + 2H2}$ gives $\ce{CH3OH}$ at 340 atm.
- State symbols are usually left out unless they are needed.

## 5. Types of chemical reactions

In a reaction, atoms are not created, destroyed or changed into other elements. Only the **bonds** between atoms break and new ones form.

| Type | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Combination | Two or more substances form **one** product | Quick lime + water |
| Decomposition | **One** substance breaks into two or more | Heating limestone |
| Displacement | A more reactive element pushes out a less reactive one | Iron nail in copper sulphate |
| Double displacement | Two compounds **swap ions** | Sodium sulphate + barium chloride |
| Redox | One substance is oxidised, another is reduced | Hydrogen over hot copper oxide |

One reaction can belong to two types. For example, a displacement reaction can also be a redox reaction.

## 6. Combination reactions

- **Quick lime + water:** calcium oxide (quick lime) reacts **vigorously** with water to give calcium hydroxide (slaked lime) and a lot of heat.
- **Whitewashing:** slaked lime reacts slowly with $\ce{CO2}$ in the air. In 2–3 days a thin layer of calcium carbonate forms and gives the wall a shiny finish. Marble is also $\ce{CaCO3}$.
- Burning of coal: $\ce{C + O2 -> CO2}$. Formation of water: $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$.
- Burning magnesium: a dazzling white flame and a white powder, magnesium oxide.

## 7. Exothermic and endothermic reactions

- **Exothermic:** heat is **given out** along with the products. Examples: quick lime + water, burning natural gas ($\ce{CH4}$), **respiration**, and vegetable matter turning into compost.
- **Respiration** is exothermic: glucose from our food combines with oxygen in our cells and releases energy.
- **Endothermic:** energy is **absorbed**. The decomposition reactions in section 8 take in heat, light or electricity, so they are endothermic.
- Not every decomposition is endothermic. NCERT gives vegetable matter breaking down into compost as an **exothermic** reaction.
- NCERT activity: mixing barium hydroxide with ammonium chloride makes the test tube feel **cold**. It is endothermic.

## 8. Decomposition reactions

Decomposition is the **opposite of combination**: one reactant breaks down into simpler products. In the examples below, energy (heat, electricity or light) is needed to break the reactant.

| Energy used | Example | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat (thermal decomposition) | Ferrous sulphate | Green crystals change colour; smell of burning sulphur |
| Heat | Limestone, $\ce{CaCO3}$ | Gives quick lime (used for cement) and $\ce{CO2}$ |
| Heat | Lead nitrate | **Brown fumes** of $\ce{NO2}$ |
| Electricity | Water | Gas bubbles at both electrodes |
| Sunlight | Silver chloride | White solid turns **grey** |

- Decomposition by heating is called **thermal decomposition**. Other books call the electricity case *electrolytic* and the light case *photolytic* decomposition (beyond the Class 10 book).
- **Ferrous sulphate:** the crystals ($\ce{FeSO4.7H2O}$) first lose their water, then break into $\ce{Fe2O3}$ (solid), $\ce{SO2}$ and $\ce{SO3}$ (gases).
- **Electrolysis of water:** a few drops of dilute sulphuric acid are added to the water. The gas collected in one tube has **double** the volume of the other. That gas is **hydrogen**, because water has 2 H atoms for every O atom.
- **Silver chloride and silver bromide** break into silver and chlorine (or bromine) in sunlight. This is used in **black and white photography**.

## 9. Displacement reactions

- A **more reactive** element displaces a **less reactive** element from its compound.
- **Iron nail in copper sulphate:** the blue colour of the solution fades (pale green iron sulphate forms) and the nail turns brownish (copper is deposited on it).
- Zinc and lead are also more reactive than copper, so they displace it from copper sulphate and copper chloride.

## 10. Double displacement and precipitation

- Two compounds **exchange ions**.
- Sodium sulphate + barium chloride: $\ce{Ba^2+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$ ions form a **white** precipitate of $\ce{BaSO4}$. Sodium chloride stays in solution.
- Lead nitrate + potassium iodide: a **yellow** precipitate of lead iodide, $\ce{PbI2}$.
- A **precipitate** is an insoluble solid that forms in a solution. Any reaction that gives a precipitate is a **precipitation reaction**. Precipitation reactions produce **insoluble salts**.

## 11. Oxidation and reduction

| Oxidation | Reduction |
| --- | --- |
| Gain of oxygen | Loss of oxygen |
| Loss of hydrogen | Gain of hydrogen |

- Heating copper powder in air: the brown surface turns **black**. Copper gains oxygen and becomes copper(II) oxide.
- Passing hydrogen over the hot black CuO turns it **brown** again. CuO loses oxygen (it is **reduced**). $\ce{H2}$ gains oxygen and becomes water (it is **oxidised**).
- One reactant is oxidised while the other is reduced in the same reaction. These are **oxidation–reduction (redox)** reactions.
- $\ce{ZnO + C -> Zn + CO}$: C is oxidised, ZnO is reduced.
- In $\ce{MnO2 + 4HCl}$ → $\ce{MnCl2 + 2H2O + Cl2}$: HCl loses hydrogen, so it is oxidised to $\ce{Cl2}$. $\ce{MnO2}$ loses oxygen, so it is reduced to $\ce{MnCl2}$.

## 12. Oxidation in everyday life

**Corrosion**
- A metal is attacked by substances around it, such as moisture and acids. It **corrodes**.
- Iron gets a reddish-brown coating: **rusting**. Silver gets a **black** coating. Copper gets a **green** coating.
- Corrosion damages car bodies, bridges, iron railings and ships. Painting iron keeps air and moisture away from it.

**Rancidity**
- Fats and oils in food get **oxidised** when kept for a long time. Their smell and taste change. They become **rancid**.
- To prevent it: add **antioxidants**, keep food in **airtight** containers, and fill bags of chips with **nitrogen** (an unreactive gas) instead of air.
