## Homologous series

:::formula{title="Alkanes (saturated, only single bonds)"}
$$\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$$
$n$ = number of carbon atoms ($n$ = 1, 2, 3, ...). Names end in **-ane**: methane, ethane, propane.

Holds for **open chains** only. A ring breaks it: cyclohexane is $\ce{C6H12}$, not $\ce{C6H14}$.
:::

:::formula{title="Alkenes (one C=C double bond)"}
$$\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$$
$n$ = 2, 3, 4, ... Names end in **-ene**: ethene $\ce{C2H4}$, propene $\ce{C3H6}$, butene $\ce{C4H8}$.

Holds for **open chains** with **one** double bond. A ring also fits $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$: cyclohexane $\ce{C6H12}$ is saturated.
:::

:::formula{title="Alkynes (one C≡C triple bond)"}
$$\ce{C_nH_{2n-2}}$$
$n$ = 2, 3, 4, ... Names end in **-yne**: ethyne $\ce{C2H2}$, propyne $\ce{C3H4}$.

Holds for **open chains** with **one** triple bond.
:::

:::formula{title="Next member of a series"}
$$M_\text{next} = M + 14\ \text{u}$$
Any two neighbours in a homologous series differ by one $\ce{-CH2-}$ unit. Its mass is 12 + 2 = **14 u** (C = 12 u, H = 1 u). $M$ = molecular mass of a member.
:::

## Functional groups and their names

| Class | Group | Name ending | 3-carbon example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Haloalkane | $\ce{-Cl}$, $\ce{-Br}$ | prefix chloro-, bromo- | Chloropropane |
| Alcohol | $\ce{-OH}$ | -ol | Propanol |
| Aldehyde | $\ce{-CHO}$ | -al | Propanal |
| Ketone | $\ce{-CO-}$ | -one | Propanone |
| Carboxylic acid | $\ce{-COOH}$ | -oic acid | Propanoic acid |
| Alkene | C=C | -ene | Propene |
| Alkyne | C≡C | -yne | Propyne |

The ketone group is a C=O in the **middle** of the chain (a carbon on both sides). The aldehyde group is a C=O at the **end** of the chain.

:::remember
**Naming in 3 steps:**
1. Count all the carbons, **including** the carbon inside $\ce{-CHO}$, $\ce{-CO-}$ or $\ce{-COOH}$. That gives the root (prop- for 3).
2. If the suffix starts with a vowel (-ol, -al, -one, -oic acid), drop the final "e" of the alkane: propane − e + one = **propanone**.
3. For a double or triple bond, change "-ane" to "-ene" or "-yne": propene, propyne.
:::

## Reactions in the chapter

:::formula{title="Combustion (burning)"}
$$\ce{C + O2 -> CO2}$$
$$\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3CH2OH + 3O2} \\ & \ce{-> 2CO2 + 3H2O} \end{aligned}$$
All give out **heat and light**. These are oxidation reactions.
:::

:::formula{title="Oxidation of ethanol to ethanoic acid"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3CH2OH} \\ & \ce{->[{alkaline} KMnO4][{heat}] CH3COOH} \end{aligned}$$
Acidified potassium dichromate ($\ce{K2Cr2O7}$) + heat does the same job. Both are **oxidising agents**: they add oxygen. This scheme is written as in NCERT and is **not balanced**: the extra oxygen comes from the oxidising agent.
:::

:::formula{title="Addition of hydrogen (hydrogenation)"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{R2C=CR2 + H2} \\ & \ce{->[{Ni or Pd}] R2CH-CHR2} \end{aligned}$$
Only **unsaturated** compounds do this. Example: ethene gives ethane. Used to hydrogenate vegetable oils (nickel catalyst).
:::

:::formula{title="Substitution by chlorine"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH4 + Cl2} \\ & \ce{->[{sunlight}] CH3Cl + HCl} \end{aligned}$$
Needs **sunlight**. Cl replaces H atoms one by one. Higher alkanes give a mixture of products.
:::

:::formula{title="Ethanol + sodium"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2Na + 2CH3CH2OH} \\ & \ce{-> 2CH3CH2O^-Na+ + H2} \end{aligned}$$
Products: sodium ethoxide and **hydrogen** gas.
:::

:::formula{title="Dehydration of ethanol"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3CH2OH} \\ & \ce{->[{hot, conc.} H2SO4][{443 K}] CH2=CH2 + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Needs **excess** concentrated sulphuric acid at 443 K. The acid is a **dehydrating agent** (it removes water). Product: ethene.
:::

:::formula{title="Esterification"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH} \\ & \ce{->[{acid}] CH3COOC2H5 + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Acid catalyst: a few drops of conc. sulphuric acid. The ester smells **sweet**.
:::

:::formula{title="Saponification"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3COOC2H5 + NaOH} \\ & \ce{-> C2H5OH + CH3COONa} \end{aligned}$$
Ester + sodium hydroxide gives back the alcohol and the sodium salt of the acid. The same kind of reaction on oils and fats (esters of long-chain acids) is used to make soap.
:::

:::formula{title="Ethanoic acid + a base"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{NaOH + CH3COOH} \\ & \ce{-> CH3COONa + H2O} \end{aligned}$$
Product: sodium ethanoate (sodium acetate) and water.
:::

:::formula{title="Ethanoic acid + carbonate or hydrogencarbonate"}
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{2CH3COOH + Na2CO3} \\ & \ce{-> 2CH3COONa} \\ & \ce{+ H2O + CO2} \end{aligned}$$
$$\begin{aligned} & \ce{CH3COOH + NaHCO3} \\ & \ce{-> CH3COONa + H2O + CO2} \end{aligned}$$
The $\ce{CO2}$ turns lime water milky. Ethanol does **not** do this.
:::
