## 1. Carbon around us

- Food, clothes, medicines and **all living things** are based on carbon.
- Yet nature has little carbon: **0.02%** of the earth's crust (in minerals, coal and petroleum) and **0.03%** of the air (as $\ce{CO2}$).

## 2. Covalent bonding

- Carbon has atomic number 6 (2, 4), so it has **4 valence electrons**.
- It can't easily make ions:
  - gaining 4 electrons ($\ce{C^4-}$) is hard: 6 protons would find it hard to hold 10 electrons;
  - losing 4 electrons ($\ce{C^4+}$) needs too much energy.
- So carbon **shares** its electrons. A bond formed by sharing a pair of electrons is a **covalent bond**.
- One shared pair = **single** bond; two pairs = **double** bond; three pairs = **triple** bond.

| Molecule | Bond | Shared pairs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$ | Single | 1 |
| $\ce{O2}$ | Double | 2 |
| $\ce{N2}$ | Triple | 3 |
| $\ce{CH4}$ | 4 single C–H | 4 |

- After sharing, each atom has a full outer shell, like a noble gas.
- **Properties of covalent compounds:**
  - strong bonds **inside** the molecule, but weak forces **between** molecules, so **low melting and boiling points**;
  - no ions are formed, so they are **poor conductors** of electricity.

## 3. Allotropes of carbon

Allotropes are different forms of the same element. Their **chemical** properties are the same; their **physical** properties differ.

| Property | Diamond | Graphite |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Each C bonded to | 4 other C atoms | 3 other C atoms (one is a double bond) |
| Structure | Rigid 3-D network | Hexagonal sheets in layers |
| Feel | Hardest substance known | Smooth and slippery |
| Electricity | Does not conduct | Very good conductor |

- **Fullerenes:** another class of allotropes. The first one found was **C-60**, shaped like a football. It is named after the architect Buckminster Fuller, whose geodesic dome it looks like.
- Diamonds can be made by putting pure carbon under very high pressure and temperature.

## 4. Why carbon forms so many compounds

1. **Catenation:** carbon bonds with other carbon atoms to form long chains, branched chains and rings. Silicon forms chains of only 7 or 8 atoms, and these are very reactive.
2. **Tetravalency:** carbon has a valency of 4, so it can bond with 4 other atoms (C, H, O, N, S, Cl and more).

Carbon's bonds are very **strong** because the carbon atom is **small**: its nucleus holds the shared electrons tightly.

:::remember
**Organic compounds:** people once thought only living things could make them (a "vital force"). In **1828 Friedrich Wöhler** made **urea** from **ammonium cyanate** and proved this wrong. Carbides, oxides of carbon, carbonates and hydrogencarbonates are **not** studied as organic compounds.
:::

## 5. Saturated and unsaturated compounds

- **Saturated:** only **single** bonds between carbon atoms (ethane $\ce{C2H6}$, propane $\ce{C3H8}$). Normally **not very reactive**.
- **Unsaturated:** at least one **double or triple** bond between carbon atoms (ethene $\ce{C2H4}$, ethyne $\ce{C2H2}$). **More reactive**.
- To draw a structure: join the carbons with single bonds, then fill the leftover valencies with H. If valencies are still left, use double or triple bonds.
- **Hydrocarbons** contain only C and H:
  - **alkanes**: saturated;
  - **alkenes**: one or more C=C double bonds;
  - **alkynes**: one or more C≡C triple bonds.

## 6. Chains, branches and rings

- Carbon chains can be **straight**, **branched** or **rings**. Each kind can be saturated or unsaturated.
- **Structural isomers:** the same molecular formula but different structures. Butane, $\ce{C4H10}$, has two:
  - straight chain: $\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2-CH3}$
  - branched chain: $\ce{CH3-CH(CH3)-CH3}$
- **Rings:** cyclohexane $\ce{C6H12}$ (saturated ring of 6 C) and benzene $\ce{C6H6}$ (a ring with alternate single and double bonds).

## 7. Functional groups

- An atom other than C and H (Cl, Br, O, N, S) that replaces hydrogen in a chain is a **heteroatom**.
- A heteroatom or group of atoms that gives the compound its **special properties**, whatever the chain length, is a **functional group**: $\ce{-Cl}$, $\ce{-Br}$, $\ce{-OH}$, $\ce{-CHO}$, $\ce{-CO-}$, $\ce{-COOH}$.

## 8. Homologous series

- A family of compounds with the **same functional group**, where each member differs from the next by **$\ce{-CH2-}$** (14 u).
- Example: $\ce{CH3OH}$, $\ce{C2H5OH}$, $\ce{C3H7OH}$, $\ce{C4H9OH}$ (alcohols).
- **Chemical properties stay similar** (they depend on the functional group).
- **Physical properties change gradually** (graded): melting point, boiling point and solubility change as the molecular mass grows.

## 9. Naming carbon compounds

- Count the carbons to get the root: meth (1), eth (2), prop (3), but (4), pent (5), hex (6).
- Add a **prefix** (chloro-, bromo-) or a **suffix** (-ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, -ene, -yne) for the functional group.
- If the suffix starts with a vowel, drop the final "e": propane → **propanol**, **propanal**, **propanone**, **propanoic acid**.

## 10. Chemical properties

**Combustion.** Carbon and its compounds burn in oxygen to give $\ce{CO2}$, heat and light.
- Saturated hydrocarbons usually give a **clean blue flame**.
- Unsaturated compounds give a **yellow, sooty flame** (lots of black smoke).
- **Too little air** makes even saturated fuels burn incompletely, with a sooty flame. Blackened vessel bottoms mean the air holes are blocked and fuel is wasted.
- Coal and petroleum contain some nitrogen and sulphur, so burning them gives **oxides of sulphur and nitrogen**: major pollutants.
- A **flame** forms only when **gases** burn. Charcoal just glows; wood first gives a flame because its volatile parts turn to gas and burn. The yellow of a candle flame comes from glowing particles of carbon (soot).
- **Fossil fuels:** coal formed from buried plants; petroleum and gas from tiny sea plants and animals, over millions of years.

**Oxidation.** Alkaline $\ce{KMnO4}$ or acidified $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$, on heating, oxidise ethanol to **ethanoic acid**. Substances that add oxygen to others are **oxidising agents**.

**Addition.** Unsaturated hydrocarbons add $\ce{H2}$ with a **nickel or palladium catalyst** to become saturated. This is **hydrogenation**, used to harden vegetable oils.
- A **catalyst** makes a reaction happen, or changes its rate, without being used up itself.
- Vegetable oils: long **unsaturated** chains. Animal fats: **saturated** chains, said to be harmful for health. Oils with unsaturated fatty acids should be chosen for cooking.

**Substitution.** Saturated hydrocarbons are fairly unreactive. In **sunlight**, chlorine replaces their H atoms one by one (a very fast reaction):

$$\ce{CH4 + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + HCl}$$

## 11. Ethanol ($\ce{C2H5OH}$)

- Liquid at room temperature; called **alcohol**; mixes with water in **all proportions**.
- A good solvent: used in tincture iodine, cough syrups and tonics.
- Even a small amount of pure ethanol (**absolute alcohol**) can kill. Long-term drinking harms health.
- **With sodium:** gives **sodium ethoxide** and **hydrogen** gas.
- **With hot, concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ (excess) at 443 K:** loses water to give **ethene**.
- **Methanol** is far more poisonous: in the liver it becomes methanal, which coagulates protoplasm. It also damages the optic nerve and causes **blindness**.
- **Denatured alcohol:** industrial ethanol made unfit to drink by adding poisons like methanol, plus a **blue dye**.
- **Alcohol as fuel:** sugarcane juice → molasses → fermented → ethanol. It is added to petrol as a cleaner fuel.

## 12. Ethanoic acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$)

- Common name **acetic acid**; a **carboxylic acid**.
- **Vinegar** is a 5–8% solution in water, used to preserve pickles.
- Pure ethanoic acid melts at 290 K, so it often freezes in cold winters. That is why it is called **glacial acetic acid**.
- A **weak acid**: unlike HCl, it is not completely ionised.
- **Reactions:**
  - with an alcohol (acid catalyst): forms an **ester** (**esterification**). Esters smell sweet: used in perfumes and flavours;
  - ester + NaOH gives back the alcohol and the sodium salt of the acid (**saponification**);
  - with NaOH: sodium ethanoate + water;
  - with carbonates and hydrogencarbonates: salt + water + **$\ce{CO2}$** (turns lime water milky).

## 13. Soaps and detergents

- **Soaps:** sodium or potassium salts of **long-chain carboxylic acids**.
- A soap molecule has two ends:
  - an **ionic, hydrophilic** head (likes water);
  - a long **hydrocarbon, hydrophobic** tail (likes oil).
- **Micelle:** in water, soap molecules form clusters with the **tails inside** (holding the oily dirt) and the **ionic heads outside** (facing water). This forms an **emulsion**, and the dirt is rinsed away.
- Micelles do not come together and settle, because their ionic surfaces **repel** each other. They are big enough to scatter light, so soap solution looks **cloudy**.
- **Hard water** has calcium and magnesium salts. These react with soap to form an insoluble **scum** (a white curdy solid), so more soap is needed and less foam forms.
- **Detergents:** sodium salts of **sulphonic acids**, or ammonium salts with chloride or bromide ions. Their charged ends do **not** form scum with $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$, so they work in hard water. Used in shampoos and cleaning products.
