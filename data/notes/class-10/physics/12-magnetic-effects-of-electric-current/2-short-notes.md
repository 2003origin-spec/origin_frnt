## 1. Electricity and magnetism are linked

- A wire carrying current **behaves like a magnet**. A compass needle placed near it gets deflected.
- **Hans Christian Oersted** found this by accident in **1820**. He showed that electricity and magnetism are related.
- A unit of magnetic field strength, the **oersted**, is named after him.

## 2. Magnets and the magnetic field

- A **compass needle** is a small bar magnet. The end pointing north is the **north pole** (north-seeking). The other end is the **south pole**.
- **Like poles repel. Unlike poles attract.**
- **Magnetic field:** the region around a magnet in which its force can be detected.
- The field has size **and** direction. Its direction at a point is the direction in which the **north pole** of a compass needle points there.

:::remember
A compass needle is deflected near a bar magnet because the needle is itself a small magnet. The magnet's field exerts forces on the needle's poles.
:::

## 3. Magnetic field lines

**Two ways to see them:**
- **Iron filings:** sprinkle filings on paper over a bar magnet and tap the paper. The filings line up along the field lines.
- **Compass:** start near the N pole, mark both ends of the needle, move the compass so its S end sits where its N end was, and repeat until you reach the S pole. Join the marks with a smooth curve.

**Properties of field lines:**
- Outside a magnet they go from the **N pole to the S pole**. Inside the magnet they go from **S to N**.
- So they are **closed curves**.
- Where lines are **crowded**, the field is **strong** (near the poles). Where they are far apart, it is weak.
- **No two field lines cross.** At a crossing, a compass would have to point in two directions at once, which is impossible.
- A **uniform** field is drawn as **parallel, equally spaced** straight lines.

## 4. Field around a straight wire

Pass a current through a long straight wire that goes through a cardboard sheet. Iron filings on the sheet form **concentric circles** around the wire.

- The circles get **bigger** as you go away from the wire.
- **More current** → stronger field (the compass deflects more).
- **Farther from the wire** → weaker field (the compass deflects less).
- **Reverse the current** → the field reverses (the compass swings the other way).

**Right-hand thumb rule:** hold the wire in your right hand with the thumb along the current. Your fingers curl in the direction of the field lines. It is also called **Maxwell's corkscrew rule**.

:::example{title="Wire over a compass (NCERT Activity 12.4)"}
A wire lies just above a compass, along north–south. Current flows from **north to south**: the N pole of the needle moves towards the **east**. Current from **south to north**: it moves towards the **west**.
:::

## 5. Field of a circular loop and a coil

- Bend the wire into a loop. Near the wire, the field lines are still small circles.
- At the **centre**, the arcs are so large that they look like **straight lines**.
- By the right-hand rule, **every part** of the loop gives a field in the **same direction** inside the loop.
- A coil of **n turns** gives a field **n times** that of one turn, because the currents in all turns go the same way and their fields add.

:::tip
For a loop, curl the fingers of your right hand along the current. The thumb points along the field inside the loop. A loop flat on a table with current **clockwise** (seen from above) has its field **into the table** inside the loop and **out of the table** outside it.
:::

## 6. Solenoid and electromagnet

**Solenoid:** many circular turns of **insulated copper wire**, wound closely in the shape of a cylinder.

| Feature | Solenoid with current | Bar magnet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Field outside | Same pattern | Same pattern |
| Poles | One end N, other end S | N and S |
| Field inside | Parallel lines, S end to N end; uniform | From S to N |

- Inside a solenoid the field lines are **parallel straight lines**, so the field is **uniform** (the same at all points).
- **Electromagnet:** a piece of magnetic material such as **soft iron** placed inside a solenoid is magnetised by the strong field. The book defines it as a soft iron core wrapped with a coil of insulated copper wire.
- (Beyond the Class 10 book) Soft iron loses almost all its magnetism when the current is switched off. Steel keeps it, so steel is used to make permanent magnets.

## 7. Force on a current in a magnetic field

**Activity 12.7:** a short aluminium rod AB hangs between the poles of a horse-shoe magnet, with the field pointing up. When current flows through the rod, it **moves sideways**.

- The force direction **reverses** if the current is reversed.
- It also **reverses** if the field is reversed (poles swapped).
- The force (and the rod's displacement) is **largest** when the current is at **right angles** to the field.
- **André-Marie Ampère** suggested that if a current exerts a force on a magnet, the magnet must exert an **equal and opposite** force on the current.

**Fleming's left-hand rule:** stretch the thumb, forefinger and middle finger of the **left** hand at right angles to each other.
- Forefinger → **field**
- Middle finger → **current**
- Thumb → **force** (motion)

**Moving charges:** a beam of moving charges is a current. For **electrons**, the current is **opposite** to their motion.

**Devices** that use current-carrying conductors in magnetic fields: electric motor, electric generator, loudspeaker, microphone, measuring instruments.

## 8. Magnetism in medicine

- Weak ion currents in our **nerve cells** make tiny magnetic fields, about one-billionth of the earth's field.
- The fields made in the **heart** and the **brain** are significant.
- **MRI** (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) uses the magnetic field inside the body to make images of body parts for diagnosis.

## 9. Domestic electric circuits

India's supply: **AC, 220 V, 50 Hz**. Power comes through overhead poles or underground cables (the **mains**).

| Wire | Insulation | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Live | **Red** | Carries the supply (the book also calls it "positive") |
| Neutral | **Black** | Return path (the book also calls it "negative") |
| Earth | **Green** | Safety; joined to a metal plate deep in the earth |

The potential difference between live and neutral is **220 V**.

**Path into the house:** mains → **main fuse** → **electricity meter** → **main switch** → line wires to the separate circuits.

- Usually **two circuits**: one of **15 A** for high-power appliances (geysers, air coolers), one of **5 A** for bulbs and fans.
- All appliances are joined in **parallel** across live and neutral, so each gets the **same 220 V**. Each has its own switch.

**Earth wire:** the metal body of an appliance (electric iron, toaster, table fan, refrigerator) is joined to the earth wire. It gives a **low-resistance path** to the earth. If current leaks to the body, the body stays at the **earth's potential**, so the user does not get a severe shock.

## 10. Fuse, short circuit and overloading

- A **fuse** protects the circuit and appliances from **overloading**. It works by **Joule heating**: too much current melts the fuse wire and breaks the circuit. It is joined **in series** with the device (Chapter 11).
- **Short circuit:** the **live and neutral wires touch directly** (damaged insulation or a fault in an appliance). The current suddenly becomes **very large**.
- **Overloading** can be caused by:
  - a short circuit;
  - an accidental rise in the supply voltage;
  - too many appliances on a single socket.

:::remember
The two common safety measures in domestic circuits are the **electric fuse** and the **earth wire**. The fuse is the most important safety device.
:::
