This chapter has few formulas of its own. The sums use the lens formula and power from Chapter 9 (Light – Reflection and Refraction). We use the **New Cartesian sign convention** of the book: distances are measured from the optical centre, and distances in front of the lens (on the object side) are negative.

## Lens tools from Chapter 9

:::formula{title="Power of a lens"}
$$P = \frac{1}{f}$$
$P$ = power in **dioptre** (D), $f$ = focal length in **metres**. $1\text{ D} = 1\text{ m}^{-1}$.
Convex (converging) lens: $P$ positive. Concave (diverging) lens: $P$ negative.
:::

:::formula{title="Lens formula"}
$$\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$
$u$ = object distance, $v$ = image distance, $f$ = focal length, all with signs. An object in front of the lens has $u$ negative.
:::

## The normal eye

:::formula{title="Range of clear vision"}
$$\text{near point} \approx 25\text{ cm}$$
$$\text{far point} = \infty$$
For a young adult with normal vision. A normal eye sees clearly anything from 25 cm to infinity. 25 cm is the **least distance of distinct vision**.
:::

:::remember
The distance from the eye lens to the retina **does not change**. So the image distance in the eye is fixed. The eye focuses near and far objects by changing its **focal length** (accommodation).
:::

## Correcting myopia (concave lens)

:::formula{title="Lens for a myopic eye"}
$$f = -x$$
$$P = -\frac{1}{x}$$
$x$ = distance of the far point of the defective eye, in metres for $P$.
**Why:** a distant object ($u = -\infty$) must give a virtual image at the far point ($v = -x$). Then $\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = -\frac{1}{x}$.
Holds when the lens is very close to the eye (we ignore the gap).
:::

:::example{title="NCERT Exercise 6 type"}
Far point = 80 cm = 0.8 m. $f = -0.8$ m, so $P = -\dfrac{1}{0.8} = -1.25$ D, a concave lens.
:::

## Correcting hypermetropia (convex lens)

:::formula{title="Lens for a hypermetropic eye"}
$$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{d} - \frac{1}{N}$$
$$P = \frac{1}{d} - \frac{1}{N}$$
$d$ = 0.25 m (normal near point), $N$ = near point of the defective eye. Put both in metres to get $P$ in dioptre.
**Why:** a book at 25 cm ($u = -d$) must give a virtual image at the eye's own near point ($v = -N$).
Since $N > d$, $f$ comes out **positive**: a convex lens. Lens assumed very close to the eye.
:::

:::example{title="NCERT Exercise 7 type"}
Near point = 1 m. $P = \dfrac{1}{0.25} - \dfrac{1}{1} = 4 - 1 = +3$ D. So $f = \tfrac13$ m $\approx 33$ cm, a convex lens.
:::

## Quick table

| Defect | What goes wrong | Lens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Myopia | Far object: image in front of retina | Concave (P < 0) |
| Hypermetropia | Near object: image behind retina | Convex (P > 0) |
| Presbyopia | Near point moves away (age) | Convex, for near work |
| Myopia + hypermetropia | Both at once | Bifocal |

## Numbers from the book

:::remember
- Eyeball: nearly a sphere, about **2.3 cm** across.
- Sun seen about **2 minutes before** actual sunrise and about **2 minutes after** actual sunset.
- Wavelength of red light is about **1.8 times** that of blue light.
- Colours of the spectrum, from least bent to most bent: **red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet**.
:::

## Prism angles (beyond the Class 10 book)

:::formula{title="Angle of deviation"}
$$i + e = A + D$$
$i$ = angle of incidence, $e$ = angle of emergence, $A$ = angle of the prism, $D$ = angle of deviation. The book only asks you to **mark and measure** $D$ (Activity 10.1); this relation is for checking your drawing.
:::
