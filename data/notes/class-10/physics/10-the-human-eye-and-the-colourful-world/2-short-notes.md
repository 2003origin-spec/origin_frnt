## 1. The human eye

The eye works like a camera. Its lens system forms an image on a light-sensitive screen called the **retina**. The eyeball is nearly a sphere, about 2.3 cm across.

| Part | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Cornea | Thin clear bulge at the front. Light enters here. **Most of the bending** of light happens at its outer surface. |
| Iris | Dark muscular diaphragm. Controls the **size of the pupil**. |
| Pupil | The opening. Controls **how much light** enters. |
| Eye lens | Clear, jelly-like lens. Gives the **fine adjustment** of focal length. |
| Ciliary muscles | Change the curvature (shape) of the eye lens. |
| Retina | Screen with many light-sensitive cells. They send electrical signals. |
| Optic nerve | Carries the signals to the brain. |

- The eye lens forms a **real, inverted** image on the retina.
- The brain reads the signals and lets us see objects as they are.

## 2. Power of accommodation

The ability of the eye lens to **adjust its focal length** is called **accommodation**.

| Looking at | Ciliary muscles | Eye lens | Focal length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Far object | Relaxed | Thin | Increases |
| Near object | Contract | Thick (more curved) | Decreases |

- The focal length cannot go below a certain minimum. So very close objects look blurred.
- **Near point** (least distance of distinct vision): the closest point seen clearly without strain. About **25 cm** for a young adult with normal vision.
- **Far point**: the farthest point seen clearly. **Infinity** for a normal eye.
- So a normal eye sees clearly from 25 cm to infinity.

:::remember
**Cataract:** in old age the eye lens can become milky and cloudy. This causes partial or complete loss of vision. Vision can be restored by **cataract surgery**.
:::

## 3. Defects of vision and their correction

The eye may slowly lose its power of accommodation, and vision becomes blurred. These are **refractive defects**. They are corrected with suitable spherical lenses.

### (a) Myopia (near-sightedness)

- Sees **near** objects clearly, but **not distant** ones.
- The far point is **nearer than infinity** (maybe a few metres).
- The image of a distant object forms **in front of the retina**.
- **Causes:** (i) too much curvature of the eye lens, or (ii) the eyeball is too long (elongated).
- **Correction:** a **concave lens** of suitable power. It brings the image back onto the retina.

### (b) Hypermetropia (far-sightedness)

- Sees **distant** objects clearly, but **not nearby** ones.
- The near point is **farther than 25 cm**. The person must hold a book far away to read.
- Light from a nearby object is focused at a point **behind the retina**.
- **Causes:** (i) the focal length of the eye lens is too long, or (ii) the eyeball is too small.
- **Correction:** a **convex (converging) lens** of suitable power. It gives the extra focusing power needed.

### (c) Presbyopia

- With age, the power of accommodation usually goes down. The near point slowly moves away. Nearby objects are hard to see without glasses.
- **Causes:** the ciliary muscles grow **weak**, and the eye lens becomes **less flexible**.
- Some people have **both** myopia and hypermetropia. They often need **bifocal lenses**:
  - upper part: **concave** lens, for distant vision;
  - lower part: **convex** lens, for near vision.
- Today, these defects can also be corrected with **contact lenses** or **surgery**.

:::remember
**Eye donation:** eyes must be removed within **4–6 hours** after death. One pair of eyes can give vision to up to **four** corneal-blind people. People of any age or sex can donate, even those who wear glasses or had cataract surgery. People who had diseases such as AIDS, hepatitis B or C, or rabies cannot donate.
:::

## 4. Refraction through a glass prism

- A triangular glass prism has **two triangular bases** and **three rectangular side faces**.
- **Angle of the prism:** the angle between its two refracting (side) faces.
- At the first face (air to glass), the ray bends **towards** the normal.
- At the second face (glass to air), it bends **away from** the normal.
- In a glass slab the emergent ray is **parallel** to the incident ray (only shifted sideways). In a prism the faces are tilted, so the emergent ray comes out at an angle to the incident ray.
- This angle is the **angle of deviation** ($\angle D$).

Names used in Fig. 10.4: incident ray PE, refracted ray EF, emergent ray FS; angles $\angle i$ (incidence), $\angle r$ (refraction), $\angle e$ (emergence), $\angle A$ (prism), $\angle D$ (deviation).

## 5. Dispersion of white light

- A prism splits white light into a band of colours. This splitting is called **dispersion**.
- The band of colours is called a **spectrum**.
- Order of colours: **V I B G Y O R** (violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red).
- **Why:** different colours bend through different angles. **Red bends the least, violet the most.** So each colour leaves along a different path.
- Any light that gives a spectrum like sunlight is called **white light**.

**Newton's experiment:** Newton was the first to get the spectrum of sunlight with a glass prism. A second similar prism did not split any colour further. Then he placed a second **identical prism upside down** (inverted) after the first. All the colours joined again and **white light** came out. So sunlight is made of seven colours.

### Rainbow

- A rainbow is a **natural spectrum** in the sky after rain.
- Tiny water droplets act like small prisms. In each droplet, sunlight is:
  1. **refracted and dispersed** on entering,
  2. **reflected internally** at the back,
  3. **refracted again** on coming out.
- A rainbow always forms in the direction **opposite to the Sun**. You see it with the **Sun behind you**.
- You can also see one through a waterfall or a fountain on a sunny day.

## 6. Atmospheric refraction

Refraction of light by the earth's atmosphere is called **atmospheric refraction**.

- **Wavering over a fire:** hot air above a fire is lighter (less dense) and has a slightly **lower refractive index** than the cooler air above. The air keeps moving, so objects seen through it seem to flicker.

### Twinkling of stars

- Starlight bends again and again as it passes through layers of air with slowly changing refractive index.
- The atmosphere bends starlight **towards the normal**. So a star near the horizon looks a little **higher** than it really is.
- Air conditions keep changing, so this apparent position keeps shifting a little.
- Stars are very far away, so they act like **point sources**. The light reaching the eye keeps changing: the star looks brighter, then fainter. This is **twinkling**.

### Why planets don't twinkle

- Planets are much closer, so they look like **extended sources** (a small disc).
- Think of a planet as many point sources. Their changes **average out to zero**, so there is no twinkling.

### Advance sunrise and delayed sunset

- We see the Sun about **2 minutes before** actual sunrise and about **2 minutes after** actual sunset.
- "Actual sunrise" means the Sun really crossing the horizon.
- The Sun's disc also looks **flattened** at sunrise and sunset, for the same reason.

## 7. Scattering of light

### Tyndall effect

- The atmosphere holds tiny particles: smoke, water droplets, dust and air molecules.
- When a beam of light hits such fine particles, its **path becomes visible**. The particles scatter the light towards us.
- Seen when sunlight enters a smoky room through a small hole, or passes through a dense forest canopy (tiny water droplets in the mist scatter it).
- The colour of scattered light depends on **particle size**:
  - very fine particles scatter mainly **blue** light;
  - larger particles scatter longer wavelengths;
  - large enough particles make the scattered light look **white**.

### Why the clear sky is blue

- Air molecules and fine particles are **smaller than the wavelength** of visible light.
- They scatter **shorter wavelengths (blue end)** much more than longer ones (red end). Red light's wavelength is about **1.8 times** blue light's.
- The scattered blue light reaches our eyes, so the sky looks blue.
- **No atmosphere, no scattering:** the sky would look **dark**. Passengers flying very high see a dark sky, because scattering is weak there.

:::remember
**Danger signals are red** because red light is scattered the **least** by fog or smoke. So it can be seen in the same colour from far away.
:::
