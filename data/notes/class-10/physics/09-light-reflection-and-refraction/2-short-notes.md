## 1. Light and reflection

- We see an object when light from it enters our eyes.
- Light seems to travel in **straight lines**. A ray is drawn as a straight line with an arrow.
- **Laws of reflection** (true for plane **and** curved surfaces):
  1. Angle of incidence = angle of reflection.
  2. The incident ray, the normal at the point of incidence and the reflected ray all lie in **one plane**.

**Image in a plane mirror:** virtual and erect, same size as the object, as far behind the mirror as the object is in front, and **laterally inverted** (left and right swap).

## 2. Spherical mirrors

The reflecting surface is part of a sphere.

- **Concave mirror:** reflecting surface curved **inwards** (faces the centre of the sphere). It converges light.
- **Convex mirror:** reflecting surface curved **outwards**. It diverges light.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Pole (P) | Centre of the mirror surface |
| Centre of curvature (C) | Centre of the sphere the mirror is part of |
| Radius of curvature (R) | Radius of that sphere; $R = PC$ |
| Principal axis | Line through P and C |
| Principal focus (F) | Point where rays parallel to the axis meet (or seem to come from) after reflection |
| Focal length (f) | Distance PF |
| Aperture | Diameter of the reflecting surface |

- C is **not** part of the mirror. It is in front of a concave mirror and behind a convex mirror.
- The principal axis is **normal** to the mirror at the pole.
- For small apertures, $R = 2f$, so F is midway between P and C.

:::remember
A concave mirror held towards the Sun forms a bright spot on paper. This spot is a real image of the Sun, at the focus. Its distance from the mirror gives the focal length (approximately). The concentrated sunlight can burn the paper.
:::

## 3. Ray diagrams for mirrors

Use **any two** of these rays. Where they meet (or seem to meet) is the image.

1. Parallel to the principal axis → passes through F (concave), or seems to come from F (convex).
2. Through F (concave), or aimed at F (convex) → comes out **parallel** to the axis.
3. Through C (concave), or aimed at C (convex) → reflects **back along the same path** (it hits along the normal).
4. Aimed at the pole P → reflects at an equal angle on the other side of the axis.

## 4. Images formed by a concave mirror (Table 9.1)

| Object at | Image at | Image is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infinity | F | Point-sized, real, inverted |
| Beyond C | Between F and C | Smaller, real, inverted |
| C | C | Same size, real, inverted |
| Between C and F | Beyond C | Bigger, real, inverted |
| F | Infinity | Not formed (see below) |
| Between P and F | Behind the mirror | Bigger, **virtual, erect** |

Object at F: the reflected rays come out parallel, so they meet only at infinity. NCERT writes "image would not be formed" for its size and nature.

**Uses:** torches, searchlights and vehicle headlights (strong parallel beam), shaving mirrors and dentists' mirrors (enlarged image), solar furnaces (concentrate sunlight to produce heat).

## 5. Images formed by a convex mirror (Table 9.2)

| Object at | Image at | Image is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infinity | At F, behind the mirror | Point-sized, virtual, erect |
| Anywhere else | Between P and F, behind | Smaller, virtual, erect |

**Use:** rear-view (wing) mirrors in vehicles. The image is always erect, and the mirror gives a **wider field of view**.

## 6. New Cartesian sign convention

- Pole P is the origin. The principal axis is the x-axis.
- The object is always on the **left**, so light falls from the left.
- Distances to the right of P are $+$, to the left are $-$.
- Heights above the axis are $+$, below are $-$.

So $u$ is always negative. For a concave mirror $f$ and $R$ are negative. For a convex mirror they are positive.

## 7. Mirror formula and magnification

$$\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$
$$m = \frac{h'}{h}$$
$$m = -\frac{v}{u}$$

- $v$ negative: image in **front** of the mirror, real.
- $v$ positive: image **behind** the mirror, virtual.
- $m$ negative: real and inverted. $m$ positive: virtual and erect.
- $|m| > 1$: enlarged. $|m| < 1$: diminished. $|m| = 1$: same size ($m = -1$ for an object at C of a concave mirror; $m = +1$ for a plane mirror).

:::example{title="NCERT Example 9.2"}
Object 4.0 cm tall, $u = -25.0$ cm, concave mirror $f = -15.0$ cm.

$\frac1v = \frac1f - \frac1u = -\frac{1}{15} + \frac{1}{25} = -\frac{2}{75}$, so $v = -37.5$ cm.

$h' = -\frac{v}{u}h = -6.0$ cm. The image is real, inverted and enlarged, 37.5 cm in front of the mirror.
:::

## 8. Refraction

When light goes **obliquely** from one transparent medium into another, its direction changes. This is **refraction**. It happens because the **speed** of light changes.

Everyday effects: a pool looks shallower, a pencil in water looks bent, letters under a glass slab look raised, a lemon in a glass of water looks bigger.

**Rectangular glass slab:**
- At the air-to-glass face, the ray bends **towards** the normal.
- At the glass-to-air face, it bends **away** from the normal.
- The bending at the two parallel faces is equal and opposite. So the **emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray**, but shifted sideways a little.
- A ray that hits the face along the normal ($i = 0$) goes straight through without bending.

**Laws of refraction:**
1. The incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal all lie in **one plane**.
2. **Snell's law:** $\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r}$ is a constant, for a given colour of light and a given pair of media.

## 9. Refractive index

- $n_{21} = \dfrac{v_1}{v_2}$: refractive index of medium 2 with respect to medium 1.
- Absolute refractive index: $n_m = \dfrac{c}{v}$, with $c = 3 \times 10^8$ m/s.
- Light is fastest in vacuum. In air it is only a little slower.
- Some values: water 1.33, kerosene 1.44, crown glass 1.52, diamond 2.42 (the highest in NCERT's table). NCERT says you need not memorise the table.

**Optical density:** the medium with the larger refractive index is **optically denser**. Light is slower in it.
- Rarer → denser: light slows down and bends **towards** the normal.
- Denser → rarer: light speeds up and bends **away** from the normal.

## 10. Spherical lenses

A lens is a transparent material bound by two surfaces, at least one of them spherical.

- **Convex (converging) lens:** thicker in the middle.
- **Concave (diverging) lens:** thicker at the edges.
- **Optical centre (O):** central point of the lens. A ray through O goes on **without deviation**.
- A lens has two centres of curvature ($C_1$, $C_2$) and two principal foci ($F_1$, $F_2$).
- NCERT uses only **thin lenses with small apertures**.

**Ray rules** (use any two):
1. Parallel to the axis → passes through F on the other side (convex), or seems to come from F on the same side (concave).
2. Through F (convex), or aimed at the F on the far side (concave) → comes out **parallel** to the axis.
3. Through the optical centre → goes straight on.

## 11. Images formed by lenses

**Convex lens (Table 9.4)**

| Object at | Image at | Image is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infinity | $F_2$ | Point-sized, real, inverted |
| Beyond $2F_1$ | Between $F_2$ and $2F_2$ | Smaller, real, inverted |
| $2F_1$ | $2F_2$ | Same size, real, inverted |
| Between $F_1$ and $2F_1$ | Beyond $2F_2$ | Bigger, real, inverted |
| $F_1$ | Infinity | Not formed (rays parallel) |
| Between $F_1$ and O | Same side as object | Bigger, **virtual, erect** |

The last row is how a magnifying glass works.

**Concave lens (Table 9.5):** always a **virtual, erect, diminished** image, between $F_1$ and O on the object's side. For an object at infinity, the image is point-sized at $F_1$.

## 12. Lens formula, magnification and power

Distances are measured from O. Convex lens: $f$ positive. Concave lens: $f$ negative.

$$\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$
$$m = \frac{h'}{h}$$
$$m = \frac{v}{u}$$

- $v$ positive: image on the **other side**, real.
- $v$ negative: image on the **same side** as the object, virtual.

**Power:** $P = \dfrac{1}{f}$, with $f$ in metres. Unit: dioptre (D). 1 D is the power of a lens of focal length 1 m.
- A short focal length means a high power: the lens bends light more.
- Convex lens: $+P$. Concave lens: $-P$.
- Lenses in contact: $P = P_1 + P_2 + \dots$ Opticians use this when they test eyes.

:::example{title="Reading a prescription"}
$+2.0$ D: convex lens, $f = \frac{1}{2.0} = +0.50$ m.

$-2.5$ D: concave lens, $f = \frac{1}{-2.5} = -0.40$ m.
:::
